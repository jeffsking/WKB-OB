# Deep Reading Report — Chapter 4

Book: Handbook of Polymer Testing – Short-Term Mechanical Tests
Author: Edward N. Brown (Rapra, 2002)
Chapter: 4 — Mass, Density and Dimensions

## 1. Core Arguments（核心论点）

CA-1. Mass, density and dimensions are linked by a fundamental physical relationship, and are grouped together because they function as indispensable input quantities for virtually every other physical test.
> "The obvious connection between mass, density and dimensions, is that density can be derived from a knowledge of dimensions and mass. However, they have been grouped together largely for convenience and also because they are measurements which are used as an essential part of other physical tests." [Line 9-15]

CA-2. These three quantities are not only technical parameters but direct cost drivers in product costing and core inputs to quality control.
> "Mass, dimensions and density are also important factors in the costing of products. In quality control, checking dimensional accuracy of components is one of the most basic quality control procedures whilst mass is an essential consideration to control quantity of ingredients, etc. Density can also be a useful control measure to monitor variation in materials." [Line 17-21]

CA-3. Because mass and dimensional measurements are made daily, they are routinely under-estimated, and this casualness propagates proportional errors into derived results such as tensile strength.
> "Measurements that are made every day have a habit of being taken for granted and this can certainly happen to the measurement of dimensions, resulting in unnecessary errors. Taking the example of the determination of tensile strength, any error in the measurement of the cross section directly results in an equivalent percentage error in the strength measurement." [Line 23-29]

CA-4. Density and dimensions are not "trivial" measurements — they require careful attention to temperature control, calibration, instrument choice, and (for soft materials) foot pressure; overlooking this is a systemic source of measurement uncertainty.
> "The accuracy of measuring instruments is specified in many cases as 1% which presumably means ± 1%. If this was literally the case then a measurement of area could potentially have an error of about ± 2%, taking account of length and width. The uncertainty of the measuring instrument is not the complete story as there are also uncertainties associated with the operator and conditions. The point is that dimensional measurements can contribute significantly to uncertainty of a measurement and this is often overlooked." [Line 376-381]

CA-5. Standardisation of dimensional measurement has been slow and inconsistent; until a general method is adopted, each test method retains its own procedure, so the user must distinguish contact vs. non-contact methods and apply the correct pressure.
> "The essentials are to distinguish between a non-contact measurement and one applying a specified pressure, in the latter case to use the correct standard pressure, and to measure within the accuracy limits specified." [Line 372-374]

## 2. Key Concepts with Operationalized Definitions

### KC-1. Density

- Definition (verbatim): "Density is mass per unit volume (at a defined temperature)." [Line 36]
- Usage excerpt: "For thin films it is often more convenient, and more useful, to measure mass per unit area rather than density. A test piece of given dimensions and uniform shape is simply cut and weighed." [Line 183-185]
### KC-2. Relative Density (specific gravity, deprecated)

- Definition (verbatim): "Relative density is mass (of a substance) compared with the mass of an equal volume of a reference substance (usually water) and being a ratio is dimensionless." [Line 37-38]
- Note: "Relative density used to be commonly known as specific gravity but this term is now deprecated and should not be used." [Line 47-49]
- Usage excerpt: "The gravimetric thickness of plastics film is often used, which is obtained from measurement of lateral dimensions and density. This obviates problems of the sensitivity of instruments needed for direct measurement of thickness." [Line 332-334]
### KC-3. Apparent (bulk) Density

- Definition (verbatim): "Apparent density is the term used when the density of, for example, a powder is measured from mass and dimensions which includes the voids between particles." [Line 49-50]
- Usage excerpt: "The bulk factor of a moulding is defined as the ratio of the volume of a given mass of moulding material to its volume in moulded form — the ratio of the density of the moulding to its apparent density before moulding." [Line 226-229]
### KC-4. Immersion Method (for density)

- Definition (operational): Weighing the test piece in air and then in a liquid of known density, from which density is derived by Archimedes' displacement.
- Usage excerpt: "The test piece is weighed in air and then in water using a balance accurate to 1 mg. The best way of suspending the test piece is by means of a very fine filament… However, if smaller than standard test pieces are used the effect of the filament could be significant. A top pan balance is not suitable." [Line 74-79]
### KC-5. Density Column (Gradient Method)

- Definition (operational): Two miscible liquids of different densities are run into a container to produce a uniform density gradient; the test piece comes to rest at the depth where its density equals that of the surrounding liquid.
- Usage excerpt: "With care a column will last several months and the range of density in a single column would not normally be greater than 0.2 mg/m but could be as little as 0.02 mg/m. This means that very fine discrimination is possible but very close control of temperature is necessary." [Line 130-133]
### KC-6. Pycnometer / Density Bottle Method

- Definition (operational): Volume determined by weighing a sealed bottle with and without the sample and with water; used for powders or irregular samples where trapped air would otherwise vitiate results.
- Usage excerpt: "A pycnometer (or density bottle) method is given for use with powders or when it is necessary to cut the sample into small pieces to avoid trapped air, as might happen with narrow bore tubing… This is a more tedious procedure than the immersion method and is generally only used as a last resort." [Line 105-111]
### KC-7. Bulk Factor

- Definition (verbatim): "The bulk factor of a moulding is defined as the ratio of the volume of a given mass of moulding material to its volume in moulded form — the ratio of the density of the moulding to its apparent density before moulding." [Line 226-228]
- Usage excerpt: ISO 171, BS 2782 Method 621 C and ASTM D1895 "all require determination of apparent powder density and moulded density by the appropriate methods discussed previously." [Line 228-230]
### KC-8. Foot Pressure (contact dimensional measurement on soft materials)

- Definition (operational): The pressure exerted by the contact foot of a dial gauge when measuring the thickness of a soft/deformable specimen.
- Usage excerpt: "The use of a dial gauge (or equivalent) on soft materials requires consideration of the foot pressure and in ISO 4648 the standard procedure is to apply 22 ± 5 kPa through a circular foot which is smaller than the test piece. For very soft materials below 35 IHRD 10 ± 2 kPa is used." [Line 286-289]
### KC-9. Surface Texture (Roughness / Waviness / Form)

- Definition (verbatim): "Surface texture can be considered as having three components, roughness, waviness and form, the essential distinction being the spacing of the texture. Roughness refers to closely spaced texture, waviness to wider spaced texture and form is the underlying shape." [Line 476-479]
- Usage excerpt: "The parameter most often used to grade the roughness of a surface is Ra (once known as CLA), the mean deviation of the surface profile above and below the centre line. Rz, which is a measure of the peak to valley height, is the next most widely found." [Line 483-486]
### KC-10. Dimensional Stability

- Definition (verbatim): "A broad definition of dimensional stability would include thermal expansion, shrinkage, softening point and the effect of liquids, which is a mix of thermal properties and environmental effects." [Line 510-512]
- Usage excerpt: "Shrinkage of plastics due to relaxation of stresses through heating is what many people think of as dimensional stability." [Line 548-550]
### KC-11. Dispersion (of compounding ingredients)

- Definition (operational): The direct estimation of the degree of dispersion is effectively a dimensional measurement using microscopy techniques.
- Usage excerpt: "The direct estimation of degree of dispersion is effectively a dimensional measurement using microscopy techniques and is just one example of the value of microscopy for fault diagnosis in polymer products." [Line 567-569]
### KC-12. Gravimetric Thickness (films)

- Definition (operational): Thickness obtained indirectly from lateral dimensions and density rather than direct mechanical contact measurement.
- Usage excerpt: "In ISO 4591 a square or circle of material of area 100 cm² is cut from the film or sheet and weighed and the density determined so that the thickness can be calculated." [Line 334-335]

## 4. Chapter Connections

4.1 Link to earlier chapters on testing fundamentals and specimen preparation.
Chapter 4 opens by framing mass, density and dimensions as "an essential part of other physical tests" (Line 11-15), directly extending Chapter 3 (specimen conditioning and preparation) and foreshadowing every subsequent short-term mechanical test. The statement that "there are very few tests which do not in some way involve the measurement of dimensions" (Line 14) is the bridge that carries Chapter 4 into Chapters 5+ on tensile, compression, flexural and impact testing.

4.2 Tensile / mechanical chapters (Ch. 5+): dimensional accuracy is the hidden variable.
The claim that "any error in the measurement of the cross section directly results in an equivalent percentage error in the strength measurement" (Line 26-28) sets the rules for all derived stress/strain results. Section 4.4.5 Extensometry (Line 495-505) is explicitly deferred: "The apparatus requirements, including range and precision, are usually specified in the test methods standard and will be discussed in the relevant sections in later chapters." This is a forward cross-reference that tells the reader the dimensional apparatus chapter is a prerequisite.

4.3 Density as a feed to abrasion, water absorption, thin-film and dispersion chapters.
- Density is used to calculate volume loss in an abrasion test (Line 12-13) — a backward hook to wear testing.
- "mass being an intrinsic factor in water absorption tests" (Line 13-14) connects Chapter 4 to environmental exposure methods (ISO 175, Line 517).
- Gravimetric thickness replaces direct thickness measurement for films (Line 332-334) — the method imported into thin-film product testing.
- "The direct estimation of degree of dispersion is effectively a dimensional measurement using microscopy techniques" (Line 567-568) folds dispersion into the dimensional chapter, which is unusual and signals that Chapter 4 is the catch-all for any test whose output is a length, mass or density.

4.4 Dimensional stability as a gateway to thermal and environmental chapters.
Section 4.4.6 (Line 508-553) explicitly defers thermal expansion, shrinkage, softening point and liquid swelling to "other books in this series" (Line 512-514) while still listing their standards (ISO 175, ISO 2577, ISO 294-4, ISO 11501, ISO 3521, ISO 8328). This chapter therefore sits as a table of contents for the entire short-term mechanical testing suite.

4.5 Standardization thread — a recurring critique across the chapter.
The author repeatedly critiques draft/ISO standards (ISO 4648 acknowledged as a reference point, Line 265-266; ISO plastics draft "riddled with errors and is not very comprehensive", Line 315-324; ASTM D4883 "essentially describes the use of a commercial instrument with no apparatus details", Line 171-172). This commentary ties Chapter 4 to the book's overarching theme: that standards are living documents and the practitioner must understand the underlying method, not merely quote a number.

4.6 Method hierarchy inside Chapter 4.
The chapter moves from the simplest quantities (mass — Line 54-60) to the derived quantity (density — Line 64-230) to the most operationally delicate (dimensions — Line 236-553) and finally to surface texture and dispersion (Line 455-614). The arc is: from intrinsic property → derived property → process-affected property → surface/structural property, mirroring the book's broader progression from bulk behaviour to structure-property relationships.

## 5. Business Connections（卫材行业应用价值）

Hygiene products (diapers, feminine care, adult incontinence) are composite constructions whose performance hinges on four material subsystems that are each directly governed by Chapter 4 measurements.

### 5.1 Core (芯体) — fluff pulp + SAP + structural webs

- Density of the core batt is the single largest cost/performance driver. Apparent density (Line 49-50) vs. true density distinguish a loosely-fluffed batt (absorption speed) from a densely-packed one (capacity). ISO 60/61 (Line 187-202) funnel/plunger methods are directly applicable to measuring the bulk density of fluff pulp and SAP blends, letting QA reject a supplier batch that arrives over-compacted (higher mass per unit volume, i.e. lower loft per kg — a direct cost leak).
- SAP (Super Absorbent Polymer) powder is the textbook case for ISO 61 non-pourable powder method (Line 196-202). Bulk density of SAP determines fill density of the diaper core; a 5% shift in bulk density translates into a proportionally different SAP kg-per-diaper at a fixed target absorbency — a very large line item.
- Bulk factor (Line 226-229) is operationally equivalent to the fluff-pulp loft factor used in core design; knowing it allows procurement to negotiate on a basis of equivalent absorbent mass rather than volume.
### 5.2 SAP (Super Absorbent Polymer) — granule and solution properties

- True density vs. apparent density (Line 36-50, 187-202) determines the volume the SAP granules occupy before hydration and the swollen gel density after. For SAP, ASTM D792 immersion method (Line 162-165) plus a pycnometer fallback (Line 105-111) is the right combination because SAP is hydrophilic and the immersion in water is actually part of the measurement.
- Mass measurement accuracy (Line 56-60) is critical when SAP is added at 100–400 g per diaper; a balance quoted "to 1 mg" but only "accurate to 1 mg" (Line 58-60) can mislead a supplier QA team into accepting imprecise equipment.
### 5.3 Nonwoven fabric (无纺布) — Topsheet, BFL, Backsheet

- Mass per unit area (basis weight / gsm) is literally "mass per unit area rather than density" (Line 183-185) — the chapter explicitly names this as the preferred metric for films and by extension nonwovens. A ±1% error in width or length measurement compounds to ≈ ±2% error in gsm (Line 376-379), which at industrial tonnage scales is a measurable cost swing.
- Dimensional stability (Line 510-553) of spunbond/meltblown fabric under hot and humid conditions (tropical storage, washing) maps directly to ISO 11501 (Line 550-551) for film/sheet shrinkage; shrinkage of the topsheet affects fit and leak performance.
- Foot pressure during thickness measurement (Line 286-291) is decisive for nonwovens: a 22 kPa foot will compress a lofty airlaid web and give a falsely low thickness. The chapter's warning that "the results will not be identical with non-contact methods" (Line 291) is the reason many nonwoven labs keep both contact calipers and a laser thickness gauge.
### 5.4 Adhesives (胶黏剂) — hot-melt and pressure-sensitive

- Apparent / bulk density of hot-melt pellets or powder adhesive (ISO 60/61, Line 187-202) controls how much adhesive is metered per linear metre of diaper — a direct unit-cost variable.
- Dispersion quality (Line 558-613) matters for adhesive performance: "The dispersion of compounding ingredients can have a large effect on appearance or physical properties" (Line 558-559). Poor dispersion of tackifiers or antioxidants in hot-melt creates weak points at the bond-line, and the split-field microscope approach (Line 577-582) is applicable to diagnosing adhesive defects.
- Dimensional accuracy of adhesive beads on the bond line: the chapter's principle that "dimensional measurements can contribute significantly to uncertainty of a measurement and this is often overlooked" (Line 379-381) applies directly to bond-line width/height QA.
### 5.5 Procurement cost control (采购成本)

- Chapter 4 is the chapter a purchasing engineer should read. Three explicit signals:

- Mass, dimensions and density are "important factors in the costing of products" (Line 17-18).
- Density can be "a useful control measure to monitor variation in materials" (Line 20-21) — a single density test on an incoming SAP or pulp bale tells you whether the supplier is selling you the grade you paid for.
- "Any error in the measurement of the cross section directly results in an equivalent percentage error in the strength measurement" (Line 26-28) — meaning acceptance testing on supplier samples must use calibrated, ISO-compliant instrumentation or you are paying for a measurement, not a decision.
- Practical cost-linked tests from the chapter: ISO 60/61 for SAP/pulp bulk density; ISO 1183 for SAP true density; gravimetric thickness (ISO 4591, Line 332-335) for thin nonwovens and films; ISO 175 (Line 517) for liquid exposure / swelling (SAP saturation).
### 5.6 QA lab setup recommendations (synthesis)

- Balance: true accuracy 1 mg, not merely "reads to 1 mg" (Line 58-60).
- Density: density column (Line 121-135) for high-throughput QC; immersion + pycnometer for incoming raw material certification.
- Dimensions: separate foot-pressure protocols for nonwoven (≤ 22 kPa per Line 287) vs. rigid plastics (micrometer preferred, Line 297-298).
- Non-contact methods (Line 383-404) — travelling/projection microscope — for O-ring-like seals, bond-line profiles, notch geometry.
