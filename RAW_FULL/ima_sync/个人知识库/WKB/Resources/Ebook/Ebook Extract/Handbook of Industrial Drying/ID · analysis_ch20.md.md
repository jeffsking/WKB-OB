# Chapter 20 Analysis — Special Drying Techniques and Novel Dryers

Handbook of Industrial Drying, 4th ed., Mujumdar (CRC Press)
Reader: hygiene-materials R&D engineer (diapers, wipes, absorbent cores, fluff-pulp procurement)

Page numbers derived from PDF-page − 21; offset anchored on Ch.1 = p.1 and should be spot-checked against the print edition.

## 1. 章节概览 / Chapter Overview

Chapter 20 (Kudra & Mujumdar) is a survey of special drying techniques and novel dryer hardware that do not fit the conventional conveyor / rotary / spray taxonomy. It is explicitly an update/overlay chapter: the authors state the focus "is on special drying techniques and selected novel dryers" and that "this chapter effectively updates several chapters in this handbook" (print p.513). It opens with Mujumdar's enumerated trends driving dryer innovation and then catalogs ~9 special techniques (§20.2.1–20.2.9) and 18 novel dryers (§20.3.1–20.3.18), closing with a brief conclusions section.

For the hygiene reader this is the highest-yield survey chapter in the assignment: it contains a directly on-point nonwoven/textile benchmark (Table 20.1, "unwoven fabric for artificial leather"), a textile dryer family (Remaflam, §20.3.10), paper/pulp-mill sludge dewatering routes (ROTADISC, Carver–Greenfield, Dry-Rex), and dielectric (RF/MW) paper drying (§20.3.18). Many other sub-sections (aerogels, pulse combustion, supercritical drying) are only distantly relevant.

## 2. 核心观点 / Key Viewpoints (verbatim + page + interpretation)

- VP1 (trends). "Increasing demands for new and high-quality products, energy-efficient processes, environment protection, and the like have stimulated progress in drying science and technology. Mujumdar has presented a summary of the motivation for development of new drying technologies and identified a number of trends, which include [1]:" — bullets: "Use of superheated steam in direct dryers; Increased use of indirect (conduction) heating; Use of combined (or integrated) heat transfer modes; Use of volumetric heating (microwave [MW]/radio frequency [RF] fields) in specialized situations; Use of two-stage (or multistage) dryers; Use of intermittent heat transfer; Use of novel combustion technologies (e.g., pulse combustion for flash drying)…" (print p.451–452).
Interpretation: The chapter frames novelty as a response to energy + product-quality pressure — the same drivers governing fluff-pulp and nonwoven-bonding oven energy/tonne.
- VP2 (selection philosophy). "Often a trade-off is required between capital and operating costs. So, the final selection must be made not on the basis of novelty but on the overall techno-economics of the drying system. It is important to stress that one should examine the drying system rather than the dryer in isolation." (print p.452).
Interpretation: A guard-rail against chasing novel hardware; applies directly to through-air vs. oven-bonding capex/opex decisions.
- VP3 (nonwoven benchmark). Table 20.1, "Comparison of Active Fluid Bed Dryer with Cylinder Dryer for Polcorfama": footnote "aUnwoven fabric for artificial leather." Fluid-bed: drying medium Air, 100 °C, 10 min, 4200 kJ/kg H₂O, serpentine length 12 m; Cylinder: Steam, 160 °C, 37 min, 22,300 kJ/kg H₂O, 56 m (print p.457).
Interpretation: The single most hygiene-relevant datapoint in the chapter — a quantified energy and footprint benchmark for drying a nonwoven web, with the active-fluid-bed giving ~5.3× lower specific energy and ~4.7× shorter web path than a heated-cylinder dryer.
- VP4 (inert-particle intensity). "As compared to the standard spray dryer, the use of a fluid bed of inert particles to dry slurries results in 15- to 17-fold higher volumetric evaporation rates under identical thermal conditions." (print p.459).
Interpretation: Process-intensification headline; relevant if any slurry/paste (e.g., SAP slurry, binder dispersion) is dried.
- VP5 (pulp/paper sludge). "As applied to processing wastes from the pulp and paper industry, for example, the primary and secondary sludges from the paper mills are fed at a controlled rate into a blend tank… The pressed material at 25 to 35% solids is fed to the ROTADISC processor for thermal drying (scalping)." (print p.465).
Interpretation: Directly maps to fluff-pulp-mill waste handling — the procurement link to mill energy and dewatering.
- VP6 (C–G, wood pulp). "Among current applications of the C–G process are drying of activated sludge from wood pulp plant, wool processing waste from textile manufacturing facility, instant coffee processing waste, dairy product waste, sewage sludge, and others." (print p.473).
Interpretation: Another pulp/paper + textile-waste anchor establishing the chapter's relevance to the hygiene supply chain.
- VP7 (textile dryer family). "A textile fabric typically goes through several drying processes before it is in the finished stage. Conventionally impinging jets, contact, infrared, or through dryers are used for drying textiles." (print p.497).
Interpretation: Spells out that textile/nonwoven drying uses the same toolbox (impinging jet, IR, through-air) as hygiene web lines.
- VP8 (Remaflam operating point). "For a polyester–cotton blended fabric with a weight of 220 g/m² and liquor absorption by fabric of 43% by volume, the fabric speed is 42 m/min for a 36% v/v methanol liquor [78]. The drying zone is about 1.6-m long. The process has been successfully applied for drying of cotton, polyester, polyamide fabrics, and blends." (print p.498).
Interpretation: Gives a real line-speed / energy-density figure for textile drying — useful as a proxy for nonwoven top-sheet/backsheet lines.
- VP9 (dielectric paper drying). "board made of mineral and cellulose components that form the structure of foamed particles stuck in a fibrous network with pore size ranging from 1 to 100 mm [98]… The main advantages of this technology are claimed to be as follows [96]:… Reduced power consumption as compared to the traditional RF equipment." (print p.510–511).
Interpretation: RF/MW volumetric heating of a cellulose/mineral fibrous board is the closest analog in the book to dielectric-assisted tissue/paper drying.
- VP10 (conclusion). "This chapter focused on novel drying technologies based on one or more concepts not found in corresponding conventional drying technologies." (print p.512); "All techniques mentioned here have found industrial-scale applications or have been field-tested. Most of the feedstocks considered are in the form of liquids, pastes, sludges, or granular solids, except for the fluid bed dryer with active solids (for drying of leather) and the Remaflam dryer for textiles." (print p.513).
- VP11 (pulse-combustion intensification). "Numerous studies… have demonstrated that a pulse combustor can offer the following advantages over the conventional (i.e., continuous) combustion systems: Increased heat and mass transfer rates (by a factor of 2 to 5)… Increase (by a factor of 2–3) the drying rate… Reduce unit air consumption by 30 to 40%." (print p.466–467).
Interpretation: Quantified intensification factors useful when scoping high-intensity drying for heat-sensitive binder dispersions.
- VP12 (impinging-stream heat transfer). "the heat transfer coefficient in a coaxial ISD is 850 W/(m² K)… the volumetric heat transfer coefficients for ISDs are also higher, reaching 125,000 W/(m³ K), which is 2.5 to 3 times higher than the values for… and 15 to 100 times higher than those for spray dryers." (print p.479–480).
Interpretation: The volumetric heat-transfer metric is the lever for footprint reduction — directly relevant to line-length compression goals on hygiene web lines.
- VP13 (RF-vacuum rate). "The drying rate is up to ten times higher than in a conventional kiln. An added benefit is no stickering of the lumber packages… no heat discoloration or brown staining during drying. Chemical oxidation due to contact with drying air is practically eliminated." (print p.512).
Interpretation: RF-vacuum eliminates oxidative discoloration — the same brightness-preservation argument as SSD (Ch19/Ch21 §21.7.14) and relevant to any heat-sensitive cellulose product.
- VP14 (Helix compactness). "The dryer with an evaporative capacity of about 8400 kg of water/h… is about 3.5 m in diameter and 5 m high… A conventional vibrated fluid bed dryer for a similar application would be about 1.2 m wide and 48 m long." (print p.502).
Interpretation: A ~10× footprint reduction for equivalent duty — the process-intensification dividend the hygiene engineer should demand when comparing dryer options.
- VP15 (C–G energy). "Products like lignite and peat have been dried successfully at the pilot level with energy consumption in the order of 135 kJ/kg water evaporated." (print p.473).
Interpretation: Multi-effect + MVR evaporation reaches an energy floor (~135 kJ/kg) far below convective drying — a benchmark for dewatering pulp-mill waste streams.
## 3. 研究方法 / Research Method

Pure review/survey chapter — no primary experiments by the authors. Each sub-section is a structured literature write-up citing vendor bulletins, patents, and IDS (International Drying Symposium) papers, supported by equipment schematics and performance tables (Table 20.1–20.30). Method is comparative: contrast novel vs. conventional dryer on specific energy (kJ/kg H₂O), residence time, footprint, and product quality.

## 4. 论证逻辑 / Argumentation

The argument is inductive and cataloguing rather than deductive: (1) motivate novelty via Mujumdar's trends; (2) for each technique/dryer, justify it against one or more of the listed criteria (enhanced quality, reduced energy, higher productivity, lower emissions, flexibility, combinability); (3) present a performance table as evidence. The through-line is process intensification + energy reduction + product-quality control, exactly the hygiene engineer's optimization triad.

## 5. 章节定位 / Role in Book

An overlay/update chapter (the authors say it "effectively updates several chapters"). It points the reader to dedicated chapters for superheated steam (Ch19), MW/RF (separate chapters), and pulp/paper drying (a later chapter). Functionally it is the "what else is out there" catalog that a process engineer consults when conventional dryers hit an energy or web-defect wall.

## 6. 优点与不足 / Strengths and Weaknesses

Strengths: Unmatched breadth (27 technologies); several directly hygiene-relevant anchors (Table 20.1 nonwoven, Remaflam textiles, ROTADISC/C–G/Dry-Rex pulp-mill sludge, RF paper board); strong quantitative performance tables.
Weaknesses: Survey depth is necessarily thin per topic — few mechanistic models, almost no governing equations (the chapter is descriptive; energy figures are empirical tables, not derived relations). Hygiene relevance is uneven: aerogel/supercritical, pulse-combustion, and many food/pharma dryers are only tangentially useful. Some performance claims are vendor-sourced and unverified.

## 7. 关键术语 / Key Terms

- Active fluid bed — fluidized bed of hygroscopic particles that extract moisture from a wet sheet/web (contact-sorption), §20.2.2.2.
- Inert-particle fluid bed (VFB) — heated inert particles (PTFE cubes) coat and dry liquid/pasty feeds; 15–17× volumetric evaporation vs. spray (§20.2.3).
- Remaflam — textile dryer that burns a methanol/water liquor on the fabric as the heat source (§20.3.10).
- Carver–Greenfield (C–G) — multiple-effect evaporation using a nonvolatile carrier oil; applied to wood-pulp-plant sludge (§20.2.7).
- ROTADISC — indirectly heated disk dryer for pulp/paper sludges (§20.2.4.2).
- Dry-Rex — ambient-air moving-bed dryer for paper-mill residues (§20.3.13).
- RF 50-Ω technology — fixed-impedance RF drying with automatic matching, applied to cellulose/mineral board (§20.3.18).
- Impinging stream dryer (ISD) — two opposed high-velocity gas streams collide; high turbulence for polymers/pastes (§20.2.9).
## 8. 微观精读 / Micro close-reading

Table 20.1 is the linchpin hygiene datapoint. Read literally: an "unwoven fabric for artificial leather" (i.e., a synthetic nonwoven web) is dried either (a) in an active fluid bed at 100 °C air / 10 min / 4,200 kJ/kg H₂O / 12 m serpentine, or (b) in a steam cylinder at 160 °C / 37 min / 22,300 kJ/kg H₂O / 56 m. The fluid-bed route cuts specific energy 5.3× and line length 4.7× while running cooler. For a nonwoven line this is a striking illustration that contact-sorption/through-bed drying of a web can beat conductive cylinder drying on both energy and footprint — relevant when specifying through-air bonding vs. calendering/oven routes for acquisition layers or backsheets.

The Remaflam detail (VP8) is noteworthy because it specifies line speed (42 m/min) and zone length (1.6 m) for a 220 g/m² polyester–cotton fabric at 43 vol% liquor uptake — an unusually concrete, scaleable operating point that the hygiene engineer can use as a first-order proxy for drying a similarly weighted spunbond/meltblown composite.

## 9. 概念深析 / Concept Deep-Dive — Process intensification for web & pulp streams

The chapter's unifying concept is intensification via altered heat/mass-transfer geometry: inert-particle coating (huge surface area per unit volume → 15–17× evaporation), impinging-stream collision zones (turbulence amplification), vibro-rotational beds (continuous surface renewal), and dielectric volumetric heating (energy deposited inside the wet matrix rather than at the surface). For hygiene materials the two transferable ideas are:
1. Volumetric vs. surface-limited drying — RF/MW (§20.3.18) deposits energy throughout the cellulose/mineral board, eliminating the thermal-lag bottleneck that makes surface IR drying slow for thick webs (cf. Ch18). The 50-Ω automatic-matching architecture (Fig. 20.61) is what makes RF industrially robust for paper.
2. Web-path compression — Table 20.1 shows a 4.7× shorter path at lower temperature; this is the same lever used to justify through-air (TAD-like) bonding vs. long oven tunnels on hygiene lines.
3. Polymer binder intensification — vortex (§20.3.4) and ISD (§20.2.9) dryers explicitly handle PVA, PVAc and "polymers" at 1.1–1.8× higher heat-transfer coefficients than spouted beds and 15–100× higher volumetric coefficients than spray; for a SAP-precursor or binder dispersion this is the route to shrink a spray-tower footprint by an order of magnitude.

## 10. 批判性评价 / Critique

The chapter over-promises breadth and under-delivers mechanism. Claims such as "15- to 17-fold higher volumetric evaporation" (print p.459) are presented without the underlying heat/mass-transfer derivation or the boundary conditions (feed rheology, inert size) that govern them; the reader cannot reproduce the number. Likewise the Remaflam "thermal efficiencies in the range of 70%" (print p.497) is stated without a definition of the efficiency basis. Several tables are vendor bulletins (Heyl & Patterson, Carlyle, Mabarex, HeatWave) and should be treated as vendor data, not peer-reviewed benchmarks. Hygiene takeaway: use this chapter for technology scouting and order-of-magnitude benchmarks, not for design.

A hygiene-specific gap is the almost total absence of fibre-property outcomes. Unlike Ch19 (which reports tensile, crystallinity, brightness for SSD pulp) or Ch17 (glass-transition quench for thermobonding), Ch20 rarely quantifies how these novel dryers affect fibre curl/kink, brightness reversion, or web defects. The single fibre-relevant datapoint is the nonwoven benchmark in Table 20.1 — and even that reports only energy and path length, not web quality. The Remaflam section describes fabric speed and liquor uptake but not residual-moisture uniformity or tensile loss. The reader must therefore pair this chapter with Ch17–Ch19 when fibre quality is the constraint.

## 11. 卫材相关性 / Hygiene Relevance & Action Cards

Rating: ★★★★☆ (strong — multiple directly on-point anchors, but survey depth is thin and many sub-sections are off-target).

- AC1 — Nonwoven/web dryer benchmarking. Use Table 20.1 (print p.457) as a reference point: active-fluid-bed (through-bed contact-sorption) drying of an "unwoven fabric" achieved 4,200 vs. 22,300 kJ/kg H₂O and 12 m vs. 56 m path vs. a steam cylinder. Action: when specifying a nonwoven drying/bonding step, benchmark against this ~5× specific-energy and ~5× footprint upside before defaulting to oven/cylinder routes.
- AC2 — Pulp-mill sludge & dewatering. ROTADISC (print p.465), Carver–Greenfield (print p.473, explicitly "activated sludge from wood pulp plant"), and Dry-Rex (print p.502, "wood pulp, pulp and paper mill sludge") are three routes for mill waste. Action: in fluff-pulp supplier audits, ask which dewatering/drying route the mill uses — it bears on both mill energy cost (passed through in price) and on biosolids/discharge compliance.
- AC3 — Textile/nonwoven line analog. Remaflam (print p.497–498) and the general statement that textiles use "impinging jets, contact, infrared, or through dryers" (print p.497) confirm that hygiene web lines share the textile drying toolbox. Action: borrow textile line-speed/zone-length data (e.g., 42 m/min, 1.6 m zone for 220 g/m²) as a sanity proxy when scoping top-sheet/backsheet drying capacity.
- AC4 — Dielectric-assisted paper drying. RF 50-Ω technology on cellulose/mineral board (print p.510–511) is the closest book analog to energy-efficient tissue/paper drying. Action: flag RF/MW-assisted drying as a candidate energy-reduction route worth a pilot vs. conventional hot-air for any future line retrofit.
- AC5 — Polymer/binder drying. Vortex dryers list polyvinyl alcohol, polyvinyl acetate (print p.491, Table 20.17); ISDs are "especially suitable for drying… polymers" (print p.484). Action: if drying SAP-precursor or binder dispersions, these intensified dryers are candidates.
