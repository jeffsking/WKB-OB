# Academic Chapter Analysis — Chapter 32: Drying of Peat and Biofuels

Source: Mujumdar, A.S. (ed.), Handbook of Industrial Drying, 4th ed., CRC Press, 2015 — Chapter 32, "Drying of Peat and Biofuels," by Roland Wimmerstedt.
Reader profile: Hygiene-materials R&D engineer (disposable diapers, wipes, absorbent cores, fluff-pulp procurement).
Prepared: 2026-08-13

Page numbers derived from PDF-page − 21; offset anchored on Ch.1 = p.1 and should be spot-checked against the print edition.

## 1. Overview / Chapter Scope

Chapter 32 treats drying as a pre-combustion energy-systems problem rather than a materials-science problem. Wimmerstedt frames peat and biofuel drying around three drivers: (1) lowering fuel moisture to raise boiler efficiency and capacity; (2) meeting process thresholds (gasification –15% MC, suspension firing % MC, briquetting 10–20% MC); (3) mitigating storage degradation. The chapter surveys thermodynamics of fuel drying (flue-gas, superheated-steam, double-effect/Peco), four dryer families (rotary, cascade, pneumatic/flash, fluid-bed steam), environmental VOC/terpene emissions, and operation/availability data, closing with equilibrium (sorption isotherms) and a candid admission that kinetics are "experience-based."

For the hygiene reader this is the weakest of the six assigned chapters: it deals with coarse, non-hygroscopic, non-absorbent bulk solids and with plant-scale energy economics, not with capillary/colloidal water in fibrous or polymeric structures. The genuine, if modest, transfer point is the free-water vs. cell-bound-water (below ~0.3 MC) distinction and the statement that wood is only moderately hygroscopic — directly analogous to fluff-pulp bale-moisture behaviour.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (with verbatim quotes + print pages)

- The moisture threshold is the design variable, not drying per se. "It is not obvious that a moist fuel must be dried before firing. On the contrary, this is considered uneconomical in many cases." (p. 743) — drying is optional and must clear a justifiable threshold.
- Moisture dictates boiler size and efficiency multiplicatively. Table 32.1 (p. 741): at 65 / 50 / 15% MC, higher calorific value stays constant at 20.6 MJ/kg while adiabatic combustion temperature falls 900 → 1200 → 1800 °C and boiler efficiency (on lower value) rises 0.85 → 0.89 → 0.93.
- Flue-gas drying: marginal returns past ~100 °C exhaust. "The primary improvement takes place when the exhaust temperature is lowered to 100 °C. Further drying leads to marginal savings only, due to the decrease in the amount of excess air." (p. 744)
- Steam-drying economy hinges on using the recovered LP steam. "The economy of the dryer is dependent on whether or not the low-pressure steam can be used in the process." (p. 745)
- Most designs are empirical. "Rather few papers deal with the kinetics of biofuel drying and most designs seem to be 'experience-based.'" (p. 751)
- Wood/peat are only moderately hygroscopic — most water is not strongly bound. "The shapes of the isotherms are similar to those of wood and imply that most of the water is not strongly bound. It can be concluded that the hygroscopicity is of rather limited importance for the drying process." (p. 750)
## 3. Research Method / Approach

The chapter is a review/synthesis, not an experimental report. Its quantitative backbone is process integration — coupling dryer mass/energy balances to boiler combustion analysis (Figure 32.1–32.2, 32.4). It mobilizes published pilot/plant data (Swedish installations, Peco, NIRO, BIOMASSTER) and equilibrium datasets (Larsson & Wimmerstedt peat isotherms, Kauman/Rosen/Simpson wood EMC). No single mechanistic drying model is derived; instead the author triangulates thermodynamic limits (heat available in flue gas at 170 °C can dry 60%→~55% fuel), equipment availability statistics, and sorption data.

## 4. Argumentation / Logical Structure

- Why dry? Moisture is an essential combustion parameter → efficiency/capacity/boiler-cost penalties (Table 32.1).
- When must you dry? Threshold-driven cases: gasification, suspension firing, briquetting, storage microbiology (Fig. 32.2 logic).
- How to dry? Three thermodynamic routes — flue gas, superheated steam, double-effect — each with its own energy/steam re-use logic.
- What equipment? Rotary → cascade → pneumatic/flash → fluid-bed steam, mapped to particle size and throughput.
- At what cost/risk? VOC/terpene emissions, corrosion/erosion, availability (flue-gas 80%+ vs steam 35–90%).
- What limits design? Equilibrium (weak binding, hysteresis) and kinetics (intraparticle resistance ∝ particle size; otherwise empirical).
The argument is consistently economic–thermodynamic and never materials-intrinsic — a deliberate contrast with Ch31 (ceramics) and Ch33 (fibres), where water–solid interactions govern everything.

## 5. Role in the Book / Volume Architecture

Ch32 sits in the "specific materials" block of the Handbook. It complements the energy-and-bulk-solid drying chapters and provides a counterweight: whereas Ch31/Ch33 emphasize drying-induced damage (cracking, shrinkage, hornification), Ch32 emphasizes drying as a net energy gain with negligible material damage because the feed is inert, coarse, and non-porous at the relevant scale. It is the chapter where "don't over-dry" is driven by energy payback, not by product quality.

## 6. Strengths and Weaknesses

Strengths: Honest about empirical practice; clear threshold logic; useful plant-availability and corrosion data; the free-vs-cell-bound water framing (p. 750) is a rare conceptual bridge to fibre science.
Weaknesses: Almost no mechanistic kinetic model; figures are low-resolution scans with OCR-garbled axis labels; heavy Nordic/peat bias limits generality; little on dewatering-before-drying (mechanical pressing) specifically — the economics are framed around thermal drying only.

## 7. Key Terms and Definitions

- DS (dry substance): dry basis; all energy figures normalised to 1 kg DS.
- Higher/lower (gross/net) calorific value: latent heat of flue-gas water vapour counted as loss (English convention) or not (Nordic/German convention).
- Flue-gas dryer: uses sensible heat of boiler flue gas down to ~100 °C exhaust; cocurrent flow reduces fire/VOC risk.
- Superheated-steam dryer: evaporating water recovered as LP steam; capacity set by condensing-steam pressure (4–5 bar doubles vs 12–25 bar).
- Double-effect (Peco) dryer: two-stage, recirculated air, steam at ~4 atm; specific steam consumption 0.65–0.70 kg steam/kg evaporated water.
- Cell-bound water: wood residue below ~0.3 MC after free water removal (p. 750).
- MVR (mechanical vapor recompression): recompresses evaporated steam when LP steam has no process use; e.g. 270 kWh/t DS [12].
## 8. Micro close-reading (verbatim excerpts)

- p. 741: "Peat produced directly from the bog contains 80 to 95% water (counted on the total mass), which is reduced to about 50% through natural drying on the bog during summer." → natural dewatering is the first, cheapest stage — a point the chapter under-uses.
- p. 742: "To avoid significant microbiological degradation during storage, the material should be dried to a moisture content of 20 to 25% [2]." → moisture threshold set by biology, not combustion.
- p. 750: "Wood is a moderately hygroscopic material at moisture contents below about 0.3, at which the free water has been removed and the residue can be regarded as cell-bound water." → the single sentence with real fibre-science resonance.
- p. 750: "The condensate in biofuel dryers is acidic and corrosive. All parts of the dryers, where condensation can take place, must be either lined or manufactured in stainless steel materials." → operational reality of volatile-organics + water.
## 9. Concept Deep-Dive — Dewatering-before-Drying Economics (the honest core)

The chapter's economics are thermal-drying economics. Its central energy logic (p. 744, Fig. 32.2): taking flue gas at 170 °C can dry a 60%-MC fuel to ~55% using sensible heat to the wet-bulb temperature; the only large efficiency gain comes from lowering exhaust to 100 °C, after which "further drying leads to marginal savings only." Translating to the hygiene supply chain: the cheapest moisture removal is always mechanical/thermal-pressing (baling, dewatering), not evaporative drying — a principle the chapter implies (natural bog drying 80–95%→50% is free) but never formalizes for non-fuel fibres. For fluff-pulp procurement the lesson is direct: bale moisture (typically 5–10%) is set by pressing + partial thermal drying; pushing below natural equilibrium by evaporative means costs disproportionately more energy per kg water — exactly the "marginal savings" cliff Wimmerstedt documents at 100 °C exhaust.

Where transfer stops (intellectual honesty): Ch32's physics is bulk-porous, gravity/forced-convection dominated. It offers no capillary-pressure model, no bound-water desorption energetics, no polymer/swellable-solid treatment. The cell-bound-water sentence is the lone bridge; everything else (boiler integration, terpene VOCs, steam recompression) does not map onto diaper-core or nonwoven processing.

## 10. Critical Assessment

A competent, candid engineering review that knows its limits ("experience-based"). Its value to the hygiene reader is structural: it teaches the discipline of asking "is drying even justified, and if so to what MC threshold?" before reaching for energy. It is weakest precisely where Ch33 is strongest — in treating water as chemically/physically bound to a fibrous matrix. The omission of explicit mechanical-dewatering energy comparison is the chapter's main missed opportunity for a process engineer.

## 11. Hygiene-Materials Relevance & Action Cards

Overall relevance: ★★☆☆☆ (weak — energy/bulk-solids; one genuine bridge).

Action Card H32-1 — Bale-moisture threshold discipline. Insight: drying past a threshold yields marginal returns (p. 744). Action: Specify fluff-pulp bale MC by storage-stability + converting-need, not by a blanket "as dry as possible"; quantify the energy cost of pushing MC below the natural equilibrium before committing. → 卫材相关性★★☆☆☆｜Avoid paying evaporative-drying energy for MC reductions that mechanical pressing + equilibration already achieve.

Action Card H32-2 — Free vs. cell-bound water language for pulp. Insight: wood's cell-bound water appears only below ~0.3 MC; above that it is free water (p. 750). Action: When auditing pulp supplier MC specs, separate "free surface water" (cheap to press out) from "cell-bound" (needs energy); align incoming-MC acceptance with this split. → 卫材相关性★★★☆☆｜Gives procurement a physically correct vocabulary to challenge supplier MC claims.

Action Card H32-3 — Corrosive-condensate vigilance (transfer to steam dryer audits). Insight: acidic condensate demands stainless/clad construction (p. 750). Action: If your line uses any steam/condensate loop for web drying, audit for condensate acidity (volatiles from additives/fragrances). → 卫材相关性★★☆☆☆｜Pre-empts corrosion failures in hygiene converting dryers.

Non-transfer (explicit): boiler integration, terpene VOC abatement, suspension-firing MC limits, Peco/MVR economics — not applicable to absorbent-product manufacturing.

## 12. Book-Map Quotes (positioning within the Handbook)

- Versus Ch31 (Ceramics): Ch31 fears shrinkage/stress cracking from bound-water loss in a rigid porous body; Ch32 states hygroscopicity is "of rather limited importance" (p. 750) — opposite regimes.
- Versus Ch33 (Fibrous Materials): Ch33 will treat fibre–water binding, hornification, and shrinkage as central; Ch32 treats the same words ("hygroscopic," "cell-bound water," p. 750) only as a footnote to combustion.
- Versus Ch28 (Osmotic Dehydration): both rely on a driving-potential framing (here thermal/energy potential; there osmotic potential) but Ch32 never mathematizes the potential.
