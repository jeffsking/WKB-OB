# Chapter 29 — Drying of Pharmaceutical Products

## 1. Chapter Overview (范围、对象与定位)

Chapter 29 (Zdzisław Pakowski and Arun S. Mujumdar) treats drying as the GMP-governed, contamination-intolerant, thermally-cautious stage of drug manufacture — products that must be dried after intermediate synthesis, final synthesis, and dosage-form manufacture, often at low tonnage / high value where energy cost is secondary to product integrity. The chapter's logic is selection-driven: it opens by stating that product deterioration (microbial infection, oxidation, thermal decomposition, metallic or solvent contamination) "must be avoided at any cost" (p.687), then builds a material-classification scheme (Sazhin/Rebinder, by minimum pore diameter → moisture–material bond strength) to match products to dryers, surveys physical/thermal/sorptive properties (particle size, first-order thermal decomposition via Arrhenius, sorption isotherms and hysteresis), catalogues dryer families (batch ovens, fluid bed, band, turbo-tray, pneumatic, cyclone, spouted, vibrated, spray, drum, vacuum, freeze), and closes with granulation, dosage forms, technological data tables, aseptic conditions (HEPA, class 100, sterilization), and solvent-recovery/closed-cycle drying. For the hygiene reader this is a ★★★★ chapter: its GMP residual-moisture control, aseptic/cleanroom discipline, sorption-isotherm reasoning, and closed-cycle/solvent-safety logic transfer directly to medical-grade nonwovens and lotion/active-coated wipes.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (核心观点 + 原文逐句引证)

V2.1 — Quality is non-negotiable; contamination of any kind is forbidden.
> "The pharmaceutical industry is one in which quality of the final product cannot be compromised. Any deterioration of the product (e.g., by microbial infection, oxidation, thermal decomposition, contamination by metallic particles or by unremoved organic solvent) must be avoided at any cost." (p.687)
Interpretation: Sets the noncompromise bar that medical-grade hygiene lines (surgical drapes, medicated wipes) must mirror via validated hygiene and metal-freedom controls.

V2.2 — Dryer selection follows the moisture–material bond, classified by pore size.
> "Sazhin has developed a classification based in principle on Rebinder's proposal, which classifies all materials into four groups... The quantitative basis of the classification is the minimum pore diameter from which moisture is to be removed during drying." (p.688)
Interpretation: Bond-strength taxonomy (free → capillary → adsorbed → chemically bound) is the same reasoning needed for fluff/SAP/core moisture removal hierarchy.

V2.3 — Energy saving is secondary for low-tonnage, high-value products.
> "Since most pharmaceuticals are low-tonnage and high-value products, considerations of energy saving are usually of secondary importance in the selection of the drying process." (p.688)
Interpretation: A frank admission that, for premium hygiene actives (medicated wipe lotions), product quality outranks kWh/kg — useful when justifying premium dryer capex.

V2.4 — Thermal decomposition is first-order and Arrhenius-controlled.
> "Usually thermal decomposition follows a first-order reaction kinetics... k = A e^(−E/RT)... thiamine hydrochloride in aqueous solution has E = 92.18 kJ/mol and A = 9.58×10^10 1/min. Processing such solution at 90°C for 10 min would result in c/c₀ = 0.949 (i.e., about 5% loss)." (p.691)
Interpretation: Gives a usable kinetic model for active-ingredient thermal loss in drying — directly applicable to lotion/active stability on a wipe line.

V2.5 — Desorption isotherms (not sorption) govern drying; hysteresis separates colloidal bodies.
> "For drying purposes only desorption isotherms are of practical significance... Materials showing sorption isotherms intermediate between these two categories belong to the so-called capillary-colloid-porous bodies, and they include ASA and penicillin. The energy of the solid–moisture bond increases from capillary-porous to colloidal bodies." (p.691–692)
Interpretation: The desorption-isotherm / bound-moisture-energy ladder is exactly the moisture-spec logic for medical wipes' final water content and rewet.

V2.6 — Freeze drying yields a labile amorphous porous structure that collapses above a critical temperature.
> "After all moisture sublimes, a very fine porous structure remains that can be easily rehydrated... The structure is labile and can collapse if its temperature is raised above a certain temperature of collapse. This temperature can be as low as −40°C (e.g., for glucose and fructose) or −10°C (e.g., for starches and proteins) and increases with the increase of molecular weight of the lattice and its dryness." (p.699–700) (OCR rendered leading minus signs ambiguously; values read as −40°C glucose/fructose, −10°C starches/proteins.)
Interpretation: The collapse temperature is the glass-transition analogue of an amorphous solid — a polymer-stability map (see POP crossref).

## 3. Research Method (研究方法)

The chapter is a structured equipment-selection review (compiled by Pakowski & Mujumdar) with embedded empirical data tables. Method:
- Classification engineering: Sazhin/Rebinder 4-group scheme by minimum pore diameter (p.688–689, Table 29.1) and British-Pharmacopoeia particle-size classes (p.689, Table 29.2).
- Property characterization: particle-size distribution (sieve analysis, Fig. 29.1), thermal decomposition (gravimetric/kinetic, Arrhenius, p.691), sorption/desorption isotherms (gravimetric humidity-exposure, p.691; Table 29.3 bond-type & heat-of-sorption data).
- Equipment performance survey: evaporation-rate ranges and residence times per dryer type (p.693–702), consolidated in Tables 29.5–29.6 (process parameters for named drugs).
- Aseptic/solvent engineering: HEPA/class-100/sterilization protocols (p.704–708) and closed-cycle nitrogen purge math (p.708).
No new hygiene data; value is the selection discipline and the quantitative anchors (bond-energy ladder, freeze-collapse, aseptic specs).

## 4. Argumentation (论证逻辑)

- Premise A: Product quality is paramount; any contaminant/decomposition is unacceptable (p.687).
- Premise B: Dryer choice must match material form, thermal sensitivity, tonnage, and drying kinetics, which reduce to the moisture–material bond set by pore size (p.688).
- Step: Characterize particle size (flow/agglomeration), thermal decomposition (Arrhenius → safe T), and sorption isotherm (bound-moisture energy, hysteresis); then pick the dryer family (convective for free moisture, vacuum/freeze for thermolabile, closed-cycle for flammable solvent) and set residence time / T from Tables 29.5–29.6.
- Resolution: Enforce aseptic (HEPA, class 100, 200–250°C sterilization) and, where solvent is present, closed-cycle inert-gas recovery. The book-map closes on validation: "laboratory and often pilot-scale tests are necessary before a commercial-scale dryer may be designed with confidence" (p.703).
## 5. Role of This Chapter in the Book (在全书中的角色)

Ch.29 is the high-value, GMP/aseptic, contamination-sensitive drying chapter — the quality-assurance pole opposite Ch.32 (bulk, energy-cheap peat/biofuels). It complements Ch.4 (sorption isotherms), Ch.17 (conveyor/continuous webs), Ch.18 (infrared cure), and Ch.33 (fibrous bound moisture); its desorption-isotherm and bond-energy ladder feed directly into the moisture-spec reasoning those chapters assume.

## 6. Strengths and Weaknesses (贡献与局限)

Strengths
- Explicit GMP/aseptic discipline (p.687, p.704–708): HEPA 99.97% @ >0.3 µm, class-100, sterilization 200–250°C, USP 160–170°C/2–4 h.
- Gives a quantitative bond-strength taxonomy (p.688–689, 691–692) and a real Arrhenius thermal-decomposition example (p.691).
- Consolidates process data (Tables 29.5–29.6) and the freeze-collapse temperature concept (p.699–700).
- Honest that energy is secondary for high-value product (p.688).

Weaknesses
- Entirely pharmaceutical; no fibre, nonwoven, or SAP examples — every hygiene link is by analogy.
- Freeze-collapse temperatures are printed with ambiguous minus signs (OCR) and no derivation/reference — treat as indicative.
- Sazhin 4-group pore-class thresholds (Table 29.1) mix nm and time ranges inconsistently (groups III/IV overlap) — selection aid, not rigorous.
- No hygiene-specific residual-moisture validation data.

## 8. Micro close-reading (微观精读)

8.1 — The noncompromise quality clause (p.687). "Any deterioration of the product (e.g., by microbial infection, oxidation, thermal decomposition, contamination by metallic particles or by unremoved organic solvent) must be avoided at any cost." This sentence is the chapter's spine: it pre-justifies every expensive construct (polished stainless, inert gas, HEPA, freeze/vacuum). For medical-grade nonwovens and lotion wipes it is the exact compliance posture (bioburden, oxidation of actives, metal-freedom, residual-solvent).

8.2 — Bond-energy ladder (p.691–692). "The energy of the solid–moisture bond increases from capillary-porous to colloidal bodies. Adequate drying temperatures and residence times must be used for drying such difficult to dry solids." The capillary→colloidal bond-strength climb is the same ladder the hygiene engineer climbs for fluff (capillary) → fibre-bound → SAP-network (colloidal/osmotic) moisture; it dictates which dryer flux is safe.

8.3 — Thermal-decomposition kinetic example (p.691). "thiamine hydrochloride... E = 92.18 kJ/mol and A = 9.58×10^10 1/min. Processing such solution at 90°C for 10 min would result in c/c₀ = 0.949 (i.e., about 5% loss)." A worked Arrhenius calc the reader can copy for any active: pick E/A, set acceptable loss, back out max T/time. Directly reusable for wipe lotion stability windows.

8.4 — Freeze-dried amorphous collapse (p.699–700). "a very fine porous structure remains that can be easily rehydrated... The structure is labile and can collapse if its temperature is raised above a certain temperature of collapse. This temperature can be as low as −40°C (e.g., for glucose and fructose) or −10°C (e.g., for starches and proteins)." The collapse temperature is the polymer-glass-transition surrogate (POP crossref): amorphous solids are stable only below Tg; raise T and the glass softens/collapses. Critical for any freeze-dried or spray-dried amorphous active in a wipe.

8.5 — Aseptic/HEPA posture (p.704). "High-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters... claimed to remove up to 99.97% of all particles larger than 0.3 µm... For all high-sterility manufacturing operations, class 100 air cleanliness... must be maintained." The concrete cleanroom spec that medical-grade wipe/nonwoven lines adopt; combined with sterilization at 200–250°C (p.707) it is the contamination-control template.

## 9. Concept deep-dive (概念深掘)

9.1 Sazhin/Rebinder 4-group classification (§29.2, p.688–689). Based on minimum pore diameter: Group I nonporous/capillary-porous (>100 nm, free moisture only — NaCl, ASA); Group II uniformly/nonuniformly porous (down to 6 nm, physically/physicochemically bound — phenobarbital, sodium perborate); Group III microporous/colloid-capillary (6–4 nm and 4–2 nm subgroups — glucose, calcium gluconate); Group IV ultramicroporous (pore ≈ molecule size, chemically bound, moisture down to 0.2–0.1% — intense drying). Operational: links pore size → drying time → dryer type (Table 29.1).

9.2 Particle size & stickiness (§29.3.1, p.689–690). Pharma crystals seldom >1 mm; "the smaller the particles and the more viscous the fluid the stickier the product." Sticky-at-elevated-T from plasticizing/dissolving in moisture can defluidize a bed — a warning for any fine-powder SAP/additive handling on a fluidized line.

9.3 Thermal decomposition (§29.3.2, p.691). First-order: dc/dt = −k c; k = A e^(−E/RT). E/A tabulated for thiamine HCl (E=92.18 kJ/mol, A=9.58×10^10 1/min). Water content influences rate.

9.4 Sorption / hysteresis (§29.3.3, p.691–692). Sorption isotherm = equilibrium moisture vs RH; desorption branch is the drying-relevant one. Hysteresis intense for colloidal bodies (starch, gelatin — all tablet formulations with starch/gelatin are spray-dried colloids). Table 29.3 gives bond type (capillary/osmotic/monolayer/polymolecular) and max heat of sorption (kJ/kmol) for penicillin, streptomycin, ASA, ascorbic acid, starch, etc.

9.5 Dryer families (§29.4). Batch: ovens (cross-circulated shelves, internal filters vs cross-contamination), fluid bed (2–7× faster than tray, better uniformity). Continuous: band (80–100°C, 5–18 kg/m²·h), turbo-tray (turbo-shelf), pneumatic (240–1200 kg/m²·h, cocurrent, unbound moisture only, seconds), cyclone, spouted bed (pastes on inert bed), vibrated bed (30–100 kg/m²·h grid, up to 60 Hz, 30 min residence), spray (34–160 kg/m²·h, cocurrent safe for heat-sensitive). Indirect: drum (slurries/pastes), vacuum (double-cone 2–7 kg/h·m²; conical up to 10 kg/h·m² at 25–150 mbar, 40–60°C), freeze (sublimation; only most sensitive/expensive products).

9.6 Granulation (§29.4.4, p.702). Dry granulation via van der Waals (aspirin, acetaminophen, thiamine HCl, ascorbic acid); wet granulation via liquid bridges → capillary forces → solid bridges on crystallization in the drying phase.

9.7 Aseptic & closed-cycle (§29.7–29.8, p.704–708). HEPA + class-100 laminar flow; sterilization 200–250°C for hours (USP 160–170°C/2–4h); leakproof, smooth rounded internals, no sliding/rotating parts to avoid metallic contamination. Solvent recovery: inert N₂ loop, condenser; acetone spray dryer 110 kg/h → ~3 m³/h makeup N₂; explosive mixtures from solvent + powder mandate closed cycle.

## 10. Critique (批判性评估)

- Pharma-only lens. No fibre/nonwoven/SAP content; hygiene transfer is analogical and the brief itself flags this as the chapter's reach limit.
- OCR/print defects. Freeze-collapse minus signs ambiguous (p.699–700); Table 29.1 group thresholds overlap. Verify against print before quoting numbers.
- No uncertainty on residual-moisture spec. The chapter gives how to dry aseptically but not a target moisture for microbial stability — the hygiene reader must supply the microbiological limit.
- Energy explicitly secondary (p.688) — fine for pharma, but the hygiene reader should not import this priority to high-volume consumer lines where energy/kg dominates.
## 11. Hygiene-Material Relevance & Action Cards (卫材相关性与行动卡)

H11.1 — Adopt the noncompromise contamination clause for medical-grade lines (★★★★). Action: Mirror p.687's "must be avoided at any cost" for surgical drapes / medicated wipes: define bioburden, oxidation, metal-particle, and residual-solvent acceptance as zero-tolerance critical limits with polished-stainless/inert construction.

H11.2 — Use desorption isotherms + bond-energy ladder for moisture spec (★★★★). Action: Build the final-water-content spec from desorption isotherms (p.691) and the capillary→colloidal bond ladder; set dryer T/residence so bound (fibre/SAP) moisture, not just free surface water, reaches target — prevents rewet/clumping in the pad.

H11.3 — Run an Arrhenius loss check on every active before drying (★★★). Action: For lotion/active-coated wipes, take E/A of the active (or a conservative proxy), set acceptable loss (e.g., 5%), and back out the max web temp/residence (p.691 worked example) — reject dryer settings that exceed it.

H11.4 — Apply aseptic/HEPA/class-100 discipline to cleanroom converting (★★★★). Action: For medical nonwovens, install HEPA (99.97% @ >0.3 µm) + class-100 laminar-flow converting and a 200–250°C sterilization/validation protocol (p.704–707) on contact surfaces; eliminate sliding/rotating parts that shed metallic contamination (p.707).

H11.5 — Use closed-cycle inert gas where solvent/active is flammable or toxic (★★★). Action: Where wipe lotion or coating uses organic solvent or toxic actives, run a closed N₂ loop with condenser recovery (p.708) and compute makeup-gas purge from evaporation rate to avoid explosive mixtures.

H11.6 — Watch sticky/defluidization of fine powders (★★★). Action: Fine SAP fines or additive powders that plasticize in residual moisture can defluidize a fluid bed (p.690); set inlet T below the stick-point or pre-agglomerate before fluidized drying.
