# Chapter 9 — Drum Dryers

## 1. Chapter Overview

Chapter 9 (Wan Ramli Wan Daud) covers the conductive/contact drum dryer — a steam-heated rotating cylinder (or pair) that spreads a viscous liquid, slurry or paste into a thin adherent sheet and evaporates moisture by the intense heat flux of condensing steam inside the drum. The chapter's logic: (i) classify by drum count/config (atmospheric double/single/twin, enclosed, vacuum double); (ii) state operating variables (steam 2–7 bar, speed 2–30 rpm, nip 0.05–0.5 mm); (iii) describe feeding/spreading (nip, roller, dip, spray/splash) and the rheology of the boiling pool; (iv) analyse the four drying zones on the drum (initial application, initial sheet, slow drying, vacuum) with heat-flux and heat-transfer-coefficient data; (v) cover product handling, control, and the product list. For the hygiene reader the chapter is relevant through three polymer-science hooks: drum dryers explicitly dry polyacrylamides (p.209) — a water-absorbing polymer cousin of SAP; they gelatinize starch slurries into shear-thinning power-law pastes that swell several-fold (p.206–207) — an aqueous-polymer-gel process; and they expose the glass-transition behaviour of sugar sheets (p.210). The drum dryer is the conductive sibling of the rotary (Ch.7) and fluid-bed (Ch.8) chapters and a candidate for drying heat-sensitive polymer hydrogels under vacuum.

## 2. Key Viewpoints

### Viewpoint 1 — Drum dryers process viscous liquids/slurries/pastes by conductive heat.

- Page: p.201
- Quote: > "The drum dryer is commonly used to dry viscous, concentrated solutions, slurries or pastes on steam-heated drums. It can also be used to dry the concentrated solutions or slurries that become more viscous or pasty because of flashing or boiling off of moisture or of irreversible thermochemical transformations of their content that occur on their first contact with the hot drum surface."
- Interpretation: The drum dryer is a thin-film conductive dryer — ideal for polymer solutions/gels too viscous for spray (Ch.10) or too sticky for fluid beds (Ch.8). The "irreversible thermochemical transformations" clause is the mechanism behind starch cooking and, by extension, any in-situ polymer gelation on the drum.
### Viewpoint 2 — High heat flux + short time yields a porous, rapidly-rehydrating sheet.

- Page: p.202
- Quote: > "Exposure of the thin sheet to the high heat flux and high temperature for a short period of time can also impart a porous structure to the dried sheet because of the rapid formation of vapor bubbles within the sheet during 'boiling-like' drying. Porous products are excellent in instant food formulations because they are more readily wetted and can be easily rehydrated."
- Interpretation: The vapor-bubble porosity mechanism is exactly the structure a superabsorbent or hydrocolloid flake wants — instant wetting/rehydration. This is the drum dryer's value for polymer gels: a porous, free-flowing powder from a gel feed.
### Viewpoint 3 — Predictive modelling fails; pilot testing is mandatory.

- Page: p.204–205
- Quote: > "the performance of a drum dryer drying a specific product cannot be adequately predicted by theoretical or semitheoretical models but must be based on drying performance test of the product on a pilot plant drum dryer."
- Interpretation: Because material properties shift during drying (thermochemical change, rheology change), every feed — including a polymer gel — needs a pilot-drum test. The chapter admits "it is still a long way before the drum dryer can be modeled completely" (p.205).
### Viewpoint 4 — Starch gelatinization makes a swelling, shear-thinning polymer gel.

- Page: p.206–207
- Quote: > "The gelatinization of starch slurries into a shear-thinning power law paste also changes the spreading and the mixing regime in the pool … It also swells up several times its original volume. In this case, the boiling pool is transformed into a rotating, asymmetric cylinder of paste at the nip."
- Interpretation: Starch gelatinization is a polysaccharide sol→gel transition producing a non-Newtonian (power-law, n) paste that swells several-fold — a canonical aqueous-polymer-gel behaviour (see POP block). The sheet thickness for shear-thinning liquid is up to 1.26× the nip (p.226).
### Viewpoint 5 — Heat flux is enormous at the application zone.

- Page: p.207–208
- Quote: > "The sudden exposure of the liquid feed to the high temperature and intense heat flux on the drum surface at the application zone causes it to immediately heat up and boil … Most of the free moisture is evaporated during this initial boiling of the feed." (p.207); "The heat flux can be as high as 85 kW/m²" (p.208); "The overall heat transfer coefficient in this zone was reported to be between 2000 and 7000 W/m² K" (p.207), falling to "600 and 1250 W/m² K" in the initial-sheet zone (p.208).
- Interpretation: The application zone dominates free-moisture removal; the sheet-zone coefficient drops ~3–10× as the dried layer insulates. Critical for sizing a polymer-gel drum run.
### Viewpoint 6 — Polyacrylamides are a named drum-dried polymer.

- Page: p.209
- Quote: > "Chemicals that have been successfully dried on a drum dryer are polyacrylamides, and various salts such as silicate, benzoate, propionate, and acetate salts."
- Interpretation: Polyacrylamide (a water-soluble acrylate-family polymer, structural cousin of crosslinked sodium polyacrylate SAP) is an explicitly validated drum-dryer feed — direct evidence the technology suits acrylate polymer gels.
### Viewpoint 7 — Vacuum drum dryer spares heat-sensitive structure.

- Page: p.208–209
- Quote: > "A heat-sensitive material dried on a vacuum drum dryer suffers less damage and retains most of its structure and functionality than that dried in ordinary atmospheric drum dryer." (p.208); "Heat-sensitive products such as pharmaceuticals and vitamin-containing products can be dried in a vacuum drum dryer." (p.209)
- Interpretation: For a thermally labile polymer hydrogel (e.g. an enzyme/SAP composite), the vacuum variant lowers T and pressure, limiting degradation — the drum equivalent of the cocurrent/heat-sensitive rule in Ch.7.
### Viewpoint 8 — Sugar sheets expose a glass-transition sticky failure.

- Page: p.210
- Quote: > "if the sugar content is high, some of the sugar does not crystallize properly as drying proceeds but becomes molten instead at well above the glass transition temperature. The uneven scrapping of the doctor's blade at the rubbery and glassy parts of the sheet forms wrinkles … become 'sticks' in the final product."
- Interpretation: This is a textbook glass-transition (Tg) observation: above Tg the amorphous sugar is rubbery/molten and sticks; below it is glassy. The doctor blade sees both and produces defects. Directly relevant to polymer films/webs where crossing Tg mid-drying causes sticking (see POP block).

## 4. Argumentation Process

- Premise: A thin adherent sheet on a hot drum dries by conductive boiling at very high flux.
- Step 1: Classify hardware (§9.2) so the feed geometry is chosen.
- Step 2: State operating variables and the hard limit that feed must still form a sheet (§9.3, p.204).
- Step 3: Explain spreading as a rheological/coating-flow problem, highlighting the starch-gelatinization non-Newtonian case (§9.3.3).
- Step 4: Quantify the four zones (application → sheet → slow → vacuum) with heat-flux/U data (§9.3.4).
- Step 5: Conclude quality is set by many coupled variables and cannot be predicted — pilot test required (§9.5, §9.6).
- The argument is honest about its own limits: "Dynamic drum dryer models … can only predict the final temperature and moisture content … are only useful for steady process operation" (p.209).
## 5. Role in the Book

Chapter 9 is the conductive/contact member of the dryer catalogue, complementing Ch.7 (convective rotary), Ch.8 (convective fluidized bed), Ch.10 (convective spray), Ch.11 (sublimation freeze), Ch.12 (dielectric). It is the only chapter treating thin-film boiling of viscous/pasty feeds, which is the regime for polymer gels and starches — linking to Ch.4's conductive (Biot>1) drying and to the polymer-science layer in the POP block.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths:
- Explicit polymer feed: polyacrylamides (p.209) — proves acrylate-family gels are drum-dryable.
- Quantified zone heat fluxes (up to 85 kW/m², U 2000–7000 then 600–1250 W/m²K) — directly usable for sizing.
- Honest glass-transition and stick-formation discussion (p.210) — a real polymer-quality failure mode.
- Vacuum variant for heat-sensitive materials (p.208–209).

⚠️ Weaknesses / contestable points:
- "Performance … cannot be adequately predicted … must be based on … pilot plant drum dryer" (p.205) — no transferable property model for polymer gels.
- Spreading models assume Newtonian or simple power-law (n); a swelling, crosslinked hydrogel (SAP) violates the constant-power-law assumption (p.206).
- Critical boiling-flux Eq 9.2 is presented in garbled OCR form (see §8); the sugar-Tg stick discussion is qualitative, not predictive.
- No mention of fibre/dust/odour from polymer degradation; control is "trial and error" (p.209).

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Drum dryer (p.201): steam-heated rotating cylinder drying a thin adherent sheet conductively.
- Nip feeding (p.207): feed at the gap between counter-rotating drums/twin drums.
- Boiling pool (p.206): the liquid held at the nip that boils and gels (e.g. starch).
- Shear-thinning power-law paste (p.206): non-Newtonian (n) gel formed by starch gelatinization; swells several×.
- Critical boiling flux E (p.207, Eq 9.2): nucleate-boiling-crisis heat flux [equation garbled in source].
- Application zone (p.207): sudden contact, boil-off of most free moisture, U 2000–7000 W/m²K, heat flux ≤85 kW/m².
- Initial sheet / slow drying zones (p.208): U falls to 600–1250 W/m²K; evaporation by Eq 9.5 E=3.6×10³ h(Tₒ−Tₑ)/DH_v.
- Doctor's blade (p.210): knife scraping the dry sheet ~3 quadrants from feed.
- Vacuum double drum dryer (p.205, p.208): enclosed, condenser/scrubber/vacuum pump; for heat-sensitive feeds.
- Glass transition (Tg) stick formation (p.210): sugar molten above Tg → rubbery/glassy uneven scraping → "sticks".
## 8. Micro close-reading (ONE key paragraph)

The starch-gelatinization polymer-gel passage (p.206–207). Verbatim:
> "The gelatinization of starch slurries into a shear-thinning power law paste also changes the spreading and the mixing regime in the pool. The sheet is thinner for more shear-thinning liquid (n < 1.0). The paste material near the surface of the rotating drums experiences a higher shear rate than those further away in the bulk of the pool. The paste nearer the surface is therefore less viscous and readily rotates with the drum. On the other hand, the bulk of the paste is more viscous and may not move at all … It also swells up several times its original volume. In this case, the boiling pool is transformed into a rotating, asymmetric cylinder of paste at the nip."
Sentence-by-sentence:
1. "gelatinization … into a shear-thinning power law paste" — a polymer sol→gel transition yielding a non-Newtonian fluid.
2. "sheet is thinner for more shear-thinning liquid (n < 1.0)" — lower n (more shear-thinning) → thinner adherent sheet.
3. "paste near the surface … higher shear rate … less viscous" — wall slip: a low-viscosity layer rides the drum.
4. "bulk of the paste is more viscous and may not move at all" — stagnant core; mean residence time longer than a Newtonian pool (heat-sensitive material degrades more, p.207).
5. "swells up several times its original volume … rotating, asymmetric cylinder of paste at the nip" — the swelling gel is the polymer-gel signature.
💡 Standout line: this paragraph is the aqueous-polymer-gel drying mechanism in miniature — exactly the regime a crosslinked polyacrylate or polyacrylamide gel would present on a drum (see POP block).

## 9. Concept deep-dive

9.1 Conductive thin-film boiling. Unlike Ch.7/8 convective dryers, the drum transfers heat through the wall by condensing steam (latent heat), giving U 2000–7000 W/m²K at application — far above convective Uₐ. Most free moisture leaves in the first boil (p.207); the dried layer then insulates (U→600–1250). For a polymer gel this means a very fast surface set followed by internal diffusion control — echoing Ch.4's Biot>1 internal-control rule.

9.2 Boiling-pool rheology & wall slip. The starch case (p.206–207) shows the pool is non-uniform: a shear-thinning, lower-viscosity layer at the drum surface (wall slip) and a stagnant bulk. More shear-thinning (lower n) → thinner sheet. A swelling SAP/polyacrylamide gel would behave similarly, so sheet thickness cannot be set by nip width alone — the power-law index n (Eq 9.1) matters.

9.3 Critical boiling flux (Eq 9.2). Zuber's pool-boiling critical heat flux (p.207): E given as a function of steam density ρ_v, water density ρ_l, surface tension s, and gravity g. The source OCR is garbled; transcribable fragment reads "E 2.177 ρ_v^0.25 (ρ_v − ρ_l)^0.25 …" with ρ_l in the denominator — i.e. the nucleate-boiling-crisis flux scales with (surface-tension·g·(ρ_l−ρ_v)/ρ_l²)^0.25. [equation garbled in source — verify against print]. This caps the safe heat flux before film boiling/dryout.

9.4 Quality via glass transition (p.210). Sugar sheets cross Tg during drying; above Tg the amorphous phase is rubbery/molten and adheres to the blade, below it is glassy — the doctor blade scrapes unevenly, forming "sticks". This is the single most polymer-relevant quality mechanism in the chapter and generalises to any amorphous polymer web/film (SAP binder, nonwoven coating) crossing Tg mid-dry.

9.5 Vacuum for labile polymers. The vacuum double drum (p.205, p.208–209) drops T and pressure, retaining "structure and functionality" — the drum-form of heat-sensitive protection (cf. Ch.7 cocurrent, Ch.11 freeze).

9.6 Control immaturity. Setpoints are "trial and error for each product" (p.209); only final T and moisture are modelled; porosity, wetting, rehydration, crystal form are not. IR inference (Rodriguez et al. [35,36]) is the only on-line moisture attempt. A hygiene formulator must therefore qualify each polymer gel on a pilot drum.

## 10. Academic Critique & Open Questions

- Spreading and drying models assume Newtonian or constant-power-law fluids (p.206, Eq 9.1); a swelling, crosslinked hydrogel (SAP) has moisture-dependent n and free volume — outside the model's validity.
- Eq 9.2 (critical boiling flux) is OCR-garbled and the chapter gives no worked example; a hygiene engineer cannot use it as-printed.
- The Tg-stick failure (p.210) is described but never quantified (no Tg value, no stick-fraction model) — yet it is the decisive defect for polymer sheets.
- Open questions: (1) What power-law index n and swelling ratio does a crosslinked polyacrylate gel show at the nip, and how does it set sheet thickness? (2) At what drum-surface temperature does an SAP/polyacrylamide gel cross its Tg and stick to the doctor blade?

## 11. Hygiene-Material Relevance & Action Cards

Relevance to diaper/wipe/absorbent-core R&D: ★★★★☆ (drum drying of aqueous polymer gels — polyacrylamide, starch gelatinization; vacuum for heat-sensitive hydrogels; Tg-stick lesson for polymer films/webs).

ACTION CARD H11.1 — Pilot-test any polymer gel before scaling. Situation: drum-drying a SAP/polyacrylamide/starch gel. Apply: run the mandated pilot-drum performance test (p.205); set steam 2–7 bar, speed 2–30 rpm, nip 0.05–0.5 mm; verify sheet forms. Effect: avoids the "sheet may not form well" failure at high feed concentration (p.204).

ACTION CARD H11.2 — Exploit vapor-bubble porosity for instant-rehydration flakes. Situation: making a porous superabsorbent/hydrocolloid flake. Apply: use the high-flux short-time boiling regime (p.202, ≤85 kW/m²) to generate internal vapor bubbles → porous sheet. Effect: porous product wets/rehydrates fast — valuable for instant hygiene or absorbent composites.

ACTION CARD H11.3 — Use vacuum drum for labile hydrogels. Situation: drying a heat-/oxygen-sensitive polymer gel (enzyme-SAP, bioactive coating). Apply: vacuum double drum (p.208–209) to retain structure/functionality. Effect: less thermal/oxidative damage than atmospheric drum.

ACTION CARD H11.4 — Stay below the glass-transition stick point. Situation: drying a polymer sheet/film (SAP binder, nonwoven coating) on a hot surface. Apply: control drum speed / cool-air at the blade (p.210) so the sheet does not cross into the rubbery/molten Tg regime before takeoff. Effect: prevents sticking, "sticks", and wrinkles — the same defect class that ruins polymer webs in through-dryers.
