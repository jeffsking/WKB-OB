# Chapter 35 — Drying of Pulp and Paper

## 1. Chapter Overview (100–200 words)

Page numbers derived from PDF-page − 21; offset anchored on Ch.1 = p.1 and should be spot-checked against the print edition.

Chapter 35 (Polat & Mujumdar) is the single most commercially relevant chapter in this Handbook for a fluff-pulp / paper-grade procurement strategist. It treats papermaking as fundamentally "a massive dehydration operation" and separates pulp drying from paper drying. The paper-drying half develops the multicylinder (conduction) dryer section, its stacked heat-transfer resistances, steam-and-condensate systems, pocket ventilation, and the full menu of alternatives — Yankee/impingement, through-air, floater, radiant/IR, and the novel thermomechanical consolidators (press, Condebelt, impulse). The pulp-drying half covers conventional cylinder/air-floater, flash, and steam drying, and—critically for hygiene buyers—names the lower-moisture-bound thermal cross-linking that destroys fiber reconstitutability. The through-line is the energy/quality/speed trade-off: drying is "the most costly and energy-intensive step" yet removes the least water in absolute terms.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (with verbatim quotes + print pages)

### Viewpoint 1 — Drying is the costliest step yet removes the least water

- Page: p.791
- Quote: "Papermaking is essentially a massive dehydration operation. … In the United States, the production of over 60 million tons of paper per year entails the removal of over 80 million tons of water by thermal dryers. … Although drying removes the least amount of water in absolute terms, it still remains the most costly and energy-intensive step in the papermaking process."
- Interpretation: Sets the economic frame: the value-adding dehydration is mostly done by pressing (to 33–55% consistency before the dryer); thermal drying is a costly tail. Procurement leverage lies in minimizing the thermal tail via upstream dewatering and buying pulp already at the right MC.
### Viewpoint 2 — Cylinder drying is conduction-limited by stacked resistances

- Page: p.794
- Quote: "The multicylinder dryer section of a paper machine consists of a number (up to 70) of large, hollow, cast iron or steel cylinders over which the web passes. … The major mode of heat transfer is the conduction through the steam-heated cylinder shells."
- Interpretation: 85–90% of paper dryers are cylinders (Table 35.1, p.793). Heat must cross steam film → cast-iron shell → paper → air film; each resistance caps the evaporative flux. This is the physics behind machine-speed ceilings and the rationale for spoiler bars, ribbed dryers and impingement boosting.
### Viewpoint 3 — Pocket ventilation and condensate control are the hidden throughput levers

- Page: p.798
- Quote: "Pocket ventilation systems can increase the drying efficiency up to 20% depending upon the previous conditions."
- Interpretation: The moist air trapped in the "pocket" (Figure 35.10) retards the draw and creates cross-machine (CM) moisture nonuniformity. A 5.5°C steam-temperature drop on dryer-limited linerboard costs ~8% production (p.796); condensate rimming and noncondensable gases strangle capacity. These are operability facts a buyer infers from a supplier's reported reel-moisture uniformity.
### Viewpoint 4 — Drying strategy sets the strength–bulk–swellability trade-off

- Page: p.799
- Quote: "At the beginning of the drying process, the fibers are free to slide over one another; but as moisture is removed they tend to come together, and at the end fiber–fiber bonding takes place. Then the sheet tends to shrink. The restraint owing to dryer felt and the tension due to draw tend to prevent shrinkage. This restraint results in a high tensile strength and very little ability to stretch."
- Interpretation: Restrained (felt/draw) drying = high tensile, low stretch, compact; tension-free floater/through-air drying = higher bulk, stretch, TEA but lower modulus. This is precisely the sheet-strength-vs-bulk axis a fluff-pulp or wipe-substrate buyer must specify.
### Viewpoint 5 — Thermomechanical drying (press/Condebelt/impulse) builds bonds and cuts energy

- Page: p.810
- Quote: "Press, impulse, and Condebelt drying techniques have made significant progress in recent years. Each of these processes uses mechanical, thermal, and interactive effects in a unique way; because of that they are sometimes called thermomechanical web consolidation processes."
- Interpretation: Above a threshold temperature/pressure, drying consolidates the web (interfibre bonding) and uses 550–1,400 kJ/kg-H₂O vs 2,500–3,500 for press drying (Table 35.6, p.813). For a fluff buyer this is the inverse warning: high-temperature drying history means collapsed pores and lost swellability.
### Viewpoint 6 — Flash drying below the lower moisture bound cross-links the fiber (the hornification clause)

- Page: p.815
- Quote: "The lower moisture bound is critical, as overheating below it can cause thermal cross-linking that makes reconstitution of the original fiber difficult. Pulp temperature is maintained below 70°C in both stages, with air inlet temperatures of 400 and 170°C, respectively."
- Interpretation: THE procurement keystone. Over-drying market pulp in the final stage welds fibres via thermal cross-linking (hornification), raising defibration energy and hurting pad integrity. Suppliers control pulp temperature °C precisely to avoid this. This is the single most actionable sentence in the chapter for fluff-pulp qualification.

## 4. Argumentation Process

- Framing — papermaking = dehydration; drying is the costly tail (p.791–792).
- Paper-drying physics — three-stage drying curve; hygroscopic hysteresis; conduction-dominated cylinder dryers with stacked resistances (p.792–795).
- System constraints — steam supply/condensate removal, noncondensables, pocket ventilation as throughput limiters (p.796–799).
- Quality coupling — shrinkage/bonding, CM moisture nonuniformity, cockling, curl, wrinkles (p.799).
- Alternatives ladder — impingement → Yankee/MG → through-air → floater → radiant/IR → profilers → Papridryer → RF/microwave (p.800–809).
- Novel thermomechanical consolidation — press, Condebelt, impulse, superheated steam (p.810–813).
- Pulp drying — conventional, flash (with the hornification clause), steam (p.814–815).
- Conclusion — conventional multicylinder still dominant; novel tech may gain acceptance within a decade (p.813).
## 5. Role in the Book

Ch35 is the Handbook's core "cellulosic web" chapter and the direct industrial counterpart to Ch33 (Drying of Fibrous Materials). Where Ch33 develops the molecular/polymer physics of fibre–water (sorption, bound/free split, lignin-Tg bonding), Ch35 supplies the machine-scale realization: cylinder conduction resistances, press/Condebelt/impulse consolidation, and the pulp-flash hornification clause. It complements Ch34 (textiles) on Yankee/through-air and Ch36 (wood) on the upstream fibre source. For the hygiene reader it is the bridge from fibre science to the supplier's actual drying line.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths: Exceptional breadth—every dryer type a pulp/paper supplier could run is catalogued with benchmark shares, heat-transfer coefficients, and a unique comparative table (Table 35.6) of press/Condebelt/impulse. The explicit statement that over-drying causes "thermal cross-linking that makes reconstitution of the original fiber difficult" (p.815) is a rare, procurement-grade hornification warning. The 30% steam-dryer cost advantage (p.815) is a concrete commercial datum.
⚠️ Limitations/contestable: The chapter is a 2006-vintage review (copyright line "(cid:2)2006") embedded in the 4th ed.; some "novel" dryers (impulse, Condebelt) remain "in the development stage" and "no commercial application" as stated (p.809, 811), so claims of 500–8,000 kg/m²h impulse rates are lab/pilot-scale. Equations 35.2–35.3 are OCR-degraded (stray symbols); symbol definitions are scattered in nomenclature. No treatment of superabsorbent polymer and no explicit "hornification" vocabulary—the cross-linking clause is the closest the text comes.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Consistency / freeness: p.791, "fiber–water suspension with initial consistencies of 0.2 to 1.0% … consistencies rise to about 18 to 23%" after screening, "33 to 55%" before dryer, "6 to 9% moisture content" final.
- Constant-rate / falling-rate stages: p.792, drying divided into "warming-up … constant-rate … one or more falling-rate stages"; Montgomery observed constant rate "up to 10 to 15% moisture content" (p.792).
- Hygroscopic hysteresis: p.792, "This hysteresis phenomenon is known for many other hygroscopic substances … There is no satisfactory theory to explain the hysteresis."
- Multicylinder dryer: p.794, "up to 70" cylinders; cast-iron, 0.91–1.83 m diameter; conduction-dominated.
- Overall heat-transfer coefficient: p.795, reciprocal = sum of steam-film, shell-conduction, paper-film resistances (Eq 35.2).
- Pocket: p.799, "space-bounded dryer cylinder, felt roll, and the sheet approaching and leaving the cylinder … called the pocket"; moist air here retards drying.
- Through-drying: p.802, "drying permeable webs by the percolation of hot air through its mass"; needs a "minimum pressure difference (threshold pressure … related to surface tension forces)" (p.803).
- Thermomechanical web consolidation: p.810, press/impulse/Condebelt "sometimes called thermomechanical web consolidation processes."
- Thermal cross-linking (hornification): p.815, overheating below the lower moisture bound "can cause thermal cross-linking that makes reconstitution of the original fiber difficult."
- Market pulp MC: p.814, "drying up to 10% moisture (90% fiber, 10% water) is necessary" for market pulp.
## 8. Micro close-reading (ONE key paragraph)

p.815 (Flash drying, lower moisture bound):
> "The lower moisture bound is critical, as overheating below it can cause thermal cross-linking that makes reconstitution of the original fiber difficult. Pulp temperature is maintained below 70°C in both stages, with air inlet temperatures of 400 and 170°C, respectively. Exhaust for the second stage is mixed with inlet air to the first stage. Surface moisture is removed in the first stage (about 30% moisture), and the more delicate second-stage drying is carried out at a lower temperature. Dried pulp is cooled before baling."

Sentence-by-sentence: (1) Claims the lower moisture bound is critical and names the failure mode—thermal cross-linking → poor reconstitution; this is hornification stated in process language. (2) Operational rule pulp temperature held °C across both stages despite 400/170°C inlet air—heat is removed fast enough that fibre temperature never reaches the cross-linking window. (3) *Energy integration*—second-stage exhaust preheats first-stage inlet (efficiency). (4) *Staging*—bulk water off first (~30% MC), delicate finish at low T. (5) *Finishing*—cool before baling to lock in quality.
💡 Standout line: "overheating below it can cause thermal cross-linking that makes reconstitution of the original fiber difficult" — the entire fluff-pulp redry-damage mechanism in one sentence.

## 9. Concept deep-dive

Drying-rate limit & machine speed (the commercial ceiling). Cylinder drying is conduction-limited: h-values for newsprint are 285–315 W/m²K, fine paper 255–285, Kraft sack 230–255, linerboard 170–230 (Table 35.3, p.796). The authors state "it is very difficult to operate conventional cylinder dryer sections at speeds much higher than 1000 m/min" (p.800), which is why impingement/through-air/press consolidators exist. For a buyer, a supplier running near the speed ceiling will run hotter and tighter to hit tonnage—raising hornification risk.

The strength–bulk–swellability axis. Restrained felt/draw drying yields "high tensile strength and very little ability to stretch" (p.799); tension-free floater drying in the shrinkage range gives "higher stretch and tensile energy absorption … whereas the modulus of elasticity and stiffness decreases" (p.804). Through-air tissue "exceed[s] those on Yankee cylinders" rates "even at fairly low temperatures" and produces "soft, high-bulk products" (p.802–803). This is the lever a wipe/tissue substrate spec must name.

Thermomechanical consolidation and the bond-building inverse. Press drying at FPL achieved "average drying rates about 10-fold at 177°C and 20-fold at 288°C compared with conventional drying of linerboard" (p.810); Back & Anderson found at 150°C surface temperature "both the tensile index and the modulus of elasticity remain at high levels in the range of 39 to 70% initial solids content, but decreases sharply at higher solids content" (p.810). Impulse reaches 500–8,000 kg/m²h at 150–500°C, 1–5 MPa, 550–1,400 kJ/kg-H₂O (Table 35.6, p.813). Lesson for fluff: any drying history in the 150–300°C, pressed regime has built permanent interfibre bonds → collapsed swellability.

Hornification via thermal cross-linking (the hygiene transfer). The flash-drying clause (p.815) is the chapter's only explicit statement of irreversible drying damage to fibre reconstitutability. "Reconstitution of the original fiber difficult" = higher mechanical defibration energy, more knots/shives, weaker pad integrity, poorer rewet—exactly the fluff-pulp quality attributes a buyer pays for. It is the industrial echo of Ch33's "permanent set" and "sorptive diffusion" hornification vocabulary.

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- The chapter is a 2006 review; "within a decade" commercialization of impulse/Condebelt (p.813) was speculative and should be re-verified against current installations.
- Machine-scale models (Eq 35.2–35.7) are 1-D, isotropic, neglect curvature/edge conduction; fine for design but not for predicting local MC in a structured fluff web.
- No SAP, no explicit hornification vocabulary, no link to fibre-level sorption physics of Ch33—the cross-linking clause is the only bridge.
- Open question 1: Can the "lower moisture bound" thermal-cross-linking threshold be quantified per pulp grade (softwood Kraft vs CTMP vs BHKP) and tied to a measurable defibration-energy penalty?
- Open question 2: Does through-air vs flash vs steam drying leave a diferenciable "drying fingerprint" recoverable from supplied pulp (CSF, bulk, rewet) that a buyer could use as a qualification screen?

## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Overall relevance: ★★★★★ (core procurement chapter).

Action Card H35-1 — Interrogate the flash-dryer lower-moisture-bound control. Insight: p.815 — overheating below the lower moisture bound causes "thermal cross-linking that makes reconstitution of the original fiber difficult"; suppliers hold pulp °C. Action: When qualifying a flash-dried fluff supplier, demand the two-stage inlet/exit temperatures and the controlled pulp temperature; require a certificate that final-stage pulp temperature stayed below the cross-linking window. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Directly governs defibration energy, knot content and pad integrity of the fluff you buy.

Action Card H35-2 — Make defibration energy a drying-history KPI. Insight: p.815 reconstitution difficulty; p.810 press/impulse bond-building. Action: Require specific refining/defibration energy (kWh/BDt) and CSF (Canadian Standard Freeness) on the CoA; trend it lot-to-lot. A rising trend signals creeping over-dry/over-temp history. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Defibration energy is the dollar-and-performance proxy for hidden hornification.

Action Card H35-3 — Specify the strength–bulk–swellability target, not just "fluff pulp." Insight: p.799 restrained drying = high tensile/low stretch/compact; p.804 tension-free = high bulk/stretch/TEA; p.802 through-air = soft high-bulk. Action: For diaper fluff specify bulk (cm³/g), tensile, and wet-collapse; for wipes substrate specify bulk + TEA + softness. Match the drying method (through-air/flash vs cylinder) to the target. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Drying method is the dominant determinant of bulk vs strength you are buying.

Action Card H35-4 — Screen knot/shive and rewet as hornification symptoms. Insight: p.815 cross-linking → poor reconstitution; [inference] cross-linked fibres resist defibrating into fines, raising screen rejects and weakening rewet. Action: Add screen-rejects (Pulmac/shive) and rewet (gravimetric) to supplier specs; reject lots exceeding baseline. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Converts the invisible drying defect into measurable acceptance criteria.

Action Card H35-5 — Specify shipping MC against the bound/free split and require equilibrium-MC data. Insight: p.814 market pulp "drying up to 10% moisture (90% fiber, 10% water)"; Ch33 FSP bound/free split. Action: Set acceptance MC so incoming water is predominantly free (pressable/equilibrated out), and request equilibrium-MC at storage RH to predict in-bale drift. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Prevents paying evaporative energy for water pressing/equilibration already removes.

Action Card H35-6 — Price the drying-method cost advantage (steam dryer). Insight: p.815 first CTMP steam-dryer install "showed a 30% reduction in overall drying costs per ton of market pulp compared with an equivalent-size flash dryer"; claimed "minor or no effect on pulp quality." Action: When a supplier runs steam drying, verify the cost saving is passed through and confirm no quality regression (bulk, brightness, rewet). → 卫材相关性★★★★☆｜A supplier cost lever you can negotiate, if quality holds.

Action Card H35-7 — Demand reel-moisture uniformity as a quality proxy. Insight: p.799 overdrying "from 6 to 4% reel moisture for a typical 800 t/d capacity linerboard machine results in $189,000 additional steam cost and 11,200 t production loss per year"; pocket ventilation lifts efficiency up to 20% (p.798). Action: Ask suppliers for CM moisture-profile uniformity data; poor uniformity implies over-drying of part of the reel = local hornification. → 卫材相关性★★★★☆｜Uniformity is a low-cost signal of disciplined drying control.
