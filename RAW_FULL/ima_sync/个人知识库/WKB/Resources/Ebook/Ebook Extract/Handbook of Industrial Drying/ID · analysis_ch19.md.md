# Chapter 19 — Superheated Steam Drying

## 1. Chapter Overview (范围、对象与定位)

Chapter 19 (Arun S. Mujumdar) treats superheated steam drying (SSD) — replacing hot air/combustion gas with superheated steam as the convective drying medium (p.437). The chapter's logic: because the exhaust is also steam, its latent heat can be recovered by condensation or mechanical/thermocompression, making SSD's net energy 1000–1500 kJ/kg water versus 4000–6000 kJ/kg for hot-air drying (p.438) — a clear energy advantage. SSD also eliminates oxygen, so there is no fire/explosion hazard and no case-hardened skin forms (p.438). The body surveys industrial applications by product — sludges, coal, beet pulp, lumber, peat, paper and tissue (§19.2.6), wood particles, plus quality (§19.2.8, Table 19.2), miscellaneous (§19.3), exhaust-steam utilization (§19.4), and conclusions (§19.5). For the hygiene reader this is a ★★★★★ energy chapter: it is the live energy-reduction route for pulp/paper drying (the fluff-pulp feedstock of diapers), it quantifies fibre-property effects of steam vs air drying (tensile +20–30 %, burst +20 %, internal bond +10–20 %, crystallinity unchanged, brightness slightly worse above 300 °C — Table 19.2, p.446), and it states the hydrothermal / glass-transition limits that govern whether SAP and binder fibres survive (p.439). The honest caveat: "No commercial dryers exist for direct drying of paper webs with superheated steam" (p.444) — the technology is promising but not yet at scale for tissue.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (核心观点 + 原文逐句引证)

V2.1 — SSD net energy is 1000–1500 vs 4000–6000 kJ/kg for air; latent heat is recoverable.
> "the net energy consumption chargeable to the steam dryer can be very low… the 1000–1500 kJ/kg water removed for SSD compared with 4000–6000 kJ/kg water removed in a corresponding hot-air dryer. Thus, reduced energy consumption is a clear advantage of SSD." (p.438)
Interpretation: The headline energy number. For a fluff-pulp line the steam-exhaust recovery is what makes SSD ~3–4× more efficient than air; the saving only materializes if the exhaust steam has a use (p.437–438).

V2.2 — No oxygen ⇒ no fire/explosion and no case-hardened skin.
> "No oxidation or combustion reactions are possible in SSD. This means no fire or explosion hazards and often also a better quality product… many products that form 'case-hardened skin' in rapid drying do not form such water-impermeable skins in SSD." (p.438)
Interpretation: Two hygiene wins: the combustible-web fire risk (Ch.15/Ch.18) vanishes, and the surface-skinning defect that blocks internal moisture egress is absent — directly relevant to nonwoven/web quality.

V2.3 — Higher rates above the inversion temperature; faster falling-rate too.
> "Higher drying rates are possible in both constant and falling rate periods, depending on the steam temperature. The higher thermal conductivity and heat capacity of superheated steam leads to higher drying rates for surface moisture above the so-called inversion temperature. Below the inversion temperature, drying in air is faster… Also, it is known that many products… do not form such water-impermeable skins in SSD." (p.438)
Interpretation: The inversion temperature is the crossover where steam beats air; below it air is faster. Note indirect heating lowers inversion (250→170 °C, p.439) — a design lever.

V2.4 — Tg/melt limit: products that transform at steam saturation T cannot be SSD.
> "Products that may melt, undergo glass transitions, or be otherwise damaged at the saturation temperature of steam at the dryer operating pressure cannot clearly be dried in superheated steam even if they contain only surface moisture." (p.439)
Interpretation: The polymer-science boundary. At 1 bar steam saturation is 100 °C (safe for most binders), but at 5 bar it is ~150 °C — near PP sheath Tm. Low-pressure SSD is recommended for Tg-sensitive products (p.446). See POP trailer.

V2.5 — Paper/tissue SSD: no commercial unit yet, but tissue is the imminent application.
> "No commercial dryers exist for direct drying of paper webs with superheated steam. For the first time, Mujumdar proposed a variety of possible configurations for direct and direct/indirect drying of paper using superheated steam… Application to tissue drying (impingement and through drying) appears to be the more imminent industrial-scale operation." (p.444–445)
Interpretation: The fluff-pulp/tissue link is explicit but pre-commercial; tissue (highly permeable) is named as the near-term target, exactly the diaper-facing substrate.

V2.6 — Fibre-property effects: tensile +20–30 %, burst +20 %, bond +10–20 %, crystallinity unchanged.
> "Burst 20% enhancement (typical); Tensile index 20–30% improvement; Scott internal bond strength 10–20% higher; Crystallinity No measurable change; Brightness Above 300°C, a little worse." (Table 19.2, p.446)
Interpretation: Steam drying improves strength indices vs air drying and leaves cellulose crystallinity untouched, but yellows (brightness loss) above 300 °C — the hydrothermal/oxidative trade the brief asks about.

## 3. Research Method & Data Sources

The chapter is an industrial-survey + feasibility review (Mujumdar; McGill handsheet tests, Finnish pilot, vendor data). Method:
- Energy-balance comparison: SSD net vs air, with/without exhaust-steam reuse (p.438, p.445).
- Product-by-product case studies: sludges, coal, beet pulp, lumber, peat, paper/tissue, wood particles (§19.2.1–19.2.7) with capacities, temperatures, energy figures.
- Quality testing: McGill static-impingement handsheet tests, Finland dynamic pilot (Table 19.2, p.446); Salin particleboard, Yoshida/Hyodo fibre dry-spinning (p.446–448).
- Feasibility factors: Table 19.3 (p.447); technoeconomic criteria (p.446, p.449).
No new fundamental modelling; value is the energy figures, the inversion-temperature concept, and the fibre-quality database.

## 4. Argumentation Process

- Premise A: Exhaust of SSD is recoverable steam → net energy far below air if the steam is reused (p.437–438).
- Premise B: No O₂ ⇒ no fire/explosion, no casehardening, better quality (p.438).
- Step: Convert any direct dryer (flash/fluid-bed/spray/impingement/conveyor) to SSD (p.439); pick low pressure for Tg-sensitive products (p.446); recover exhaust via compression/condensation (§19.4).
- Resolution: SSD is justified for large, continuous, low-value, high-moisture, fire-risk or quality-sensitive streams (Table 19.3, p.447); paper/tissue is the promising near-term niche (p.445) with 15–85 % energy savings depending on integration (p.450).
Honest limits: high capital/ancillary cost, complex sealing, start-up condensation (+10–15 % residence, p.439), fouling, limited field experience (p.438–439).
## 5. Role in the Book

Ch.19 is the energy-recovery convective variant — the steam-medium counterpart invoked by Ch.16 (flash, p.399), Ch.17 (conveyor, p.439), and Ch.18 (impingement, p.440) as the explosion-free medium. Its distinct contribution is the latent-heat recovery economics and the fibre-quality evidence, which no other chapter supplies. It is the handbook's answer to the energy-cost/tonne question for pulp/paper.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

Strengths
- Quantifies the energy prize: 1000–1500 vs 4000–6000 kJ/kg (p.438); paper 1500 kJ/kg net (p.445) vs 4800–5700 air (p.445); beet pulp 2900 vs 5000 (p.441); paper savings 15–85 % (p.450).
- Gives the fibre-property database (Table 19.2): strength up, crystallinity unchanged, brightness worse >300 °C (p.446).
- States the Tg/melt limit explicitly (p.439) and the no-casehardening / no-fire advantage (p.438).
- Lists the exact dryer types convertible to SSD and the feasibility factors (Table 19.3).

Weaknesses / contestable
- Pre-commercial for paper/tissue. "No commercial dryers exist for direct drying of paper webs with superheated steam" (p.444); tissue is "imminent" but not deployed. Hygiene relevance is prospective, not current practice.
- Ancillary cost dominates. "Cost of the ancillaries… is typically much more significant than the cost of the steam dryer alone" (p.438); SSD "justifiable only for very large tonnage, continuously operated systems" (p.438).
- Steam sink required. Savings vanish if exhaust steam has no use (p.437–438) — a mill-integration prerequisite.
- Brightness loss at high T. Hydrothermal yellowing above 300 °C (p.446) is a real optical defect for white fluff/pulp.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Superheated steam drying (SSD): steam replaces air as convective medium; exhaust is recoverable steam (p.437).
- Inversion temperature: crossover where steam drying out-rates air; lowered by indirect heating (250→170 °C, p.439).
- Net energy consumption: SSD 1000–1500 kJ/kg vs air 4000–6000 (p.438).
- No case-hardened skin: SSD advantage (p.438).
- Saturation-temperature limit: products that melt/undergo Tg at steam saturation T excluded (p.439).
- Vapor recompression / thermocompression: exhaust-steam recovery (§19.4).
- Papridryer: impingement + through-drying with steam (p.444–445).
## 8. Micro close-reading (微观精读)

8.1 — The energy sentence (p.438). "the 1000–1500 kJ/kg water removed for SSD compared with 4000–6000 kJ/kg water removed in a corresponding hot-air dryer. Thus, reduced energy consumption is a clear advantage of SSD." This is the single most important number in the chapter for the brief's energy question: SSD is 3–4× more energy-efficient provided the exhaust steam is reused. The qualifier (reuse) is the whole story — without a steam sink the advantage collapses (p.437).

8.2 — The Tg/melt gate (p.439). "Products that may melt, undergo glass transitions, or be otherwise damaged at the saturation temperature of steam at the dryer operating pressure cannot clearly be dried in superheated steam even if they contain only surface moisture." Note the subtlety: even surface-moisture products fail if their polymer transforms at steam saturation T. At 1 bar that T is 100 °C (safe); at 5 bar ~150 °C (approaching PP sheath Tm). Low-pressure SSD is the workaround (p.446).

8.3 — Tissue as imminent (p.444–445). "Mujumdar proposed a variety of possible configurations for direct and direct/indirect drying of paper using superheated steam… Application to tissue drying (impingement and through drying) appears to be the more imminent industrial-scale operation." The impingement + through-drying (Papridryer) config is exactly Ch.15/Ch.17 merged under steam — and tissue is the permeable grade named.

8.4 — Fibre-quality table (Table 19.2, p.446). "Tensile index 20–30% improvement; Scott internal bond strength 10–20% higher; Crystallinity No measurable change; Brightness Above 300°C, a little worse." Steam drying strengthens the sheet (no oxidative chain scission, no casehardening) while leaving cellulose crystallinity intact; only brightness suffers at high T. This is the "strong effects on fibre properties" the brief flags.

8.5 — Savings band (p.450). "the energy savings potential in paper drying can range from 15 to 85%." The spread shows savings are integration-dependent, not guaranteed — honest and important for scoping.

💡 Standout line: "Tensile index 20–30% improvement … Crystallinity No measurable change" (Table 19.2, p.446) — steam drying strengthens pulp without degrading its crystalline structure, the fibre-property result that matters most to fluff-pulp procurement.

## 9. Concept deep-dive (概念深掘)

9.1 Energy-recovery logic (§19.1, §19.4). Air-dryer exhaust latent heat is "generally difficult and expensive to recover" (p.437); SSD exhaust is steam at lower enthalpy, recoverable by condensation or mechanical/thermocompression. Net SSD = 1000–1500 kJ/kg (p.438); paper net as low as 1500 kJ/kg if exhaust fully reused (p.445) vs 4800–5700 air (p.445). Savings 15–85 % (p.450).

9.2 Inversion temperature (§19.2). Above it, steam's higher k and C_p give faster surface-moisture removal; below it air is faster. Indirect heating lowers inversion 250→170 °C (p.439) — a second heat-input path widens the steam-advantage window.

9.3 Convertible dryer types (p.439–440). Flash, fluid bed, spray, impinging jet, conveyor, agitated bed, packed/through-circulation, impinging-stream, vibrated fluid bed — i.e. Ch.16/17/18 hardware runs on steam. Heat-transfer intensity examples: steam-fluidized beet pulp h ≈ 200 W/(m²K) vs 20–50 in steam-tube rotary (p.441); sludge steam-fluidized volumetric h_v up to 90 kcal/(m³ h °C) (p.439).

9.4 Fibre-property evidence (Table 19.2, p.446). McGill handsheet impingement-steam tests vs air: burst +20 %, tensile index +20–30 %, internal bond +10–20 %, stretch/folding/permeability/wet-strength little change, crystallinity unchanged, brightness slightly worse >300 °C. Salin: steam-dried wood particles give particleboard with 9 % less glue (p.446). Yoshida/Hyodo: dry-spinning in superheated solvent vapour → "stronger and finer fibers without surface wrinkles" (p.446–448).

9.5 Feasibility (Table 19.3, p.447). Justified when: energy cost high + product low-value/commodity; quality superior in steam; fire/explosion/oxidation risk high; large continuous tonnage; exhaust steam has a use.

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- Prospective, not proven, for paper/tissue. The chapter itself states no commercial paper-web SSD exists (p.444); all strength data are McGill static / Finnish pilot handsheet tests (p.446, p.521). Scale-up to a running tissue machine is unvalidated.
- Steam sink dependency. The 1000–1500 kJ/kg figure assumes exhaust reuse; a standalone SSD with no sink reverts toward air-dryer economics (p.437–438). The brief's energy-cost/tonne must state this condition.
- Brightness/yellowing. Hydrothermal yellowing above 300 °C (p.446) is quantified only as "a little worse"; the mechanism (pentosan/hemicellulose hydrolysis, lignin redistribution) is not analysed.
- Open question: For a fluff-pulp + SAP + bicomponent-fibre web, what is the maximum safe steam saturation T before the PP/PET sheath crosses Tm or SAP network hydrolyses? The chapter gives the Tg/melt principle (p.439) but no hygiene-specific window.
- Open question: Does SSD's no-casehardening benefit translate to fewer linting/defect issues in the final nonwoven, or only to the pulp sheet? Not separated in Table 19.2.
## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Relevance rating: ★★★★★ (energy + fibre-property, with honest pre-commercial caveat for tissue).

H11.1 — Scope SSD as the pulp/paper energy lever (★★★★★). Action: For fluff-pulp or tissue lines, model SSD at 1000–1500 kJ/kg vs 4000–6000 air (p.438); require a steam sink (mill integration) for the saving to survive (p.437).

H11.2 — Expect stronger pulp, watch brightness (★★★★★). Action: Steam drying raises tensile +20–30 % and internal bond +10–20 % with crystallinity unchanged (Table 19.2, p.446); cap steam T below ~300 °C to avoid brightness/yellowing loss (p.446) on white fluff.

H11.3 — Use low-pressure SSD for binder/SAP sensitivity (★★★★). Action: Where PP/PET sheath or SAP could approach steam saturation T, run low-pressure SSD (p.439, p.446) so saturation T stays below the polymer Tm/Tg; see POP trailer.

H11.4 — Eliminate fire/explosion on combustible webs (★★★★★). Action: SSD removes O₂ → no fire/explosion and no case-hardened skin (p.438); a compliance path for combustible nonwoven/tissue lines that Ch.15/Ch.18 flag as hazardous.

H11.5 — Combine impingement + through-drying under steam (★★★★). Action: Adopt the Papridryer-style steam impingement + through-drying config (p.444–445) for tissue; it is the Ch.15/Ch.17 merge under an explosion-free medium.

H11.6 — Budget ancillaries, not just the dryer (★★★★). Action: SSD ancillary cost (feed/discharge seals, exhaust recovery) exceeds the dryer alone and needs very large continuous tonnage (p.438); scope capital accordingly before committing.
