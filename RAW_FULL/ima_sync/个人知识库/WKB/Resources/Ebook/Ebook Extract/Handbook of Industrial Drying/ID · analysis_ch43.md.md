# Chapter 43 — Drying of Coal

## 1. Chapter Overview (100–200 words)

Page numbers derived from PDF-page − 21; offset anchored on Ch.1 = p.1 and should be spot-checked against the print edition.

Chapter 43 (Pikoń & Mujumdar) is a large equipment catalogue of coal-drying technology: rotary, rotary-tube, chamber-with-stirrers, pneumatic, fluid-bed, spouted-bed, vibratory, mill-type, shaft, moving-bed, and superheated-steam dryers, followed by low-rank-coal, hot-oil, hot-water, combined grinding-drying, Fleissner, biomass, and mechanical-thermal-expression variants. The authors repeatedly return to two themes with genuine cross-industry value: (1) safety — explosion panels, cocurrent operation to avoid ignition, low-oxygen/inert (superheated-steam) drying to eliminate fire/explosion and spontaneous-combustion risk in reactive low-rank coals; and (2) bulk-solids drying economics — heat consumption quoted per kg of water evaporated (≈2800–3700 kJ/kg H₂O depending on dryer type). For a hygiene reader the substrate is irrelevant, but the safety and energy-economics lessons transfer to any combustible-dust / fine-powder drying or airlaid line.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (with verbatim quotes + print pages)

### Viewpoint 1 — Coal drying splits moisture into constant-rate surface and falling-rate hygroscopic phases

- Page: p.992
- Quote: "The evaporation of surface moisture takes place in the first drying period at a constant drying rate. … Hygroscopic moisture depends on the rank of coal; it decreases with the age of coal. The evaporation of hygroscopic moisture takes place in the second drying period at a falling drying rate."
- Interpretation: Same two-period structure the hygiene reader saw in Ch33/Ch35 (free vs bound water). The transfer stops at the generic physics — coal rank has no analogue in fluff/SAP, but the constant-then-falling curve does.
### Viewpoint 2 — Rotary dryers run cocurrent to avoid ignition

- Page: p.992
- Quote: "Generally these dryers operate in the cocurrent mode to avoid the possibility of ignition. The drying medium is hot air or combustion gases."
- Interpretation: A direct-fired design choice driven purely by fire safety. Relevant to any hygiene line where hot combustion gas meets a combustible particulate (fluff, SAP fines, airlaid web).
### Viewpoint 3 — Pneumatic dryers carry explosion flaps

- Page: p.999
- Quote: "The system is equipped with explosion flaps (17)."
- Interpretation: Even a standard coal pneumatic dryer is built with pressure-relief explosion panels. The hygiene lesson: any high-velocity pneumatic conveyor of fine combustible dust (fluff, SAP, airlaid furnish) should carry the same.
### Viewpoint 4 — Low oxygen / inert drying eliminates fire-explosion and spontaneous-combustion risk

- Page: p.1009
- Quote: "It is important not to have high oxygen content in the drying gas to avoid explosion and fire hazard. This is a key issue in coal drying. Low-rank coals can be highly reactive and hence they are more susceptible to fire and explosion hazard due to spontaneous combustion. Hence indirect dryers have some advantages in this regard."
- Interpretation: The central safety thesis. Inert (low-O₂) or indirect drying is the defence against combustible-dust explosions and self-heating — directly transferable to fine hygroscopic powders on hygiene lines.
### Viewpoint 5 — Superheated steam drying removes the fire/explosion hazard entirely

- Page: p.1007
- Quote: "Among those of major interest in coal drying are no fire or explosion hazard, ability to dry at elevated temperatures, faster drying rates leading to small equipment size … Low-temperature (150 to 200 °C) … greatly reduces the danger of ignition."
- Interpretation: Steam as the drying medium is intrinsically inert. The chapter quantifies the rate gain (≈0.2 kg/m³·s at 200 °C inlet vs ≈0.014 rotary, ≈0.14 pneumatic) — a generic bulk-drying-economics data point.
### Viewpoint 6 — Drying is an energy-cost line quoted per kg of water removed

- Page: p.993 / p.1007
- Quote: "The heat consumption in such dryers amounts to about 3700 kJ/kg H₂O." … superheated steam "The heat consumption is 2800 to 3500 kJ/kg H₂O."
- Interpretation: The chapter's recurring economic metric is kJ per kg water evaporated. This is the right denominator for comparing any two drying routes (and matches the Ch35 energy framing).

## 5. Role in the Book

Ch43 is the bulk-solids / high-tonnage drying chapter. It parallels Ch37 (minerals) and Ch38 (sludge) in treating large particulate streams, and complements Ch44 (feeding) and Ch45 (emission control) for any solid-handling dryer. Its safety and energy-economics frames are generic enough to apply to non-coal powders.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths: Exceptional equipment breadth; concrete operating tables (Table 43.1, 43.2) and full sizing equations; explicit, repeated treatment of fire/explosion/spontaneous-combustion safety and inert (steam) drying — rare and valuable. Energy quoted consistently as kJ/kg H₂O.
⚠️ Limitations/contestable: Substrate (coal) has no hygiene analogue; equations are generic bulk-solids correlations, not validated for cellulose/SAP. "Low-rank coals can be highly reactive … spontaneous combustion" (p.1009) is asserted without a kinetic/self-heating model. OCR degrades several equations (e.g., porosity Eq 43.30, Eq 43.44). The greenhouse-gas framing (p.1003) is dated (2006).

## 8. Micro close-reading (ONE key paragraph)

p.1009 (Recent developments — safety):
> "It is important not to have high oxygen content in the drying gas to avoid explosion and fire hazard. This is a key issue in coal drying. Low-rank coals can be highly reactive and hence they are more susceptible to fire and explosion hazard due to spontaneous combustion. Hence indirect dryers have some advantages in this regard."

Sentence-by-sentence: (1) Directive — oxygen control in the drying gas is the primary explosion/fire prevention lever. (2) Severity — this is "a key issue," not a footnote. (3) Mechanism — reactive (low-rank) material self-heats → spontaneous combustion → explosion risk. (4) Design consequence — indirect (non-oxygen) dryers are preferred where self-heating is likely. The logic is the universal combustible-dust safety rule: remove the oxidant, not just the ignition source.
💡 Standout line: "not to have high oxygen content in the drying gas to avoid explosion and fire hazard" — the inert-atmosphere principle that transfers to any fine-powder drying line.

## 9. Concept deep-dive

Inert / low-O₂ drying as the explosion defence. The chapter makes two distinct safety arguments: (a) avoid ignition by design — cocurrent rotary dryers (p.992), explosion flaps on pneumatics (p.999); and (b) remove the hazard by removing oxygen — low-O₂/inert gas and, most decisively, superheated steam drying which "no fire or explosion hazard" (p.1007) because the medium is non-oxidising. For hygiene lines handling fine combustible powders (fluff fibres, SAP fines, airlaid furnish), argument (b) is the same engineering logic used in inerted pneumatic conveying and nitrogen-blanketed dryers.

Bulk-solids drying economics via kJ/kg H₂O. Every dryer type is benchmarked by heat consumption: rotary ≈3700, steam 2800–3500 kJ/kg H₂O (p.993/1007). The chapter also ranks drying rate per unit volume (rotary 35–120, pneumatic 400–900 kg/m³h, steam ≈0.2 kg/m³·s, p.993/1007). These are generic bulk-drying cost metrics: for hygiene, the lesson is to compare drying routes on energy per kg water removed, not per kg product — the same denominator Ch35 used for pulp.

Where transfer stops. The substrate-specific physics — coal rank governing hygroscopic moisture (p.992), spontaneous combustion of low-rank coals (p.1009), coking/briquetting MC targets (p.992) — has no cellulose/SAP analogue. The two-period drying curve and the fluidization/minimum-velocity correlations are generic, but their constants (Archimedes-based Re_cr, porosity 0.55–0.75) are calibrated for coal, not fluff or SAP.

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- No self-heating kinetic model (no Frank-Kamenetskii / adiabatic-ramp data); "spontaneous combustion" (p.1009) is asserted, not quantified.
- Heat-consumption figures are plant/vendor averages; no separation of latent heat vs losses, so true thermodynamic efficiency is not derivable.
- Equations carry OCR damage (porosity Eq 43.30, Eq 43.44) and are coal-calibrated.
- Greenhouse-gas framing (p.1003) is 2006-vintage and not updated.
- Open question 1: Can the chapter's inert (steam) drying safety logic be formalised as an oxygen-concentration limit for fine hygroscopic hygiene powders, the way it is for coal?
- Open question 2: Are the kJ/kg-H₂O benchmarks portable to fluff/SAP drying, or must they be re-derived for cellulose/acrylate systems?
