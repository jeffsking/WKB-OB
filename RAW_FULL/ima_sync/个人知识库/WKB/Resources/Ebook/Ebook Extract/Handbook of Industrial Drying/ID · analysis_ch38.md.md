# Ch38 — Dewatering and Drying of Wastewater Treatment Sludge

## Academic-Depth Chapter Analysis · Handbook of Industrial Drying (4th ed., Mujumdar, CRC Press)

Page-number caveat: Print page = PDF page − 21; offset anchored on Ch.1 = p.1 and should be spot-checked against the print edition. (Source markers inside this chapter confirm the anchor, e.g. the embedded tag "PDF page 911 (print p.890)".) Where a verbatim marker was missing or ambiguous, the convention [未找到原句/页码缺失] is used and no page is invented.

## 1. Overview

Chapter 38 reviews the complete water-removal chain for wastewater-treatment sludge: (i) moisture classification (how tightly water is bound), (ii) mechanical dewatering (filtration, pressing, centrifugation — water removed without evaporation), and (iii) thermal drying (direct, indirect, combined, and hybrid dryers). Its organizing thesis is economic and environmental: because sludge arrives at >95% moisture, evaporating that water thermally is ruinously expensive, so the engineering logic is to push as much dewatering as possible into the cheap, non-evaporative mechanical stage before any thermal drying is justified. The chapter is a process-equipment survey aimed at plant designers and utility engineers, not at polymer or material scientists.

Reader-fit for the hygiene-materials / fluff-pulp strategist: the substrate (municipal/industrial sludge) is far from absorbent hygiene products, so direct process transfer is thin. The durable, substrate-independent lessons are two: (1) the dewatering-before-evaporation energy economics rule — which applies equally to dewatering a SAP/fiber slurry or a fluff-pulp furnish before a dryer — and (2) the sticky/plastic-phase handling problem that appears whenever a wet, hydrophilic solids suspension is dried through an intermediate paste zone. Relevance rating: ★☆☆☆☆ (one star) — a real process-economics lesson, but low substrate relevance.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (verbatim, with print pages)

VP-1 — Sludge is overwhelmingly water, so evaporation is the cost driver.
> "Wet sludge from wastewater treatment plants has greater than 95% moisture content." (p.900, Conclusion)
> "The moisture in sludge can be as high as 99%." (p.887, §38.3)

VP-2 — Dewatering (no evaporation) must precede thermal drying; this is the chapter's central economic claim.
> "Sludge drying is an energy intensive process. Consequently dewatering, i.e., removing water without evaporation, becomes an important process before thermal drying is utilized." (p.888, §38.4)

VP-3 — Bound water is the thermodynamic floor of mechanical dewatering.
> "The water that can be removed by mechanical dewatering is usually termed as 'free water' and the remaining as 'bound water.' The bound water content is the theoretical limit of mechanical dewatering [17]." (p.887, §38.3)
> "The bound water content can be determined by methods such as dilatometric determination, vacuum filtration, expression, drying, and thermal analysis. The dilatometric method is based on the assumption that free water can be frozen at −20°C." (p.887–888, §38.3)

VP-4 — The four moisture classes and how each is released.
> "This distribution takes the following forms [16]: (a) free moisture that is not attached to the sludge particles and can be removed by gravitational settling; (b) interstitial moisture that is trapped within the flocs of solids or exists in the capillaries of the dewatered cake and can be removed by strong mechanical forces; (c) surface moisture that is held on the surface of the solid particles by adsorption and adhesion; and (d) intracellular and chemically bound moisture." (p.887, §38.3)

VP-5 — Mechanical dewatering raises solids only to a modest ceiling; numbers matter.
> "The pressure from the tensioned belts squeezes the water out of the sludge with a continuous cake discharged at about 15 to 25% solid content when the feeding solid concentration varies from 2 to 5%." (p.889, §38.4)
> "The current generation of centrifuges can achieve solid contents of 25 to 35% [34]." (p.890, §38.4)

VP-6 — Drying passes through a sticky/plastic phase that must be mechanically sheared.
> "Once the sticky zone (solid content between 55 and 70%) is reached, the sludge turns into a paste and strong shearing is necessary in order to have a high degree of mixing." (p.891, §38.5.2)
> "in an indirect drying system, the dried sludge is frequently mixed with raw sludge to have a solid content of about 70% in order to avoid the sticky zone." (p.891, §38.5.2)
> "the physical status of the sludge will change from a wet zone to a sticky zone before granulation depending on the solid content [54]." (p.891, §38.5.2)

VP-7 — Direct vs. indirect dryer trade-off: emissions vs. rate.
> "Direct dryers are simple in design but the vapors released from the sludge have to be separated from the drying medium, especially in the situation when the drying medium is to be recycled to save energy." (p.892, §38.5.3)
> "The indirect drying system has the advantage of producing minimal amounts of vapors and is therefore easy to manage [3]." (p.892, §38.5.3)
> "The drying rate of indirect dryers may be lower than that of direct dryers because the latter can operate at much higher temperatures." (p.892, §38.5.3)

VP-8 — Direct drying of fine powder at high temperature carries explosion risk.
> "In direct drying with high solid contents at high temperatures, prevention of the explosion of the fine powders must be seriously considered." (p.899, §38.5.6)

VP-9 — Thermal drying is also a pathogen/sterilization step.
> "Thermal sterilization is a good by-product of thermal drying. The US EPA requirement of 70°C for 2 h is equivalent to 90°C for 10 to 15 min, which can be easily satisfied by indirect dryers." (p.899, §38.5.6)

VP-10 — Economics: dewatering is cheap, drying is capital-heavy, waste-heat reuse dominates.
> "The cost of sludge dewatering ranges from about US$34 to 57/t of dry solids at respectively 150 and 60 t/day of dry solids using a high-throughput centrifuge. In the case of belt filter presses, the cost for a similar dewatering capacity would be US$40 and US$64, respectively [7]." (p.899, §38.5.6)
> "The capital investment for sludge drying is roughly US$1.8 million/t/h evaporation rate. Variations can be found from the low-cost flash drying systems to the highly sophisticated expensive closed-loop systems." (p.899, §38.5.6)
> "Utilizing the waste heat from the burning of sludge is considered to be very important. For example, flue gas drying preceding incineration can save as much as 60% on the cost compared with direct incineration in large sludge plants [78]." (p.899, §38.5.6)

## 3. Research Method

The chapter is a review/survey article, not a primary experimental study. Its method is:
- Taxonomy + equipment catalogue: water is classified by binding strength; dewatering and drying equipment are enumerated with schematic figures (Figures 38.2–38.16).
- Literature synthesis: 80 references, weighted toward 1990s–2001 patents and practice papers (US EPA, Metcalf & Eddy, Girovich's Biosolids Treatment and Management).
- Empirical data presented as ranges drawn from cited practice: solids content after each dewatering device, evaporation-rate envelopes of dryers, and cost bands (US$/t dry solids).
- Determination methods for bound water are described as laboratory procedures (dilatometric freeze at −20°C, vacuum filtration, expression, DSC) but the chapter reports them rather than performing them.

No new models are derived; the drying-curve description (constant → first falling → second falling rate) is borrowed from generic drying theory and mapped onto free/interstitial/surface water.

## 4. Argumentation / Logical Structure

- Define the problem (§38.1–38.2): sludge volume and disposal pressure; global production table (Table 38.1) frames it as an environmental/economic burden.
- Characterize the water (§38.3): four moisture classes; bound water = theoretical floor of mechanical dewatering. This is the physical premise of the whole economic argument.
- Dewater mechanically first (§38.4): because thermal drying is energy-intensive, push water out non-evaporatively; survey vacuum/belt/centrifuge/membrane presses with achievable solids.
- Then dry thermally if needed (§38.5): solar → fundamentals (sticky zone, rate periods) → direct/indirect/combined/hybrid dryers, each with capacity and emission characteristics.
- Cross-cutting issues (§38.5.6): pathogens, odor, explosion, economics, waste-heat reuse.
- Conclusion (§38.6): restates the dewater-then-dry logic and cost bands; calls for higher-efficiency, lower-emission, cheaper dryers.
The logic is sound and internally consistent: the §38.3 bound-water floor motivates the §38.4 dewatering-first rule, which constrains the §38.5 dryer selection.

## 5. Role in the Book

Ch38 sits in the "Drying of Specific Materials" / waste-and-biosolids cluster. It complements the mineral/coal/enzyme chapters in this batch by representing the wet, sticky, biologically active end of the solids-drying spectrum — the opposite extreme from free-flowing coal or crystalline enzymes. Its main book-level contribution is the dewatering-first energy hierarchy and the sticky-zone concept, both of which are reused implicitly in chapters on paste- and slurry-drying (e.g. spray and indirect dryers in Ch10/Ch12). It is a plant-engineering chapter; it does not advance drying theory.

## 6. Strengths / Weaknesses

Strengths
- Clear, defensible economic thesis: dewater (cheap) before you dry (expensive).
- Honest cost ranges and capacity envelopes grounded in cited practice.
- Excellent figure library (38.2–38.16) for equipment selection.
- Explicit treatment of the sticky zone as a design constraint — a genuinely transferable insight.

Weaknesses
- No quantitative energy comparison (kJ/kg-H₂O) between dewatering and evaporation — the central claim is asserted, not computed.
- Drying-rate-period ↔ water-class mapping (free→interstitial→surface) is presented as established but is conceptually loose (same water can span periods).
- Equipment coverage is patent-heavy and dated (2001 horizon); few modern (post-2010) low-temperature/heat-pump or electrified options.
- Explosion discussion (VP-8) is a single sentence — no dust-classification or inerting detail, unlike Ch43/Ch45.

## 8. Micro close-reading (paragraph-level)

¶ "Sludge drying is an energy intensive process…" (p.888). This single sentence is the hinge of the entire chapter. Note the precise wording: dewatering is defined negatively — "removing water without evaporation" — which is exactly why it is cheaper. The causal "Consequently" ties the energy fact to the process-sequence recommendation.

¶ Bond-strength ladder (p.888). The chapter reports that the measured bound-water content depends on method because the apparent bond strength differs: vacuum filtration ≈ 1 kJ/kg, drying test 20–40 kJ/kg, expression ≈ 70 kJ/kg ("almost the value of the heat of physisorption"). This is a subtle but important point: "bound water" is operational, not absolute — a lesson for anyone specifying a dewatering limit on a SAP/fiber slurry.

¶ Sticky-zone paragraph (p.891). The remedy described — "the dried sludge is frequently mixed with raw sludge to have a solid content of about 70% in order to avoid the sticky zone" — is a classic back-mixing / recycle strategy also seen in rotary and fluid-bed drying of sticky feeds. The number 70% is the upper sticky-zone bound; operating just above it avoids paste formation.

¶ Explosion sentence (p.899). One sentence, no elaboration. For a hygiene converting line this is the only safety-relevant bridge, and it is under-developed here — the reader must go to Ch43/Ch45 for real inerting/explosion-protection detail.

¶ Cost paragraphs (p.899–900). The numbers are internally consistent between §38.5.6 and the Conclusion: dewatering US$34–64/t dry solids; drying US$65–80/t dry solids; capex ~US$1.8 M per t/h evaporation. The chapter's honest message: drying costs more per ton than dewatering, reinforcing VP-2.

## 9. Concept deep-dive — The dewatering-before-evaporation hierarchy

The chapter's most transferable idea is an energy hierarchy of water removal:

- Gravity / thickening — zero added energy.
- Mechanical dewatering (vacuum, belt, centrifuge, press) — removes water as liquid; cost US$34–64/t dry solids; raises solids only to ~15–35% (belt) or 25–35% (centrifuge).
- Thermal drying / evaporation — removes water as vapor; cost US$65–80/t dry solids plus ~US$1.8 M/t/h capex; needed only when final solids must reach 55–95%.
The physics: evaporation carries the latent heat of vaporization (~2260 kJ/kg at 100°C) for every kilogram of water, whereas mechanical dewatering expends only the work to overcome capillary/bond strength (order 1–70 kJ/kg per the bond-strength ladder). That is 1–2 orders of magnitude of energy difference — the quantitative backing the chapter asserts but does not compute. For the hygiene engineer this maps directly onto: dewater the SAP/fluff slurry or the wet-laid web mechanically (vacuum, press, through-air pre-dewatering) before the thermal through-air dryer, exactly as the chapter prescribes for sludge.

Sticky-zone corollary: any hydrophilic, fine-solids suspension that must be dried will transit a paste region. The design responses are (a) back-mix recycle to stay above 70% solids, (b) strong mechanical shear (cage mill, kneading disc, porcupine agitator), or (c) indirect heated-surface with self-cleaning scrapers. These are the same tools a formulator uses when drying a sticky hygiene ancillary (e.g. a superabsorbent masterbatch or a coated-fiber slurry).

## 10. Academic critique

- Claim without computation. The central "dewatering is cheaper than evaporation" claim would be far more persuasive with a side-by-side kJ/kg-H₂O or $/kg-H₂O figure. The bond-strength ladder (1–70 kJ/kg) and the latent-heat figure are both present in the literature the chapter cites — their synthesis is the missing step.
- Drying-period ↔ water-class mapping is loose. Assigning "first falling rate = interstitial, second = surface" (p.891) over-simplifies; in practice multiple water classes coexist and the period reflects internal mass-transfer resistance, not a single class.
- Stale equipment horizon. Patents stop at ~2001; no heat-pump, mechanical-vapor-recompression, or electrified low-temp dryers — now central to sludge and to energy-sensitive hygiene lines.
- Safety under-weighting. Explosion risk gets one sentence despite handling fine combustible powders at high T — a gap the book fills better in Ch43 (coal) and Ch45 (emissions/explosion).
None of these flaws undermine the chapter's utility as a process-sequence guide; they limit its value as a quantitative design reference.

## 11. Hygiene relevance & action cards

Overall relevance: ★☆☆☆☆ — substrate is municipal/industrial sludge, not absorbent hygiene materials. The durable transfer is process-economics logic and sticky-feed handling, not equipment specifics.

H38-1 — Apply the dewater-first hierarchy to any hygiene slurry/furnish. Before committing web or SAP-slurry to a thermal dryer, maximize mechanical dewatering (vacuum, press, through-air pre-dewater, screw press). The energy gap (≈1–70 kJ/kg mechanical vs ≈2260 kJ/kg latent) means every kg of liquid removed mechanically saves an order of magnitude of thermal energy. Relevance: ★☆☆☆☆ — economic principle only.

H38-2 — Expect and design around a sticky/plastic phase. Any hydrophilic fine-solids suspension (SAP masterbatch, Coated-fiber slurry, binder-laden furnish) will pass through a paste zone (~55–70% solids analog). Use back-mix recycle to stay above it, or strong shear (kneading/disc/porcupine agitator) — same remedies the chapter gives for sludge. Relevance: ★☆☆☆☆ — handling principle only.

H38-3 — Treat thermal drying as a sterilization/odour step only when needed. The chapter shows 70°C/2 h (or 90°C/10–15 min) inactivates pathogens (p.899). For hygiene, the parallel is heat treatment of recycled/fiber streams; note the energy cost before assuming thermal routes. Relevance: ★☆☆☆☆.

H38-4 — Prefer indirect/closed-loop when vapour capture matters. Indirect dryers "produce minimal amounts of vapors and are therefore easy to manage" (p.892). On a converting line where VOC/odour capture is regulated (see Ch45), the same logic favours closed-loop/indirect drying to shrink the gas to treat. Relevance: ★☆☆☆☆ — feeds Ch45.

H38-5 — Reuse waste heat; quantify the 60% saving. Flue-gas/vent-gas heat reuse "can save as much as 60% on the cost" (p.899) and mechanical vapour compression "can improve the energy efficiency considerably" (p.899). Build waste-heat recovery into any new hygiene drying line rather than treating dryer exhaust as pure liability. Relevance: ★☆☆☆☆.
