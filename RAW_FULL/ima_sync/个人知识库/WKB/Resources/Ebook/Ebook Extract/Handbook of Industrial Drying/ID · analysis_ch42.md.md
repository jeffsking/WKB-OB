# Chapter 42 — Drying of Enzymes

## 1. Chapter Overview (100–200 words)

Page numbers derived from PDF-page − 21; offset anchored on Ch.1 = p.1 and should be spot-checked against the print edition.

Chapter 42 (Pilosof & Sánchez) treats enzyme drying as a damage-minimisation problem: enzymes are heat-labile protein catalysts that "are deactivated due to heat-induced structural changes," yet solid (powder/granule) form is required for storage stability and dust-safety. Spray drying is the industrial workhorse; the chapter covers drying-chamber layouts (cocurrent preferred to minimise heat deactivation), the activity-loss kinetics (loss rises with outlet temperature and as moisture falls below 15–20%), and the central stabilisation science — sugars, polyols, and salts that form a glassy amorphous matrix (Tg 60–200 °C) in which the protein is immobilised and hydrogen-bonded as a water substitute. The second half covers powdered-detergent enzyme granulation (fast-mixer, prilling, extrusion, spray-coating of cores) aimed at eliminating active enzyme dust. Relevance to hygiene is modest but real for enzyme/active-containing wipe and cleaning formulations.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (with verbatim quotes + print pages)

### Viewpoint 1 — Enzymes deactivate on heating; drying must preserve activity

- Page: p.979
- Quote: "In general, enzymes have a disadvantage in that they are deactivated due to heat-induced structural changes or, in the case of proteolytic enzymes, due to decomposition by themselves."
- Interpretation: The core conflict — drying is needed for stability but the step itself damages the protein. Defines the whole chapter's optimisation target: keep activity retention "close to 100%."
### Viewpoint 2 — Cocurrent spray drying minimises heat deactivation

- Page: p.980
- Quote: "When the spray-drying operation is cocurrent … there is less danger of overheating as the evaporation rates are high … Thus, cocurrent drying chambers are preferred to minimize heat deactivation of enzymes during the process."
- Interpretation: A direct design rule for any heat-sensitive active (enzyme, probiotic, active ingredient) dried by spray — match the chapter's Ch45 emitter-layout logic with cocurrent to protect the payload.
### Viewpoint 3 — Activity loss rises with outlet temperature and below 15–20% moisture

- Page: p.981
- Quote: "Enzyme activity decreases with increasing outlet air temperatures. … Significant enzyme inactivation occurs when moisture decreases below 15 to 20%."
- Interpretation: Two operational limits: cap outlet air temperature, and do not over-dry. Table 42.4 quantifies this (e.g., fungal α-amylase at 78–80 °C outlet loses 26–38% activity vs 4–10% at 63–66 °C).
### Viewpoint 4 — Sugars/salts form a glassy matrix that immobilises and substitutes for water

- Page: p.983 / p.984
- Quote: "proteins are mechanically immobilized in the glassy, solid matrix during dehydration. The restriction of translational and relaxation processes is thought to prevent protein unfolding … sugars can continue to protect the dried protein by hydrogen bonding to the protein … serving as water substitutes when the hydration shell of the enzyme is removed."
- Interpretation: The stabilisation mechanism is vitrification — a glassy carbohydrate matrix locks the protein and replaces its hydration shell. This is the protein/biopolymer analogue of the glass-transition state.
### Viewpoint 5 — A glass-forming carrier needs Tg 60–200 °C and chemical inertness

- Page: p.984
- Quote: "a carrier substance, which must be hydrophilic … that must exist in a glassy amorphous state with a glass-transition temperature (Tg) above 20 °C (a desirable range is therefore between 60 and 200 °C) and should be sufficiently chemically inert."
- Interpretation: Quantifies the stabiliser spec. Trehalose/maltose-type disaccharides are preferred glass-formers (p.985). Directly informs formulation of an enzyme-containing wipe or sachet.
### Viewpoint 6 — Enzyme granules must be coated to eliminate active dust

- Page: p.986
- Quote: "the enzyme particles have to be coated in such a way that no active enzyme dust is present or released during handling. … the coating has to stabilize the particles against abrasive forces and protect the granule against chemical agents such as water, hydrogen peroxide, and peracetic acid (the bleach that migrates in detergent formulations)."
- Interpretation: Dust is both a worker/end-user allergy hazard and an activity-stability problem; coating must also dissolve readily in use liquor. Relevant to enclosed enzyme-wipe dosing and handling hygiene.

## 4. Argumentation Process

- Framing — enzymes are labile proteins; solids needed for stability but drying damages (p.979).
- Spray-drying basics — cocurrent, three phases, inert gas for oxidation-sensitive (p.980).
- Activity-loss kinetics — outlet T and low-moisture limits; Table 42.4; two-stage spray+fluid-bed to avoid (p.981–982).
- Glass-state stabilisation — sugars/polyols/salts, Tg 60–200 °C, vitrification mechanism (p.983–985, Tables 42.5–42.8).
- Detergent granulation — dust elimination via four granulation routes; coating against H₂O₂/peracetic acid (p.986–989).
- Conclusion (implicit): stability = process + formulation + encapsulation.
## 5. Role in the Book

Ch42 is the book's only biomolecule-drying chapter and the direct counterpart to Ch45 (emission control — here dust is a biological hazard) and Ch44 (feeding/atomisation of the enzyme slurry). It is the Handbook's bridge from bulk/industrial drying to heat-labile active ingredients — the same regime an enzyme-wipe or active-coated-substrate formulator operates in.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths: Unusually mechanistic for a drying chapter — it explains why stabilisers work (glassy matrix, hydrogen-bond water substitution, Tg window) rather than just listing them. Tables 42.4–42.8 give quantitative residual-activity and storage data. Explicit dust/health hazard framing (p.986) is responsible and rare.
⚠️ Limitations/contestable: Heavily patent-derived (Table 42.3, 42.7–42.8 cite patents); some data are proprietary-sourced and not independently reproducible. "Inactivation occurs also in formulations kept well below their glass-transition temperatures [9]" (p.983) flags that the Tg theory alone is incomplete. No kinetics model (no Arrhenius/First-order deactivation fitted). 2006-vintage; modern protein-engineering stabilisers under-covered.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Enzyme = protein catalyst: p.979, "Enzymes are protein catalysts of high molecular weight."
- Heat deactivation: p.979, "deactivated due to heat-induced structural changes."
- Cocurrent preference: p.980, cocurrent "preferred to minimize heat deactivation of enzymes."
- Inert gas for oxidation-sensitive: p.980, "it is preferable to use inert gas, such as nitrogen, during spraying and drying."
- Activity-loss limits: p.981, loss "increases with increasing outlet air temperatures"; "Significant enzyme inactivation occurs when moisture decreases below 15 to 20%."
- Two-stage spray+fluid-bed: p.981, loss "can be reduced and even almost completely avoided when a controlled combination of spray drying and fluidized-bed drying is used."
- Hydration-shell removal: p.983, final air-drying stress "is the removal of the enzyme's hydration shell … can result in irreversible inactivation upon rehydration."
- Glassy matrix / water substitute: p.983, "proteins are mechanically immobilized in the glassy, solid matrix"; sugars "hydrogen bonding to the protein … serving as water substitutes."
- Carrier Tg spec: p.984, glassy amorphous state "with a glass-transition temperature (Tg) above 20 °C (desirable 60 and 200 °C)" and "sufficiently chemically inert."
- Lactose dose-response: p.984 (Table 42.5), 0% → 83% residual; 0.5% → 90%; 1% → 96% after drying.
- Salt (MgSO₄) recovery >100%: p.984, "a recovery of more than 100% active enzyme after drying and after long-term storage"; 5–90% salt added.
- Coating against bleach: p.986, coating protects against "water, hydrogen peroxide, and peracetic acid."
## 8. Micro close-reading (ONE key paragraph)

p.983 (Solute-induced protection mechanism):
> "sugars can continue to protect the dried protein by hydrogen bonding to the protein at some critical point during dehydration … serving as water substitutes when the hydration shell of the enzyme is removed. … The proteins are mechanically immobilized in the glassy, solid matrix during dehydration. The restriction of translational and relaxation processes is thought to prevent protein unfolding, and spatial separation between the protein molecules … is proposed to prevent aggregation."

Sentence-by-sentence: (1) Mechanism — sugars H-bond to the protein where water once did, i.e., they are molecular water-substitutes. (2) State — the protein is locked in a glassy solid matrix. (3) Unfolding suppression — immobilisation restricts the molecular motion that drives denaturation. (4) Aggregation suppression — spatial dilution in the matrix keeps protein molecules apart. The whole paragraph is a vitrification theory of biopreservation.
💡 Standout line: "serving as water substitutes when the hydration shell of the enzyme is removed" — the single sentence explaining why a sugar glass preserves an enzyme through drying.

## 9. Concept deep-dive

Vitrification as the stabilisation principle. The chapter's deepest claim is that a carbohydrate glass (Tg 60–200 °C, p.984) immobilises the enzyme and replaces its hydration shell via hydrogen bonding (p.983). The quantitative lever is the dose: Table 42.5 shows 0.5% lactose already lifts residual activity of Bacillus alkaline protease from 83% to 90% after drying and to 95% after 1 h at 90 °C; 1% reaches 96%. Magnesium sulfate can give ">100% active recovery" (p.984) — i.e., the salt both improves drying yield and long-term storage. For an enzyme-wipe formulator, this is the formulation playbook: embed the active in a glassy polyol/sugar/salt matrix rather than leaving it as free dried protein.

Activity-loss operating window. Two hard limits from p.981: cap outlet air temperature (Table 42.4: every enzyme loses more as outlet T rises) and never dry below 15–20% moisture in the first stage — finish in a low-temperature fluid bed (40–50 °C, p.982) to reach final MC without the hydration-shell-removal shock (p.983). The two-stage spray→fluid-bed layout (p.981–982) is the engineering realisation of this window.

Encapsulation for dust/chemical safety. Section 42.3 reframes the problem as dust hazard: 1960s 100%-dust powders replaced by coated granules; coating must block active-dust release, resist abrasion, survive "hydrogen peroxide, and peracetic acid (the bleach that migrates in detergent formulations)," yet "dissolve readily in the washing liquor, even at low temperatures" (p.986). For hygiene, this is the template for an enclosed, low-dust, chemically-protected enzyme or active granule in a wipe.

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- Patent-derived data (Tables 42.3, 42.7–42.8) are not independently reproducible; stabiliser "more than 100% recovery" is suspicious and unexplained mechanistically.
- The glass-state theory is incomplete: "inactivation occurs also in formulations kept well below their glass-transition temperatures [9]" (p.983) shows Tg alone does not predict survival.
- No fitted deactivation kinetics (no k(T) Arrhenius, no moisture-dependence model), so a formulator cannot predict shelf-life from first principles.
- Open question 1: Can the residual-activity data be collapsed onto a single Tg–moisture–outlet-T master curve to predict enzyme survival for a new wipe formulation?
- Open question 2: Does the peracetic-acid/bleach protection mechanism for coated granules transfer to the oxidant species present in disinfecting wipes?
