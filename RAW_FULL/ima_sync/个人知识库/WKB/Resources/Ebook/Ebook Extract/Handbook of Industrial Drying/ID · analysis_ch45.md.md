# Chapter 45 — Dryer Emission Control Systems

## 1. Chapter Overview (100–200 words)

Page numbers derived from PDF-page − 21; offset anchored on Ch.1 = p.1 and should be spot-checked against the print edition.

Chapter 45 (Jumah & Mujumdar) treats the drying plant as an open-loop source of dust, gases, and odors and argues that emission control is no longer optional: "More stringent demands governing environmental aspects of the plant operations have resulted in better compliance." The authors organize the field into three control targets — particulate matter (PM), gaseous emissions, and odor — and then catalogue the three most-widely-used devices (cyclones, fabric filters/baghouses, wet scrubbers) with their operating principles, advantages/disadvantages, selection, design equations, and performance data. The second half surveys emission control in selected industries (minerals, chemicals, pharmaceuticals, food/agriculture), showing how the generic devices are combined into compliance trains. The central commercial message — that "the cost of the equipment needed to comply with regulations can exceed that of the dryer itself" — and the VOC-recovery logic are the parts most transferable to hygiene converting lines.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (with verbatim quotes + print pages)

### Viewpoint 1 — Dryers are a primary emission source and control is now mandatory

- Page: p.1039
- Quote: "Dryers are one of the major sources of atmospheric emission of dust, gases, and odors."
- Interpretation: Sets the compliance frame. On a hygiene converting line (coating oven, airlaid former, fluff defibration), the dryer is exactly where lotion/adhesive VOCs, perfume, and fibre fines leave the process. The chapter treats this as inherent, not exceptional.
### Viewpoint 2 — Organic-liquid removal demands solvent recovery economics, not just destruction

- Page: p.1040
- Quote: "When an organic liquid is to be removed from a material, the emissions may include vapors, mists, odors, and smoke. In this case, recovery of the solvent is frequently desirable in order to lower costs, prevent a safety hazard, and eliminate air pollution."
- Interpretation: This is the core hygiene sentence — coating-oven exhaust (lotion solvent, adhesive carrier, printing ink) is explicitly a recoverable resource, not merely a pollutant. A buyer should ask whether a supplier destroys or recovers solvent.
### Viewpoint 3 — Condensers are the first stage of any VOC/odor train

- Page: p.1042
- Quote: "moisture-laden or organic vapor emissions should first be passed through a condenser that is capable of removing a high percentage of the water vapor or condensable volatile organic compounds (VOCs). This minimizes the odorous gas stream to be treated or allows recovery of a costly solvent."
- Interpretation: The prescribed sequence is condenser → (adsorber / scrubber / oxidiser). For hygiene lines this is the architectural template for an odour/VOC capture system serving a lotion or hot-melt coating dryer.
### Viewpoint 4 — Control-capital can exceed dryer-capital

- Page: p.1041
- Quote: "In some drying installations, the cost of the equipment needed to comply with regulations can exceed that of the dryer itself."
- Interpretation: A supplier's emission-control spend signals whether the line was designed clean from the start. It frames the procurement question of whether the abatement cost is passed through or amortised in quality/risk reduction.
### Viewpoint 5 — Baghouses give the highest particulate capture but carry fire/explosion duty

- Page: p.1049 / p.1052
- Quote: "Capable of meeting particulate emission standards in most countries." … "Baghouses must be safeguarded against dust explosions because considerable dust holdup in the baghouse can occur. Explosion panels that relieve directly … are normal features of baghouses."
- Interpretation: For airlaid and fluff-defibration exhaust (fine cellulosic/SAP dust), baghouses (>99% efficient, Table 45.2) are the right tool but require explosion panels, grounding, and spark protection — exactly the hygiene-line combustible-dust concern.
### Viewpoint 6 — Pharmaceutical/VOC control menu applies directly to coated-web odour

- Page: p.1065
- Quote: "of greatest concern are VOCs … Many of these compounds are photochemically reactive … An added concern is that some of these compounds are toxic … Typical control measures … are condensation, absorption … adsorption (using activated carbon beds), thermal destruction (flares, boilers and process heaters, thermal incinerators, and catalytic incinerators), and vapor containment. Water- or brine-cooled condensers are the most common control devices, with carbon adsorbers in occasional use."
- Interpretation: This is the reuseable control "menu" for a hygiene coating/airlaid line emitting VOCs or odorous plumes — condenser first, then carbon or thermal/catalytic oxidation.

## 4. Argumentation Process

- Framing — dryers emit dust/gas/odor; stricter rules force control (p.1039–1040).
- Classification — control equipment grouped into PM, gaseous, odor; selection driven by environmental + engineering + economic factors (p.1040–1041).
- Condenser-first principle for organic/odor streams (p.1042).
- Device deep-dives — cyclones (p.1043–1045), baghouses (p.1046–1053), wet scrubbers (p.1053–1055): each with principle, adv/disadv, selection, design eqs, performance.
- Industrial application — minerals (p.1056–1059), chemical (p.1060–1064: phosphate, potash, soap/detergent, soda ash), pharmaceutical (p.1065–1066), food/agriculture (p.1067).
- Conclusion (implicit) — compliance trains are process-specific; best-demonstrated technology varies by dryer type and pollutant.
## 5. Role in the Book

Ch45 is the Handbook's plant-engineering / environmental-compliance capstone. It complements the unit-operation chapters (spray, fluid-bed, flash, rotary) by treating the exhaust side every dryer needs. Its Table 45.2–45.3 and the industry survey directly extend the air-quality obligations a buyer must impose on a coating-oven, airlaid, or defibration supplier. It pairs naturally with Ch44 (feeding, which sets the dust/loading the collector must handle) and with the spray-drying chapters referenced for detergent/pharma layouts (Table 45.20, Figures 45.7–45.10).

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths: Unusually practical — gives design equations (cyclone saltation velocity, baghouse gas-to-cloth ratio and pressure-drop, scrubber penetration), standard selection tables, and real AP-42 emission factors. The explicit "condenser-first" rule and the pharmaceutical VOC control menu are directly reusable. Table 45.18 (soap/detergent) and Table 45.20 (spray-dryer layouts) quantify achievable particulate levels.
⚠️ Limitations/contestable: A 2006-vintage review; EPA factors are US-centric and "emission standards are different in different countries" (p.1056). Equations carry OCR degradation (cyclone saltation velocity, Dennis–Klemm constants scrambled, p.1043/1051). No hygiene-substrate treatment — the link to lotion/adhesive/coating dryers is the reader's inference, not the authors'. No odour-threshold or VOC-species data.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- PM / gaseous / odor classification: p.1040, equipment grouped as "(a) equipment for controlling particulate matter (PM); (b) equipment for controlling gaseous emissions; and (c) equipment for controlling odor emissions."
- Condensable VOC: p.1042, condenser "capable of removing a high percentage of the water vapor or condensable volatile organic compounds (VOCs)."
- Cyclone cut size & efficiency: p.1044, Table 45.2 cyclones collect 50–90% (10–100 µm), bagfilters >99% (>1 µm), wet scrubbers 80–99%.
- Gas-to-cloth ratio: p.1050, "apparent velocity of the gas approaching the cloth"; usual drying design factor "4 (ft³/min)/ft² of cloth area (1.2 (m³/min)/m² of cloth area)."
- Baghouse collection efficiency: p.1052, "Collection efficiency 99 to 99.9%"; operating pressure drop "500 to 2500 Pa"; bag life "1 to 4 y."
- Wet-scrubber energy classes: p.1054, low (1–5 in H₂O, 250–1250 Pa) → high/venturi (15–35 in H₂O, 3750–8750 Pa, particles to 0.5 µm, 99.5%).
- Closed-cycle drying: p.1065, recommended "if (1) Flammable, toxic, or valuable solvents are used … (2) No atmospheric pollution … is permitted … (3) Powder–air explosion/fire risks exist."
- Spray-dryer layouts (Table 45.20): p.1066, open-cycle cyclone >250 mg/Nm³ → bagfilter + absolute filter 0.1–5; recycled systems "Negligible."
## 8. Micro close-reading (ONE key paragraph)

p.1042 (General considerations — condenser-first principle):
> "It is worth noting that moisture-laden or organic vapor emissions should first be passed through a condenser that is capable of removing a high percentage of the water vapor or condensable volatile organic compounds (VOCs). This minimizes the odorous gas stream to be treated or allows recovery of a costly solvent."

Sentence-by-sentence: (1) Directive — organic/odorous streams should be condensed first; names the captured species as "water vapor or condensable VOC". (2) Dual payoff — condensation either shrinks the burden on the downstream odor treatment, or recovers a "costly solvent" for reuse. The logic is architectural: a condenser is the cheap front-end that makes the expensive oxidiser/adsorber behind it smaller and the solvent behind it recoverable.
💡 Standout line: "allows recovery of a costly solvent" — the sentence that reframes coating-oven exhaust as recoverable asset rather than pure waste.

## 9. Concept deep-dive

The emission-control device ladder and where hygiene lines sit. Table 45.2 (p.1044) anchors selection: cyclones (cheap, dry, but only 50–90% on 10–100 µm, useless below 5–10 µm) → fabric filters (>99%, down to  µm, but fire/explosion and dew-point limited) → wet scrubbers (simultaneous gas+particle, no fire risk, but wastewater + corrosion + high energy at high efficiency). For airlaid and fluff-defibration exhaust (fine cellulosic + SAP dust, potentially combustible), the text's own logic (p.1049, p.1052) points to baghouses with explosion panels and carbon/steel grounding, or — where humidity is high — wet scrubbers. For coating-oven VOC/odour, the condenser-first rule (p.1042) plus the pharma VOC menu (p.1065: condensation → adsorption → thermal/catalytic destruction) is the prescription.

Cyclone pressure drop & the saltation limit. ΔP = ρ_g u_i² N_H / 2 (where N_H = number of inlet velocity heads, p.1044). The corollary is a cutoff: "Cyclone efficiency increases with inlet velocity up to a certain value at which saltation occurs and particles bounce along the cyclone wall and cannot be separated effectively" (p.1043). The saltation-velocity correlation is OCR-degraded:
u_s = 2400 ρ_g^{0.2} (b/D)^{1/2} / [ g (ρ_p − ρ_g)/ρ_g ]^{1/2}  [equation garbled in source — fractional exponents and symbols scrambled in the original; transcription approximate].
For a buyer, the practical point: cyclones must be sized for the actual dust loading and gas density or they either re-entrain (lose fine SAP/fibre) or over-pressurise the fan.

Baghouse pressure-drop model (Dennis–Klemm). The operating filter with dust cake:
ΔP = S_E + k_2 c_i V² t  [p.1051]
where S_E = effective residual fabric drag (N·s/m³), k_2 = specific cake-resistance coefficient (N·s/g·m), c_i = inlet dust concentration (g/m³), V = velocity (m/s), t = filtration time (s). The outlet concentration correlation is OCR-scrambled but reads as a form c_o = [P_ns + (0.1 − P_ns) e^{−a w}] c_i + C_R with P_ns ≈ 1.5×10⁻⁷ exp[12.7(1 − e^{1.03V})]. The lesson: pressure drop is dominated by cake build-up, so cleaning method (shake / reverse-air / pulse-jet, Table 45.9) and gas-to-cloth ratio (design 1.2 m³/min/m², p.1050) govern both capture and fan power.

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- The chapter is a 2006 review; AP-42 factors and EPA NSPS thresholds are US-specific and must be re-anchored to the buyer's jurisdiction (p.1056: "emission standards are different in different countries").
- Device models are generic and dust-specific constants (S_E, k_2, P_ns) must be experimentally fitted (p.1051); the provided defaults are for woven glass/flyash.
- OCR degradation in the equation blocks (p.1043, p.1051) means the formulas cannot be used verbatim without the original handbooks.
- No odour-threshold data, no VOC-species inventory, no link to hygiene chemistries.
- Open question 1: Can a hygiene coating-oven exhaust be characterised (VOC species, condensation curve, dew point) well enough to size a condenser + carbon/oxidiser train from Table 45.3 efficiencies alone?
- Open question 2: For airlaid/fluff fines, is a pulse-jet baghouse or a venturi scrubber the lower total-cost collector once wastewater and explosion-protection costs are included?

## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Overall relevance: ★★★★★ (highest in this batch — direct compliance/odour/particulate transfer to converting lines).

Action Card H45-1 — Demand the condenser-first VOC train on any coating/lotion/airlaid line. Insight: p.1042 — organic/odorous exhaust "should first be passed through a condenser … allows recovery of a costly solvent." Action: In supplier audits, require the abatement flow diagram; reject any design that feeds VOC-laden air straight to an oxidiser without a condenser/recovery stage. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Directly governs odour, VOC compliance and solvent-cost recovery on lotion/adhesive/printing dryers.

Action Card H45-2 — Specify particulate control for airlaid/fluff-defibration exhaust as baghouse + explosion protection. Insight: p.1044 Table 45.2 (baghouse >99% on  µm); p.1049/p.1052 baghouses need explosion panels, grounding, spark protection for combustible dust. Action: Require supplier to certify baghouse efficiency ≥99%, explosion-relief panels, carbon/steel-wire grounded cloth, and spark arrestor upstream. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Cellulosic + SAP fines are combustible; capture efficiency and explosion safety are non-negotiable.

Action Card H45-3 — Use Table 45.20 to set an exhaust-PM acceptance limit. Insight: p.1066 — open-cycle cyclone layout >250 mg/Nm³; bagfilter + absolute filter 0.1–5; recycled systems "Negligible." Action: Set a contractual particulate-emission limit (e.g., ≤20 mg/Nm³ for a bag-filtered line) referencing the achievable layout classes. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Converts invisible exhaust dust into a measurable, enforceable spec.

Action Card H45-4 — Interrogate odour control chemistry on soap/detergent-style spray lines. Insight: p.1063 — "The main atmospheric pollution problem in soap manufacturing is odor" controlled by "scrubbing with an acid solution"; surfactant organics "vaporized in the spray dryer … condense in the tower exhaust air stream into droplets," paraffin alcohols/amides give "a highly visible plume." Action: For any surfactant-containing or fragranced web, ask what scrubber chemistry (acid/water/activated-carbon) suppresses odour and plume. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Fragrance and surfactant carry-over is the dominant odour source on hygiene lines.

Action Card H45-5 — Ask recovery vs destruction on solvent streams. Insight: p.1040 — "recovery of the solvent is frequently desirable … to lower costs, prevent a safety hazard, and eliminate air pollution." Action: Require the supplier to state whether coating/printing solvents are recovered (closed-loop/condenser + carbon) or destroyed (thermal/catalytic incineration), and the associated OPEX. → 卫材相关性★★★★☆｜Recovery vs destruction changes both compliance risk and unit cost you pay.

Action Card H45-6 — Require dew-point/condensation discipline on baghouse exhaust. Insight: p.1052 — baghouses operating "below the dew point … with high water vapor content" cause condensation, especially outdoors in winter; p.1059 asphalt/cement note condensation on fabric "poses a serious fire hazard." Action: Verify the collector is insulated/heated and runs above dew point; request the exhaust dew-point vs operating-temperature margin. → 卫材相关性★★★★☆｜Prevents plugging, corrosion and fire on humid fluff/airlaid exhaust.

Action Card H45-7 — Evaluate closed-cycle drying for solvent/odour-critical webs. Insight: p.1065 — closed-cycle recommended for flammable/toxic/valuable solvents, no atmospheric release permitted, or powder–air explosion risk. Action: Where lotion/solvent or fine powder dominates, ask whether a closed-cycle or indirect-fired layout (Figures 45.7–45.9) was considered. → 卫材相关性★★★★☆｜Eliminates both odour escape and powder-explosion pathways.
