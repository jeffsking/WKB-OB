# Ch37 — Drying in Mineral Processing

## Academic-Depth Chapter Analysis · Handbook of Industrial Drying (4th ed., Mujumdar, CRC Press)

Page-number caveat: Print page = PDF page − 21; offset anchored on Ch.1 = p.1 and should be spot-checked against the print edition. (Source header in this file confirms the anchor: "PDF pages 898-905 | print pages 877-884 | offset 21".) Where a verbatim marker was missing or ambiguous, the convention [未找到原句/页码缺失] is used and no page is invented.

## 1. Overview

Chapter 37 is a survey of dryer types used in the mineral and metallurgical industry, written by the handbook's editor (A.S. Mujumdar). Its scope is deliberately economic and selection-oriented: it enumerates conventional dryers (hearth, grate, shaft/tower, rotary) and the less-common types (spray, vacuum, fluid-bed, flash, conveyor, screw, drum, rotating-shelf), then summarizes their thermal-performance envelopes and the rules of thumb for choosing among them. The through-line is bulk-solids economics — drying is treated as a low-value, high-tonnage, energy-hungry unit operation at the handling/beneficiation stage, not as a product-quality step.

Reader-fit for the hygiene-materials / fluff-pulp strategist: the substrate (ore concentrates, coal, kaolin, titanium dioxide) is unrelated to absorbent hygiene products, so direct process transfer is minimal. The durable, substrate-independent lessons are: (1) the dewatering-before-thermal-drying rule re-appears here independently (p.878) — reinforcing Ch38's economic thesis; (2) the dusting / fire / explosion hazards of drying fine solids (p.878) connect to Ch43 (coal) and Ch45 (emissions/explosion); (3) sticky/caking feed handling (p.880) mirrors Ch38's sticky zone; (4) fuel choice and heat-recovery trade-offs (coal vs gas, superheated steam) are generic plant-economics. Relevance rating: ★☆☆☆☆ (one star) — bulk-solids plant economics only, low substrate relevance.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (verbatim, with print pages)

VP-1 — Drying in minerals gets too little technical/economic attention despite being energy-intensive.
> "Thermal removal of water from solids or slurries is an important operation carried out in numerous mineral-processing and metallurgical-processing applications. Although drying is a highly energy-intensive operation that is also increasingly difficult at lower moisture contents, no special attention is generally given to the technical and economical aspects of the drying process employed in the mineral- or metallurgical-processing industry." (p.877, §37.1)

VP-2 — Establish necessity, then maximize mechanical dewatering before thermal drying. (echoes Ch38's core thesis)
> "As a general rule, one must establish the necessity of drying before undertaking further action. If it is deemed necessary, it is essential to maximize mechanical dewatering (by filtration, centrifugation, pressing, and other processes) to reduce the thermal load in drying." (p.878, §37.1)

VP-3 — Two generic hazards of drying fine solids: dusting below 4% and fire/explosion.
> "It may be noted that below a typical solid moisture content of 4%, dusting may pose serious handling and environmental problems." (p.878, §37.1)
> "Fire and explosion hazards as well as hazards associated with the handling of toxic materials are commonly encountered in the drying of solids in the mineral and metallurgical industry." (p.878, §37.1)

VP-4 — Conventional dryers are thermally inefficient; performance envelopes are quantified.
> "Evaporative capacity: 5 to 25 kg water evaporated/h/m² (hearth area); Fuel consumption: 1500 to 4500 kcal/kg water evaporated; Thermal efficiency: 10 to 30%" (hearth type). (p.878, §37.2.1)
> "Its evaporative capacity is at least three times that of hearth-type dryers, i.e., 25 to 75 kg water evaporated/h/m² of grate area. The thermal efficiency is also much superior from 30 to 60%." (grate type). (p.878, §37.2.2)

VP-5 — Rotary dryers dominate; performance and selection rules.
> "they range from 1.5 to 4 m in diameter and are 6- to 30-m long. Evaporative capacities are of the order of 30 to 120 kg/h/m³ (dryer volume); thermal efficiencies range from 35 to 70%." (p.879, §37.3.1)
> "For directly heated counterflow or parallel-flow rotary dryers, the typical heat requirement for evaporation ranges from 1200 to 2200 Btu/lb." (p.879, §37.3.1)
> "For product moistures below 1%, a counterflow dryer is recommended if the product is not heat-sensitive. For thermolabile materials with product moisture in excess of 1%, a parallel-flow dryer is desirable as the feed is in contact with hot gases while drying the surface moisture." (p.879–880, §37.3.1)

VP-6 — Sticky/caking solids need more residence time or special internals.
> "For sticky or caking solids, which do not shower well, it is often necessary to allow more residence time in the dryer." (p.880, §37.3.1)
> "New designs include special internals within the shell to break up the formation of large agglomerates in the drum so that a rotary dryer could be used to dry sticky or pastelike feeds." (p.880, §37.3.1)

VP-7 — Spray drying of mineral slurries: bone-dry in one step, slurry must be 55–70% solids and pumpable.
> "By means of spray drying, flotation concentrates (pulp, sludge, and precipitate) can be dried to a bone-dry state (less than 0.5% moisture) in a single continuous operation." (p.881, §37.3.2)
> "The solids content in the slurry is typically between 55 and 70%. A pumpable slurry is needed to accomplish atomization." (p.881, §37.3.2)

VP-8 — Pyrophoric materials become inert only when bone-dry.
> "Pyrophoric materials, which tend to undergo spontaneous combustion at moisture levels of 3 to 5%, can be dried to a bone-dry level at which they are inert." (p.881, §37.3.2)

VP-9 — Vacuum drying: leak draws air in (safe), no dust, low capital.
> "Compared with the conventional open-hearth dryer, vacuum drying offers the advantages of low capital costs, low installation and maintenance costs, and environmentally safe operation with no dust problems." (p.882, §37.3.3)
> "Because the dryer operates under vacuum, a leak results in air being drawn into rather than out of the system." (p.882, §37.3.3)

VP-10 — Fluid-bed is energy-efficient; superheated steam eliminates air-film resistance and enables heat recycling.
> "Fluid-bed dryers are energy-efficient, consuming as little as 1500 Btu/lb of water evaporated." (p.883, §37.3.4)
> "With superheated steam, the mass-transfer resistance offered by the air film through which the evaporated moisture must diffuse is eliminated. The exhaust is also steam, which can be condensed to recover heat or compressed or reheated for total or nearly total recycling." (p.884, §37.3.10)

VP-· — Economics rule selection; coal firing and dewatering-first re-emphasized.
> "Better thermal economy is obtained with fluid-bed, spray, and flash dryers, but the rotary is currently more economical for very-high-tonnage operation of products with low unit value." (p.883–884, §37.3.10)
> "Care should be exercised in selecting the most cost-effective drying system (e.g., nonthermal dewatering using filtration or centrifugation preceding thermal drying)." (p.884, §37.3.10)
> "Several competing dryer systems can generally accomplish the desired performance, albeit with different capital and operating costs. Energy costs should be considered, and energy-conservation measures should be employed when possible." (p.884, Conclusion)

## 3. Research Method

The chapter is a practice-oriented review by the handbook editor, not a primary study. Method:
- Equipment taxonomy + performance tables drawn from field experience (Kramm's field-installation table for indirect rotary dryers is cited but not reproduced in full here).
- Rules of thumb and operating envelopes (diameters, residence times, temperatures, Btu/lb, kcal/kg) presented as industry norms rather than derived models.
- Literature pointer synthesis: detailed dryer designs are explicitly deferred to other handbook chapters; only three references close the chapter (Kramm 1980, IEC bulletin, Kudra & Mujumdar 2001).
- Qualitative selection logic: counterflow vs parallel-flow by heat-sensitivity; sticky feeds need internals; pyrophoric needs bone-dry.

No experiments, no new correlations, no energy computations are performed in-chapter.

## 4. Argumentation / Logical Structure

- Frame (§37.1): drying is essential but under-engineered in minerals; list why industry dries (freight, handling, downstream efficiency, cold-climate freezing, flash-smelting necessity).
- Ground rule (§37.1): prove necessity → maximize mechanical dewatering → beware dusting (%) and fire/explosion.
- Conventional dryers (§37.2): hearth / grate / shaft, with quantified performance; hearth dismissed as inefficient.
- Rotary in depth (§37.3.1): the dominant mineral dryer — sizing, counter/parallel flow, sticky-feed handling.
- Other dryers summarized (§37.3.2–37.3.9): spray, vacuum, fluid-bed, flash, conveyor, screw, drum, rotating-shelf — each with niche and limits.
- Future trends (§37.3.10): coal firing, superheated steam, heat pumps; reiterate dewatering-first and cost-effective selection.
- Conclusion (§37.4): competing systems differ in cost; conserve energy.
The argument is consistent and selection-logic-driven; it does not advance drying science but is a competent procurement/selection guide.

## 5. Role in the Book

Ch37 is the bulk-solids / low-unit-value anchor of the "Drying of Specific Materials" cluster. It bookends Ch43 (coal — a mineral subset with explosion focus) and complements Ch38 (sludge — wet/sticky/bioactive extreme). Its unique book-level contribution is the quantified performance envelope of conventional dryers and the editor's explicit selection philosophy ("several competing systems… different capital and operating costs"), which frames the whole materials section. It is plant-economics, not theory.

## 6. Strengths / Weaknesses

Strengths
- Editor-level, vendor-neutral selection guidance; honest about rotary's dominance for low-value high-tonnage.
- Quantified envelopes (Btu/lb, kcal/kg, thermal efficiency %) give the reader real comparators.
- Cross-links the dewatering-first rule (also Ch38) and the dust/fire/explosion hazards (also Ch43/Ch45) without redundancy.
- Good coverage of sticky-feed and pyrophoric special cases.

Weaknesses
- Dated (2001 horizon): no heat-pump reality, no modern electrified/low-temp options; coal-firing trend is now environmentally contested.
- Performance numbers are given without derivation or uncertainty; a reader cannot tell whether 10–30% hearth efficiency is typical or worst-case.
- Superficial on emission control (only a passing nod to electrostatic precipitators/scrubbers) — Ch45 carries that load.
- "Thermal efficiency" is used loosely (10–30% hearth vs 35–70% rotary) without defining the basis (e.g. vs LHV), so cross-comparison is approximate.

## 7. Key Terms (glossary)

|||
|---|---|
|Mechanical dewatering|Filtration/centrifugation/pressing to cut thermal load before drying (p.878).|
|Dusting|Handling/environmental problem when solids fall below ~4% moisture.|
|Hearth dryer|Solids on directly heated floor; conduction; 10–30% efficiency; now unpopular.|
|Grate dryer|Wet granules on reciprocating grate with through-air; 30–60% efficiency; used for "green balls."|
|Shaft / tower dryer|Granular solids showered through vertical/horizontal drying medium; 35–60% efficiency.|
|Rotary dryer|Inclined rotating drum, cocurrent or countercurrent; 35–70% efficiency; dominant in minerals.|
|Counterflow vs parallel-flow|Counterflow for 1% moisture.|
|Bone-dry|% moisture; required for flash smelting and to inert pyrophoric materials.|
|Pyrophoric|Spontaneously combusts at 3–5% moisture; inert only when bone-dry.|
|Spray drying|Atomized slurry (55–70% solids, pumpable) dried in one step; special abrasion-resistant wheel.|
|Fluid-bed dryer|Upward hot gas; 1500 Btu/lb; needs fluidizable, nonsticky feed; feed ≤3/4 in.|
|Flash/pneumatic dryer|Hot-air transport of pulverized solids; removes only surface moisture in seconds.|
|Superheated steam drying|Eliminates air-film mass-transfer resistance; exhaust steam condensable/recyclable.|

## 8. Micro close-reading (paragraph-level)

¶ "As a general rule, one must establish the necessity of drying…" (p.878). This is the editor's signature pragmatic stance: don't dry by default. The phrase "maximize mechanical dewatering… to reduce the thermal load" is the same economic hierarchy Ch38 builds its whole thesis on — appearing here independently confirms it is a handbook-wide principle, not chapter-specific.

¶ Dusting threshold (p.878). "Below a typical solid moisture content of 4%, dusting may pose serious handling and environmental problems." This is a powerful, quotable design constraint: over-drying fine solids creates a dust/explosion/environmental liability. For any powder-handling line (including SAP or fluff-pulp fines) it implies an optimal minimum moisture, not "as dry as possible."

¶ Counterflow vs parallel-flow rule (p.879–880). The logic is physically precise: counterflow exposes dry product to hottest gas (maximizes final dryness, needs heat-stable product); parallel-flow exposes wet feed to hot gas but the solid temperature never exceeds wet-bulb (safe for heat-labile). This is a clean, reusable selection heuristic.

¶ Sticky-feed internals (p.880). "special internals within the shell to break up the formation of large agglomerates" — the same agglomeration-breaking idea as Ch38's cage-mill/shear remedies for the sticky zone, here implemented as rotary-drum lifters/breakers.

¶ Spray-drying slurry spec (p.881). "solids content in the slurry is typically between 55 and 70%… A pumpable slurry is needed to accomplish atomization." Note the 55–70% band coincides exactly with Ch38's sticky-zone band (55–70%) — a slurry in that range is both atomizable and at the paste threshold, explaining why mineral spray dryers need abrasion-resistant wheels (p.881).

¶ Superheated-steam paragraph (p.884). The claim that eliminating the air film removes the external mass-transfer resistance, and that exhaust steam is condensable/recyclable, is the same argument Ch43 and Ch45 make for inert/closed-cycle drying — here stated at the minerals level.

## 9. Concept deep-dive — Bulk-solids drying economics and the selection matrix

The chapter's real intellectual content is a selection matrix for low-unit-value, high-tonnage solids:

|||
|---|---|
|Energy cost|Maximize mechanical dewatering first; pick high-efficiency type (fluid-bed/spray/flash) when tonnage allows.|
|Product value|Rotary wins for "very-high-tonnage operation of products with low unit value" (p.884).|
|Heat sensitivity|Parallel-flow if thermolabile & >1% target; counterflow if % & stable.|
|Feed stickiness|Extra residence time or shell internals; cannot use grate/flash for sticky/pasty.|
|Dust/explosion|Beware % moisture dusting; vacuum or closed systems reduce dust (p.882).|
|Fuel availability|Coal firing increasingly used; superheated steam enables heat recycling.|

Energy hierarchy (cross-chapter): Ch37's "maximize mechanical dewatering… to reduce the thermal load" (p.878) + "nonthermal dewatering using filtration or centrifugation preceding thermal drying" (p.884) is the same dewatering-first principle Ch38 derives from sludge. Both chapters thus independently validate the rule that the hygiene engineer should apply to any slurry/furnish: dewater mechanically, dry thermally only as needed.

Dusting vs dryness tension: the % dusting threshold (p.878) is a crucial counterweight to "dry as much as possible." In powder handling (SAP, fluff fines, mineral-like ancillaries), the optimum final moisture is a band, not a minimum — too wet = handling/clumping, too dry = dust/explosion. This is the hygienic-plant equivalent of Ch43's self-heating/explosion concern.

## 10. Academic critique

- No derivation of any number. Thermal efficiencies, Btu/lb, kcal/kg are asserted as norms; the reader cannot reproduce or challenge them. A one-line definition of the efficiency basis (vs fuel LHV) would fix most comparability issues.
- Dated fuel narrative. "dryers will increasingly use coal when possible" (p.883) reflects 2001 energy prices; today's decarbonization pressure inverts this for many sites. The chapter should be read as a historical procurement baseline, not a forward recommendation.
- Emission control deferred. Given that fire/explosion and toxic-material hazards are named (p.878), the thin treatment of gas cleaning (one phrase on ESP/scrubber at p.882) is a gap Ch45 must fill.
- "Several competing systems… different costs" (p.884) is true but unactionable without a quantitative cost model; the chapter stops at philosophy.
None of this invalidates Ch37 as a selection primer; it limits its value as a design or sustainability reference.

## 11. Hygiene relevance & action cards

Overall relevance: ★☆☆☆☆ — bulk mineral solids; no direct substrate link to absorbent hygiene products. Transfer is limited to plant-economics and powder-handling principles.

H37-1 — Adopt the dewatering-first rule for any hygiene slurry/furnish. "maximize mechanical dewatering… to reduce the thermal load in drying" (p.878) and "nonthermal dewatering using filtration or centrifugation preceding thermal drying" (p.884) apply directly to SAP-slurry or wet-laid furnish: dewater mechanically before the through-air dryer. Relevance: ★☆☆☆☆ — economic principle (shared with Ch38).

H37-2 — Respect a dusting floor, not just a dryness target. "below a typical solid moisture content of 4%, dusting may pose serious handling and environmental problems" (p.878). For SAP/fluff fines handling, set a minimum allowable moisture to suppress dust/explosion, paralleling Ch43. Relevance: ★☆☆☆☆.

H37-3 — Use counter/parallel-flow logic for heat-sensitive hygiene streams. "For thermolabile materials with product moisture in excess of 1%, a parallel-flow dryer is desirable" (p.880) — when drying heat-labile binders/coatings, choose concurrent hot gas so solids stay near wet-bulb. Relevance: ★☆☆☆☆ — selection heuristic.

H37-4 — Expect sticky-feed handling for paste-like hygiene ancillaries. "special internals within the shell to break up the formation of large agglomerates" (p.880) — when drying sticky Coated-fiber/SAP masterbatch feeds, specify agglomeration-breaking internals or back-mix (cf. Ch38 sticky zone). Relevance: ★☆☆☆☆.

H37-5 — Evaluate superheated-steam / closed-loop for heat recovery. "With superheated steam… the exhaust is also steam, which can be condensed to recover heat or… recycled" (p.884) — for energy-sensitive hygiene lines, consider steam drying with condensate heat recovery (links to Ch43/Ch45 closed-cycle). Relevance: ★☆☆☆☆.
