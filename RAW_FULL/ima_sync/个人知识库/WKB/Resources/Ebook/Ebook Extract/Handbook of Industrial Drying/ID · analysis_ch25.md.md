# Chapter 25 — Drying of Fruits and Vegetables

## 1. Chapter Overview

Page numbers derived from PDF-page − 21; offset anchored on Ch.1 = p.1 and should be spot-checked against the print edition.

Chapter 25 (Jayaraman & Das Gupta) surveys the dehydration of fruits and vegetables as a postharvest preservation strategy, moving from commodity context (§25.1–25.2) through pretreatments (§25.3: alkaline dip, sulfiting, blanching) to an encyclopedic inventory of drying hardware (§25.4: solar, hot-air, fluidized-bed, explosion-puffing, foam, microwave, spray, drum, freeze, osmotic, heat-pump, ultrasonic) and closing on the chemistry of quality loss during drying and storage (§25.5). The arc is practical: perishable, >80%-water plant tissue must be stabilized, and every drying choice trades throughput against volatile/heat-labile retention, texture collapse, and microbial/oxidative shelf life. The chapter's analytical spine is the water-activity (a_w) framework and the glass-transition (Tg) concept, which the authors use to rationalize browning, lipid oxidation, pigment loss, shrinkage, and microbiological stability. For a hygiene-materials reader the value is methodological: the same a_w / sorption-isotherm / Tg / monolayer toolkit that governs dried-food stability applies, with substrate substitution, to residual-moisture specification and warehouse moisture-regain of fluff pulp and SAP.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (6, each with page + verbatim quote + interpretation)

### Viewpoint 1 — Hot-air drying causes irreversible textural collapse

- Page: p.625
- Quote: > "The problem of hot air drying, which is still the most economical and widely used method for dehydrating piece-form vegetables and fruits, is the irreversible damage to the texture, leading to shrinkage, slow cooking, and incomplete rehydration."
- Interpretation: The dominant, cheapest technology destroys cell-wall architecture (loss of membrane differential permeability, loss of turgor, protein denaturation, starch crystallinity, macromolecular H-bonding). This is the canonical shrinkage / case-hardening mechanism and the central quality-vs-rate tension of the whole chapter.
### Viewpoint 2 — Pectin is the structural determinant of rehydration capacity

- Page: p.626
- Quote: > "Levi et al. [84] observed that pectin, one of the major cell wall and intercellular tissue components, played a significant role in the rehydration capacity of dehydrated fruits."
- Interpretation: Collapsed capillaries and disrupted cell walls (80% intact cells with pretreatment vs 0% without, Jayaraman et al. [81]) make rehydration a network-integrity problem, not merely a diffusion problem. Pectin = the polysaccharide load-bearing matrix — directly analogous to a crosslinked hydrogel network.
### Viewpoint 3 — Water activity is the master stability variable

- Page: p.626
- Quote: > "It is defined as the ratio of the vapor pressure of water P in the food to the vapor pressure of pure water P0 at the same temperature (a_w = P/P_0)."
- Interpretation: a_w subordinates temperature as the key parameter for deteriorative reactions. The chapter states microorganisms cannot grow when a_w ≤ 0.6–0.7, and couples a_w to browning, lipid oxidation and NEB. This is a substrate-agnostic physical law usable for microbial-specification of any dried hygiene feedstock.
### Viewpoint 4 — Monolayer / BET optimum defines the safe storage moisture

- Page: p.629
- Quote: > "It has been suggested that the optimal amount of water for long-term storage corresponds in most dehydrated foods to the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) monolayer value."
- Interpretation: Not "drier is always better." There is a single optimal residual moisture (the BET monolayer). Below/above it, stability degrades — a precise prescription for setting fluff-pulp and SAP target moisture rather than minimizing blindly.
### Viewpoint 5 — Glass transition governs collapse, stickiness and caking

- Page: p.627
- Quote: > "Glass transition is a second-order phase transition that occurs over the temperature range at which amorphous solid materials are transformed into viscous, liquid state [89]."
- Interpretation: Amorphous low-moisture solids (food powders, spray-dried agglomerates, rubbery stored samples) transition to a viscous liquid above Tg, causing collapse, stickiness, caking. The authors tie shrinkage during drying/storage to the Tg–rubbery transition. This is identical physics to polymer Tg.
### Viewpoint 6 — Rate-quality trade-off is explicit and quantified

- Page: p.620
- Quote: > "In general, rapid drying retained a greater amount of ascorbic acid than slow drying."
- Interpretation: Faster drying improves heat-labile retention but (Viewpoint 1) worsens shrinkage; slower drying reverses both. The trade-off is the recurring engineering decision and maps onto drying of heat-sensitive hygral layers and coated webs.

## 4. Argumentation Process

The authors build from a need (postharvest loss of >80%-water perishables, p.603–605) → enumerate stabilization options → focus on dehydration → survey hardware (each technique graded by quality/energy/throughput) → then explain quality losses through a unified physical-chemical lens. The rhetorical pivot is §25.5, where a_w and Tg replace ad-hoc "quality" talk: browning, oxidation, pigment loss, shrinkage, and microbial growth are all re-expressed as functions of a_w and Tg. Conclusion (§25.5.9): storage stability is governed by moisture, temperature, oxygen, and light, with the BET monolayer as the optimum. The logic is "mechanism-first," which is exactly why it transfers.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Water activity (a_w): (p.626) "the ratio of the vapor pressure of water P in the food to the vapor pressure of pure water P0 at the same temperature (a_w = P/P_0)."
- Sorption isotherm: (p.626) "The relationship between equilibrium moisture content and water activity, known as the sorption isotherm, is an important characteristic that influences many aspects of dehydration and storage."
- Monolayer / BET value: (p.629) optimal storage water "corresponds in most dehydrated foods to the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) monolayer value."
- Glass transition (Tg): (p.627) "amorphous solid materials are transformed into viscous, liquid state."
- Nonenzymatic browning (NEB / Maillard): (p.623) "a group of diverse reactions between amino groups and active carbonyl groups, leading eventually to the formation of insoluble, brown, polymeric pigments, collectively known as melanoidin pigments."
- Blanching: (p.608) pretreatment that inactivates enzymes; reduces microbial count >99.9% (p.628).
## 8. Micro close-reading (ONE key paragraph)

Target (p.626, water-activity definition and microbial threshold):
> "During the last three decades water activity, a_w, has played a major role in many aspects of food preservation and processing. It is defined as the ratio of the vapor pressure of water P in the food to the vapor pressure of pure water P0 at the same temperature (a_w = P/P_0). Next to temperature, it is now considered as probably the most important parameter having a strong effect on deteriorative reactions. … It is now well known that microorganisms cannot grow in the dehydrated food system when the water activity range is less than or equal to 0.6–0.7, but other reactions, enzymic and nonenzymic (e.g., lipid oxidation, NEB, etc.) that cause change in color, flavor, and stability continue during processing and storage."

Sentence-by-sentence breakdown:
1. Function: situates a_w as the dominant modern preservation variable (rhetorical framing).
2. Technique: gives the operational definition via vapor-pressure ratio — a measurable, substrate-independent quantity.
3. Keywords: "most important parameter" — elevates a_w above temperature for deterioration control.
4. Logic relation: contrasts microbial thresholds (hard cut-off at a_w ≤ 0.6–0.7) with chemical reactions (NEB, lipid oxidation) that proceed even below it — i.e., killing microbes ≠ stabilizing chemistry.
💡 Standout line: > "microorganisms cannot grow … when the water activity range is less than or equal to 0.6–0.7, but other reactions … continue during processing and storage" — the single sentence that should drive any dried-feedstock microbial spec.

## 9. Concept deep-dive

Core concept A — Water activity (a_w) as stability metric
- Definition: p.626 quote above (a_w = P/P_0).
- Operational definition? Yes — measurable by vapor-pressure or dew-point; the chapter uses it to set SSP (safe-storage-point) drying endpoints (p.626: "reliable guide to … the drying end point required for an SSP").
- Usage in this chapter: microbial limit (≤0.6–0.7, p.626), NEB peak at a_w 0.4–0.6 (p.627), lipid oxidation minimum at 0.3–0.5 then rising (p.627), chlorophyll/pigment stability windows (carotenoid max at 0.43 a_w, p.627), shelf-life models Table 25.11/25.12.
- Academic origin: food-science water-relations school (Labuza/Troller/Roos cited).
- Relation: sits upstream of the monolayer and Tg concepts.

Core concept B — Glass transition temperature (Tg)
- Definition: p.627 quote above.
- Operational definition? Yes — critical Tg values used to avoid collapse/caking/stickiness in powders and low-moisture foods (p.627: "can be reduced by not exceeding their critical values based on Tg determination").
- Usage: freeze-drying collapse, spray-drying stickiness, powder caking during storage (p.627); shrinkage during drying linked to Tg–rubbery transition (del Valte et al. [91], p.627).
- Origin: polymer/physical-chemistry (Roos, Karel, Kokini [89,90]).
- Relation: complements a_w — both are state variables for amorphous solids.

Core concept C — Monolayer (BET) optimum
- Definition: p.629 quote above.
- Operational: BET monolayer = target residual moisture for max storage stability; some items (spinach, cabbage, orange juice) most stable at zero moisture, others (potato, corn) at monomolecular value (p.629) — i.e., optimum is material-specific.

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- Substrate specificity gap: the a_w/stability windows are calibrated on plant foods; the framework transfers but every numerical threshold must be re-derived for cellulose/SAP substrates. Open question: what is the BET-monolayer a_w of a given fluff-pulp grade or SAP, and does a microbial-growth cut-off near 0.6–0.7 hold for those materials? [inference]
- Tg–shrinkage causality unresolved: del Valte et al. [91] showed sugar infusion reduced shrinkage (20–65% volume protection) yet data "did not indicate that structural collapse could be reduced by diminishing the difference between drying temperature and glass transition temperature" (p.627) — the lever (solute plasticization vs T–Tg gap) remains open.
- Sulfite dependency: chapter concedes "there is no practical substitute for SO2" for NEB control (p.623) — a food-additive answer with no analogue in non-food drying.
## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Overall rating: ★★★★☆ (methodological transfer strong; numeric constants non-portable).

Substrate-difference caveat (per assignment): fruits/vegetables are living, enzyme-active, polysaccharide/cell-wall matrices; fluff pulp (cellulose fibre) and SAP (crosslinked polyacrylate gel) are industrial polymers. The physics of a_w, sorption isotherm, monolayer, and Tg transfers; the values and biochemical pathways (NEB, enzymatic browning, carotenoid oxidation) do not. Do not invent "diaper applications" for carrots.

ACTION CARD 1 — Residual-moisture microbial spec (from Viewpoint 3/§25.5.6 & §25.5.8)
- Situation: specifying incoming fluff-pulp or SAP moisture for warehouse storage.
- How to apply: adopt the a_w ≤ 0.6 microbial-stability principle; measure pulp/SAP EMC–a_w isotherm (method from Ch24) and set received-moisture so equilibrium a_w stays below the microbial-growth cut-off; keep oxygen/light controlled per §25.5.9.
- Expected effect: prevents mold/yeast growth in baled pulp and caked SAP without over-drying energy waste. (p.626, p.628)

ACTION CARD 2 — BET-monolayer target instead of "driest possible" (from Viewpoint 4)
- Situation: drying or conditioning absorbent-core furnish.
- How to apply: determine the substrate's BET monolayer (Ch24 GAB/EMC method) and target that moisture, not minimum; note some polymers may be most stable near-zero like spinach/cabbage (p.629).
- Expected effect: avoids brittle/case-hardened web and re-moistening shock; maximizes storage stability. (p.629)

ACTION CARD 3 — Avoid Tg-collapse in dried hygral coatings (from Viewpoint 5)
- Situation: spray-/drum-drying or thermally treating hydrocolloid or SAP-containing layers.
- How to apply: keep process/storage temperature below the material's Tg (amorphous state) to prevent stickiness, caking, collapse (p.627); plasticizing solutes (sugars in food; analogous humectants/additives in hygiene) lower Tg and must be accounted for.
- Expected effect: stable free-flowing powder / intact web structure. (p.627)
