# Chapter 26 — Drying of Herbal Medicines and Tea

## 1. Chapter Overview (范围、对象与定位)

Chapter 26 (Guohua Chen and Arun S. Mujumdar) is a substrate-oriented survey of how thermal drying degrades or preserves the bioactive value of botanical materials — culinary/medicinal herbs, tea leaves (green/oolong/black), ginseng roots, and cut herbal-medicine pieces. Its governing argument is that for these materials the quality of the dried product (retained active ingredients, colour, aroma) — not merely moisture removal — is the design objective, and that quality is set jointly by temperature, drying method, and moisture-transport path. The chapter opens with the economic scale of herbal medicine (US$12 billion in 1994, p.633), then treats small-quantity household drying, the multi-stage tea processes (withering → killing-out/fermentation → staged hot-air drying), a catalogue of tea dryers (multitray, louver, conveyor-belt, fluidized/vibro-fluidized bed), ginseng shed-drying, and finally a quantitative dataset (Tables 26.2–26.5) on convective vs infrared drying of 27 Chinese herbs. The recurring theme — a quality-vs-rate trade-off governed by a material-specific "allowed temperature" — is the transferable payload for a hygiene reader; the substrates themselves (tea, ginseng, glycyrrhiza) are not diaper materials.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (核心观点 + 原文逐句引证)

V2.1 — Quality is set by active-ingredient retention, and improper heating destroys it.
> "The quality of the herbs depends very much on the contents of active ingredients. It is known that heating or thermal drying, if not carried out properly, can cause a significant loss of the active ingredients." (p.634)
Interpretation: The chapter's thesis in one sentence — the objective is not dryness but preservation of a heat-labile functional payload. This is the transferable frame: a drying process is judged by what survives, not only by residual moisture.

V2.2 — There is a two-sided temperature window: too hot destroys actives, too cold accelerates enzymatic/microbial decomposition.
> "The selection of proper drying temperature is vital. Too high a temperature may cause loss of active ingredients. On the other hand, too low a temperature may actually accelerate decomposition by promoting enzyme activity within the plant itself or even microbial attack, particularly for sugar-containing substances." (p.634)
Interpretation: A non-monotonic quality-vs-temperature relation — a genuine mechanistic insight. Unlike inert engineering webs, biologically active substrates have a lower temperature bound too, because slow drying prolongs the window for enzymatic self-degradation.

V2.3 — Each tea type demands a different process/dryer because the target biochemistry differs.
> "The majority of the teas can be classified as green tea, oolong tea, and black tea. Each requires a different process and hence a different drying system is required. The quality of dried tea depends to a great extent on the drying system and operating conditions used." (p.635)
Interpretation: Killing-out (green) arrests enzymatic oxidation early; black tea deliberately ferments before high-temperature firing to "seal the flavors." The same leaf yields opposite products purely through the heat/timing schedule — a strong statement that drying is a compositional control lever, not just dewatering.

V2.4 — Staged (ramped) temperature protocols balance high rate against low product temperature.
> "In order to maintain a high drying rate and a low temperature of herb pieces, it is suggested to use staged drying with variation of temperatures of heated air." (p.641)
Interpretation: The explicit method takeaway — multi-step temperature programming (e.g. Radix Glycyrrhizae 40/50/70/(30→77→60)°C) resolves the rate-quality conflict better than a single set-point, because most heat exposure occurs while evaporative cooling still protects the solid.

V2.5 — Method choice, not just temperature, governs quality: IR can be worse than hot air for some actives.
> "When infrared drying is used, the loss of active ingredients is even higher for some herbs as shown in Table 26.4." (p.641–642)
Interpretation: Radiative heating raises internal/surface temperature differently from convection; the mode of heat supply and moisture removal independently affect degradation. The data are not one-directional — some herbs (Polyporus, Rhizoma Ligustici) fare better under IR — so the interaction is substrate-specific.

V2.6 — Bed depth controls internal temperature gradients and drying time.
> "When the bed depth is increased from 25 to 50 mm, the drying time is 2.7 times longer and within the material, the maximum temperature differences increased from 10 to 18°C at a drying temperature of 66°C." (p.641)
Interpretation: A concrete geometry-vs-uniformity result: doubling bed depth nearly triples drying time and widens the internal ΔT, i.e. the core lags and over-heats relative to the surface. An "optimal depth ~30 mm" emerges — directly analogous to fixed-bed uniformity problems in any packed hygroscopic solid.

## 4. Argumentation Process (论证过程)

- Premise A: Botanical value resides in heat-labile active ingredients whose retention defines quality (p.634).
- Premise B: Temperature has a two-sided effect — excess destroys actives, deficit invites enzymatic/microbial decay (p.634).
- Evidence 1 (qualitative): Tea process narratives show that timing/temperature of killing-out vs fermentation determines whether the same leaf becomes green, oolong, or black tea (p.635–637).
- Evidence 2 (quantitative): Tables 26.2–26.5 quantify ingredient loss (0–100 %) vs actual temperature and method; some losses reach 100 % (Herba Menthae menthol, Herba Caricis phenylethyl alcohol) even at "allowed" temperatures (p.640–643).
- Synthesis: Because a single set-point cannot simultaneously maximise rate and minimise degradation, the authors advocate staged temperature programming and method selection matched to the specific active (p.641).
- Conclusion: "temperature control is important in retaining the active ingredients... Uniform drying of the small herb pieces can give better quality" (p.643).
Rhetorically the chapter leans on authority + tabulated exemplars rather than a derived model; it repeatedly warns that "currently listed values of allowed temperature are simply too high" (p.641), positioning its data as a corrective to received practice.

## 5. Role in the Book (在全书中的角色)

Ch.26 sits in the handbook's product-specific applications block (Part IV, food/biological substrates) alongside Ch.27 (roots/tubers) and Ch.28 (osmotic dehydration). It repeatedly defers dryer mechanics to the process chapters — "The reader is referred to Chapter 1 of this handbook for information on selection criteria for dryers" (p.634) — and to fluidized-bed / freeze / microwave chapters for equipment detail. Its distinctive contribution is the quality-degradation lens: where Ch.15–18 optimise heat/mass transfer, Ch.26 insists that transfer intensity must be capped by a biochemical constraint. For the hygiene reader it is a methodological analogue chapter, not an equipment chapter: the substrate difference (living plant tissue with assayable actives) limits direct transfer, but the quality-vs-rate discipline and the staged-temperature and bed-depth findings generalise.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses (优势与不足)

✅ Strengths
- States the quality objective explicitly and mechanistically (two-sided temperature window, p.634) rather than treating drying as pure dewatering.
- Supplies a rare quantitative degradation dataset — %-active-loss vs temperature and method, with named assays (HPLC/GC/UV) — enabling apples-to-apples method comparison (Tables 26.2–26.5).
- Demonstrates the bed-depth → internal-ΔT → time coupling with numbers (25→50 mm ⇒ 2.7× time, ΔT 10→18 °C, p.641).
- Candidly challenges received "allowed temperature" values as too high (p.641).

⚠️ Weaknesses / contestable points
- Heavily descriptive; no transport model, no drying-curve equations, no diffusivity data — mechanism is asserted, not derived.
- Data are single-sourced (mostly ref [23]) and OCR-garbled in the tables (multiple concatenated numbers), reducing traceability.
- No error bars, replication, or statistical treatment; "quality" is operationalised only as single-assay % loss.
- Dryer descriptions are generic and overlap other chapters; little that is new on equipment.
- Some claims are anecdotal/traditional ("pick herbs just after the dew has gone", p.634) with no evidentiary basis.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts (关键术语)

- Active ingredient / active substance: (p.634) the assayable functional compound (e.g. menthol, glycyrrhizic acid) whose retention defines dried-herb quality — "The quality of the herbs depends very much on the contents of active ingredients."
- Allowed temperature: (p.640, Table 26.2) the maximum air/material temperature a given herb tolerates before significant active loss; the chapter argues several listed values are "simply too high" (p.641).
- Withering: (p.635) controlled pre-drying moisture reduction of fresh leaf ("water content falls to about 50% (wet basis)") that conditions the leaf for rolling.
- Killing-out (kill-out): (p.635) high-temperature step (pan/steam/bake, wok 400–470 °C) "designed to arrest the enzymatic reaction, oxidation in particular" in green tea.
- Fermentation / curing: (p.636–637) deliberate enzymatic oxidation in oolong/black tea, later sealed by high-temperature firing.
- Staged drying: (p.641) temperature-programmed schedule to keep material temperature low while sustaining rate.
- Vibro-fluidized bed dryer: (p.638) grid vibrated at half-amplitude of several mm, 10–20 Hz, "claimed to produce less fines and result in gentler handling of the fragile leaves."
- Electrohydrodynamic (EHD) drying: (p.641) high-voltage DC corona accelerating surface air movement; "gives very high quality of dried material... as high quality as freeze dried."
## 8. Micro close-reading (微观精读)

Chosen paragraph — the two-sided temperature window (p.634). Full verbatim text:

"Tubers and roots take longer to dry than flowers and leaves even though the former are often cut up into pieces. The selection of proper drying temperature is vital. Too high a temperature may cause loss of active ingredients. On the other hand, too low a temperature may actually accelerate decomposition by promoting enzyme activity within the plant itself or even microbial attack, particularly for sugar-containing substances."

Sentence-by-sentence:

|||||||
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|1|"Tubers and roots take longer to dry than flowers and leaves even though the former are often cut up into pieces."|Establishes geometry/structure controls internal resistance|Comparative concession ("even though")|tubers, roots, cut up|Sets up that size reduction does not fully remove the internal-diffusion penalty|
|2|"The selection of proper drying temperature is vital."|Thesis statement|Assertion, emphatic ("vital")|proper drying temperature|Pivot from geometry to the controlling variable|
|3|"Too high a temperature may cause loss of active ingredients."|Upper bound of the window|Causal claim|too high, loss of active ingredients|First horn of the dilemma|
|4|"On the other hand, too low a temperature may actually accelerate decomposition by promoting enzyme activity within the plant itself or even microbial attack, particularly for sugar-containing substances."|Lower bound — the counter-intuitive half|Contrastive connective + mechanism naming ("enzyme activity", "microbial attack")|too low, accelerate decomposition, enzyme activity, microbial attack, sugar-containing|Second horn; converts a monotonic assumption into an optimisation problem|

The paragraph's technique is to destroy a naive monotonic heuristic ("cooler is always gentler") by naming a competing kinetic pathway that runs faster the longer the material stays wet and warm-but-not-hot. Note the hedging modality throughout ("may", "may actually") — the authors assert mechanism without quantifying either rate.

💡 Standout line: "too low a temperature may actually accelerate decomposition by promoting enzyme activity within the plant itself" (p.634) — degradation is a residence-time problem as much as a peak-temperature problem.

## 9. Concept deep-dive (概念深掘)

9.1 The "allowed temperature" as a quality-constraint construct.
- Definition (p.640, Table 26.2 header): the tabulated "Allowed air T, °C" against "Actual Maximum T, °C" for each herb — an administratively specified ceiling (e.g. 70–80 °C for Cortex Mori Radicis, 50–60 °C for Radix Glycyrrhizae, "Ambient" for Herba Menthae).
- Operational definition? Partly. It is operational in that it is a numeric air-temperature limit tied to a named assay outcome, but the chapter shows the construct is not validated: > "It is anticipated that for some herbs the currently listed values of allowed temperature are simply too high." (p.641) Herba Menthae is listed "Ambient" yet reached 68.5 °C with 100 % menthol loss (Table 26.5, p.643).
- Usage in this chapter: (i) as a benchmark for compliance — > "some herbs remain active even though drying was carried out a temperature exceeding the allowed values such as Polyporus umbellatus, Radix Trichosanthis, Radix Glycyrrhizae, Rhizoma Ligustici chuanxiong" (p.642); (ii) as a target the staged-drying protocol is designed to respect (p.641).
- Relation to other concepts: couples to maximum internal ΔT (a bed-depth consequence, p.641) — the material peak, not the air set-point, is what the ceiling must govern.
- Academic origin: Chinese pharmacopoeial/handbook practice (refs [5], [6], [23]); not derived from first-principles kinetics in this chapter.

9.2 Quality-vs-rate trade-off and its resolution by temperature programming.
- The trade-off is stated implicitly: high air temperature raises drying rate (Table 26.3: mean rate rises 5.6 → 8.6 g/kg·min as air goes 40 → 70 °C, while time falls 70 → 48 min) but raises material peak temperature (33.5 → 63 °C) and internal ΔT (7.8 → 28 °C).
- The staged case (30→77→60 °C) achieves an intermediate outcome: material max 49 °C, ΔT 7.6 °C, mean rate 6.9, time 55 min — i.e. near-70 °C productivity at near-40 °C thermal stress. This is the chapter's most quantitatively defensible method claim.
- Mechanism [inference]: during the early high-moisture period, evaporative cooling pins the solid temperature well below the air temperature, so aggressive heat can be applied safely; the ramp-down before the falling-rate period prevents the solid temperature from tracking the air once the latent-heat sink weakens. This is the same reasoning as Ch.15's evaporative-cooling argument, applied as a schedule rather than a set-point.

9.3 Mode-of-heat-supply effect (convective vs IR).
- > "Besides the material temperature, the way in which the heat is supplied and the moisture is taken away from the drying chamber definitely plays a role in the quality of the dried herbs." (p.642)
- Table 26.5 (p.643) shows the effect is bidirectional: IR worse for Fructus Aurantii (1.4 → 9.5 %), Citrus reticulata (0 → 40.0 %), Astragalus (0 → 17.6 %), Radix Angelicae (29.1 → 55.6 %); IR better for Radix Trichosanthis (66.7 → 27.8 %), Rhizoma Ligustici (22 → 3.8 %), Radix Salviae (33.7 → 11.9 %), Caulis Lonicerae (28.6 → 0 %), Rehmannia (11.4 → 0.08 %).
- Interpretation: IR deposits energy at/near the surface with high heating-plate temperatures (200–388 °C per Table 26.4), producing steeper surface-temperature excursions but shorter total exposure. Whether that helps depends on whether the active degrades by peak temperature (IR hurts) or by cumulative time-at-temperature (IR helps). The chapter does not articulate this dichotomy; it is my reading of the data pattern. [inference]
- Synergy claim: > "When infrared drying is combined with vibro-fluidized bed, Figure 26.10, the drying rate as well as the quality of the dried product is improved significantly." (p.642) — i.e. adding particle motion removes the surface-overheating penalty of IR.

## 10. Academic critique & open questions (学术批判与开放问题)

- No kinetic framework. The chapter documents % loss at a single endpoint but never fits degradation kinetics (Arrhenius order, activation energy) — so its results cannot be extrapolated to a new schedule. Given it advocates staged drying, an integrated thermal-history metric (e.g. ∫k(T)dt) is the obvious missing tool.
- Confounded comparisons. In Table 26.5 the hot-air and IR runs differ in both actual maximum temperature and heating history (e.g. Rheum Officiale 70.2 °C air vs 98 °C IR), so "method effect" and "temperature effect" cannot be separated. The claim that method matters "besides the material temperature" (p.642) is therefore under-identified.
- 100 % loss anomalies. Herba Caricis phacotae and Herba Menthae show 100 % loss under both methods (p.643). For volatile actives (phenylethyl alcohol, menthol) this is presumably evaporative stripping rather than thermal decomposition — a mechanistically distinct pathway the chapter does not distinguish.
- Contradiction on process variables. > "neither the air flowrate nor the initial moisture content affect the quality of dried herb pieces" (p.641) sits uneasily with the strong bed-depth effect (p.641) and with the volatile-stripping cases, where gas-phase convection should matter directly.
- Unquantified novel methods. EHD is claimed "as high quality as freeze dried" but > "The economics of this novel drying process are not clear as yet." (p.641)
- OCR/typographic integrity. Tables 26.2–26.5 contain concatenated and arrow-garbled cells (e.g. "20 75 84 68 47", "! ! ! !"); temperatures printed as "348C"/"400–4708C" are clearly 34 °C / 400–470 °C with a lost degree symbol. Numbers quoted here are limited to unambiguous cells; ambiguous cells were not used.
Open questions: (1) Can a single time-temperature-integral criterion replace per-herb "allowed temperature" tables, and would it survive the volatile vs thermolabile distinction? (2) Is the IR-better/IR-worse split predictable a priori from the active compound's volatility and thermal-decomposition onset?

## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards (卫材相关性与行动卡)

Overall relevance: ★★☆☆☆ (2/5) — method-transferable, substrate-irrelevant.

Where the substrate difference limits transfer (stated plainly): tea leaves, ginseng roots and cut herb pieces are living plant tissue whose value is a set of assayable secondary metabolites. Fluff pulp, SAP and nonwoven webs have no volatile actives, no enzymes and no microbial self-degradation pathway; their "quality" variables are fibre bonding/kink, brightness, SAP gel properties, and residual monomer — not menthol retention. Consequently none of Ch.26's temperature tables, tea process schedules, or dryer descriptions can be transferred numerically to hygiene converting. There is also no diaper, wipe or pulp application anywhere in this chapter, and I have invented none. What does transfer is the methodological architecture of a quality-limited drying process.

H11.1 — Adopt the two-sided temperature window as a QA frame for pulp/SAP drying (★★★). Situation: Setting dryer temperature for wet-laid fluff pulp sheet or post-polymerisation SAP drying. How to apply: Do not treat "lower temperature = safer." Ch.26's mechanism (p.634) — that low temperature prolongs the wet residence time and thereby accelerates a competing degradation pathway — maps onto hygiene analogues: prolonged wet residence promotes microbial growth/odour in pulp bales and slime in wet-end systems, and extends the time for residual-monomer and crosslinker side reactions in SAP. Define an upper bound (thermal damage: fibre hornification, SAP surface-crosslink over-cure) and a lower bound (wet-residence-driven degradation). Expected effect: A defensible two-sided process window instead of a one-sided ceiling.

H11.2 — Replace single set-points with staged temperature programming (★★★). Situation: A drying step where product damage caps throughput (fluff-pulp flash/airborne drying, SAP fluid-bed drying, wipe lotion cure). How to apply: Copy the Radix Glycyrrhizae result (p.641, Table 26.3): the staged 30→77→60 °C schedule delivered mean rate 6.9 g/kg·min and material max 49 °C, versus a 70 °C constant run at 8.6 g/kg·min but material max 63 °C and internal ΔT 28 °C. Front-load heat while the evaporative-cooling sink is strong; ramp down before the falling-rate period. Expected effect: Recovers ~80 % of the high-temperature rate benefit at roughly the thermal stress of a 40–50 °C run — quantitatively the same lever as Ch.15's evaporative-cooling window, but expressed as a schedule.

H11.3 — Treat bed depth as a first-order uniformity variable in any packed-bed hygiene drying (★★★). Situation: Drying SAP granules in a static/shallow fluid bed, or drying baled/roll goods where a packed depth exists. How to apply: Ch.26 measured that 25→50 mm bed depth gave 2.7× drying time and internal ΔT widening from 10 to 18 °C at 66 °C air, with an optimum near 30 mm (p.641). Run the same depth-sweep experiment on the actual granule/mat and pick depth from the ΔT curve, not from throughput alone. Expected effect: Prevents the classic core-wet/surface-over-dried defect (the casehardening analogue) and bounds the property spread within a batch.

H11.4 — Benchmark heating mode against the damage mechanism, not against rate (★★). Situation: Choosing between convective, IR, or IR+vibro-fluidised heating for a heat-sensitive hygiene component (lotion/emulsion coating cure, SAP surface crosslinking). How to apply: Ch.26 shows the mode effect is bidirectional and substrate-specific (Table 26.5, p.643) and that IR's surface-overheating penalty is largely removed by adding particle motion (p.642). Screen both modes experimentally on the actual assay of interest (e.g. residual monomer, CRC/AUL, actives in lotion) rather than assuming the faster mode is worse. Expected effect: Avoids a wrong a-priori mode exclusion; identifies the IR+agitation combination as the candidate for surface-sensitive particulates.
