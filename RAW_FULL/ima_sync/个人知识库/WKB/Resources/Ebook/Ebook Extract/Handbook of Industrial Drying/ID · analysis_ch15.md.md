# Chapter 15 — Impingement Drying

## 1. Chapter Overview (范围、对象与定位)

Chapter 15 (Arun S. Mujumdar) is the process-engineering core of the Yankee-hood and through-air technologies named in the assignment brief: it covers impinging hot-air jets for rapid convective drying of continuous sheets — explicitly tissue paper, photographic film, coated paper, nonwovens, and textiles (p.383). The chapter's logic: impingement is chosen only when the dominant moisture to remove is unbound/surface moisture and high external heat/mass-transfer rates are wanted; once drying becomes internal-diffusion-controlled, the high flux can degrade heat-sensitive product. The body delivers a design procedure (nozzle configuration → geometry → velocity/temperature/spacing → drying-rate calc → recycle), Martin's empirical Nu/Sh correlations for round and slot arrays, and a catalogue of extraneous effects (crossflow, semiconfinement, mass flux, large ΔT, suction, surface motion, oblique impingement, swirl). For the hygiene reader this is a ★★★★★ chapter: it is the handbook layer governing tissue Yankee hoods, through-air nonwoven bonding ovens, and lotion/coating cure on wipes, and it supplies the energy benchmark (3100 kJ/kg water, 75–90 % thermal efficiency) for the energy-cost/tonne question plus the web-defect mechanisms (case hardening, cracking, coating streaks, fire hazard).

## 2. Key Viewpoints (核心观点 + 原文逐句引证)

V2.1 — Impingement is the sheet-drying technology for tissue, nonwovens, coated substrates.
> "Impinging jets of various configurations are commonly used in numerous industrial drying operations involving rapid drying of materials in the form of continuous sheets (e.g., tissue paper, photographic film, coated paper, nonwovens, and textiles) or relatively large, thin sheets (e.g., veneer, lumber, and carpets)..." (p.383)
Interpretation: The chapter names the hygiene substrate classes verbatim — tissue, nonwovens, coated paper — confirming impingement as the relevant dryer family.

V2.2 — Impingement is for unbound moisture only; internal-diffusion control ⇒ degradation risk.
> "Impinging jet drying is recommended only if a major fraction of the moisture to be removed is unbound. If the drying rate is internal diffusion controlled, the high heat transfer rates of the impingement system can often result in product degradation if the product is heat-sensitive." (p.383–384)
Interpretation: This is the governing design rule. Hygiene webs (fluff, nonwoven, SAP) carry bound moisture in the falling-rate period; pushing impingement heat flux there risks fibre damage / curl / casehardening.

V2.3 — Very thin sheets (tissue) dry fast; heavier grades only lose surface moisture.
> "For rapid drying of very thin sheets (e.g., tissue), high-velocity impingement of hot air jets is very effective. On the other hand, for drying of heavier grades of paper and textiles, for example, impingement drying is effective only to remove surface moisture." (p.384)
Interpretation: Distinguishes tissue (fully impingement-dried) from heavier nonwoven/airlaid mats (impingement only for the surface — through-air or conveyor does the core).

V2.4 — Quality hazards: case hardening, cracking, coating streaks, fire.
> "Extremely high drying rates obtained by the use of high-velocity jets at elevated temperatures may cause such problems as case hardening or cracking due to rapid shrinkage. For combustible products, there is a significant fire hazard unless due care is taken in design and operation." (p.384)
> "in the printing, packaging, and converting industry, in which printing techniques are used to deposit a thin film of coating onto a moving substrate... very high shear stresses generated by jet impingement can cause the high-velocity ink or coating to flow, causing undesirable streaks (railroading) on the product." (p.384)
Interpretation: These four defects — casehardening, cracking, streaks, fire — are exactly the web-defect classes the brief asks about (curl, web defects); tissue is explicitly combustible.

V2.5 — Evaporative cooling holds surface below degradation/ignition.
> "Typically, the jet velocity and temperature may range from 10 to 100 m/s and 100 to 350°C, respectively, depending on the product. Because of the extremely high evaporative cooling in the constant rate period, the product surface temperature can be held well below its degradation or ignition temperature." (p.385)
Interpretation: The constant-rate evaporative-cooling mechanism is the safety lever — a 350 °C jet can keep a tissue surface far below ignition because the latent heat sink dominates.

V2.6 — Energy benchmark: 3100 kJ/kg, 75–90 % efficiency.
> "a well-designed dryer should yield a thermal efficiency of the order of 75–90%. Specific power consumption of 3100 kJ/kg of water evaporated has been reported for air-floater dryers." (p.388)
Interpretation: Concrete energy-cost/tonne anchors for the brief's energy question.

## 3. Research Method (研究方法)

The chapter is a design-guidance review + correlation compilation (Mujumdar's own group dominates the references). Method:
- Design procedure (§15.2): 6-step algorithm — choose nozzle config → geometry → velocity/temp/spacing (interrelated; Re > 2000 for fully turbulent) → compute constant-rate drying via empirical correlations with corrections → surface-temperature check → recycle-ratio via enthalpy balance.
- Correlation review (§15.3): Martin [9] Nu/Sh correlations for array-of-round-nozzles (ARN) and array-of-slot-nozzles (ASN), valid over stated Re / f / H/d ranges with ±15 % accuracy.
- Extraneous-effects survey (§15.3.2/§15.4.3): crossflow, semiconfinement, mass flux, large ΔT, suction, surface motion, oblique impingement, swirl — each quantified where data exist.
- CFD note (§15.4): k–ε and Reynolds-stress models "still not very reliable" but useful for trends.

No new empirical hygiene data; the value is the empirical transfer correlations and the design discipline.

## 4. Argumentation (论证逻辑)

- Premise A: Impingement gives very high h/m → popular for rapid drying / small equipment (p.383).
- Premise B: High h/m only helps when moisture is unbound; for diffusion-controlled drying it degrades heat-sensitive product (p.384).
- Step: Therefore select impingement only for surface moisture; design nozzle array + recycle to hit target rate at acceptable surface temperature; apply corrections for crossflow, mass flux, ΔT, confinement.
- Resolution: Use Martin correlations (constant-rate), keep Re > 2000, control jet T/velocity by product thermal sensitivity, exhaust properly to avoid crossflow uniformities and fire.
The argument is candid that most industrial designs are "arbitrary and trial-and-error" (p.386) and that CFD is not yet predictive — appropriate scope honesty.
## 5. Role of This Chapter in the Book (在全书中的角色)

Ch.15 is the high-intensity convective dryer chapter, complementing Ch.14 (gentle inert-bed), Ch.16 (pneumatic/flash — dilute-phase particulates), Ch.17 (conveyor — continuous web/belt), Ch.18 (infrared — radiative). It is the single most hygiene-central dryer chapter because tissue, nonwoven, and wipe-coating drying are explicitly its applications. It supplies the external heat/mass-transfer coefficients (h, hₘ) that Ch.4's property tables and Ch.17's conveyor ovens consume.

## 6. Strengths and Weaknesses (贡献与局限)

Strengths
- Names tissue, nonwovens, coated paper, textiles as primary applications (p.383) — directly on-target.
- Gives a usable 6-step design procedure and Martin's Nu/Sh correlations with validity ranges.
- Explicitly catalogs web defects (casehardening, cracking, streaks, fire) and their causes (p.384).
- Concrete energy anchors: 3100 kJ/kg water, 75–90 % thermal efficiency (p.388).
- Candid on crossflow (Nu can fall by factor 2), recycle necessity, and CFD limits.

Weaknesses
- Constant-rate focus: falling-rate must use "experimentally determined drying rate data or a validated mathematical model" (p.384) — no falling-rate model given.
- Correlations are for stationary, impermeable, plane surfaces; hygiene webs are moving, permeable, curved (Yankee).
- Large-ΔT regime (jet 100–700 °C) poorly correlated: measured h "somewhat lower than Martin predicts" above 100 °C (p.392).
- No hygiene-specific data (tissue basis weight, nonwoven permeability, lotion coating rheology); relevance is by explicit application class, not by example.

## 8. Micro close-reading (微观精读)

8.1 — The unbound-moisture rule (p.383–384). "Impinging jet drying is recommended only if a major fraction of the moisture to be removed is unbound. If the drying rate is internal diffusion controlled, the high heat transfer rates of the impingement system can often result in product degradation if the product is heat-sensitive." This sentence is the chapter's indispensable design gate. It tells the hygiene engineer: impingement is a surface dryer. For a fluff/SAP core whose moisture is bound, impingement over-drives the surface, crosses the degradation window, and casehardens the skin while the interior stays wet — exactly the defect class the brief cares about.

8.2 — Defect catalogue (p.384). "case hardening or cracking due to rapid shrinkage... significant fire hazard unless due care is taken... very high shear stresses... cause the high-velocity ink or coating to flow, causing undesirable streaks (railroading)." Four hygiene-relevant failure modes in one paragraph: (1) casehardening, (2) cracking, (3) fire (tissue is combustible), (4) coating streaks. For lotion-coated wipes the shear/streak mechanism is a direct coating-quality constraint on jet velocity and nozzle spacing.

8.3 — Evaporative-cooling safety lever (p.385). "Because of the extremely high evaporative cooling in the constant rate period, the product surface temperature can be held well below its degradation or ignition temperature." The latent-heat sink is what lets a 350 °C jet dry tissue safely; the corollary (p.385) is that once bound moisture dominates, surface temperature rises rapidly — the precise moment to back off flux.

8.4 — Energy anchors (p.388). "thermal efficiency of the order of 75–90%. Specific power consumption of 3100 kJ/kg of water evaporated has been reported for air-floater dryers." These two numbers are the energy-cost/tonne baseline for any impingement/air-floater line.

8.5 — Crossflow uniformity (p.389). "performance of adjoining jets can fall even by a factor of 2... For ASN, the effect of crossflow is severe... No more than two jet rows should be allowed prior to exhaust." Cross-machine moisture nonuniformity (web defect) is directly governed by exhaust-port placement — a concrete design rule.

## 9. Concept deep-dive (概念深掘)

9.1 Design procedure (§15.2). Six steps: (1) nozzle config, (2) geometry (≥50 designs exist, mostly arbitrary), (3) jet velocity/temperature/spacing — interrelated; Re must exceed ~2000 for fully turbulent; temperature set by product thermal sensitivity, (4) constant-rate drying-rate calc with corrections, (5) recycle ratio by enthalpy balance, (6) iterate for recycle temperature/humidity. A disciplined loop the hygiene engineer can lift directly.

9.2 Martin correlations (§15.3). For ARN: Nu = f(Re, Pr, H/d, f) with range 2000,000, 0.004, 2/d, ±15 %. For ASN: similar with 1500,000, 2.5₀, 1/S Optimal spacing ~1.40H (S≈0.2H, D≈0.18H). These let the reader size nozzle pitch and predict h for a tissue Yankee hood or nonwoven impingement zone.

9.3 Crossflow & exhaust (§15.4.3.1). Spent flow must be removed before crossing downstream jets; ASN severe, staggered ARN tolerates 3–5 rows. Drives the cross-machine uniformity of the dried web — a first-order quality variable.

9.4 Mass-flux correction (§15.4.3.3). High evaporation thickens the thermal–concentration boundary layer, reducing h/m by up to ~15 % (spray) / ~10 % (paper). Most correlations are no-net-mass-flux; apply Keey's film/boundary-layer correction factors.

9.5 Suction synergy (§15.4.3.5). Combined impingement + through-drying (suction) raises average h/m by 10–15 % and is "particularly useful for drying in the falling rate period of heat-sensitive products" (p.391) — the exact regime for nonwoven through-air bonding.

9.6 Nonuniform arrays (p.391). Varying D/pitch/S/W to tailor local rates is "particularly useful for drying in the falling rate period of heat-sensitive products" — a tuning lever for graded moisture profiles.

9.7 Swirl undesirable (p.391). Nu decreases with swirl number → do not introduce swirl.

## 10. Critique (批判性评估)

- Constant-rate bias. The chapter can only calculate constant-rate drying; falling-rate needs external data/models (p.384). Hygiene webs spend most residence in falling-rate → the chapter under-delivers there.
- Geometry limits. Correlations are stationary/impermeable/plane; moving permeable Yankee/nonwoven webs need extension.
- Large-ΔT gap. At jet 100–700 °C, measured h falls below Martin; "no correlation exists" for many configs (p.392) — high-temp tissue hoods must be validated experimentally.
- Recycle penalty. Up to 90 % exhaust recycle needed (p.385) but jet contact time too short to use moisture capacity → expensive air handling; the energy anchor (3100 kJ/kg) already includes this.
- OCR noise. Concatenated words ("impingement", "noflow") and dropped subscripts in Eqs/Nu; re-verify against print.
## 11. Hygiene-Material Relevance & Action Cards (卫材相关性与行动卡)

H11.1 — Gate impingement on unbound moisture only (★★★★★). Action: For tissue Yankee and nonwoven hoods, use impingement for the surface/constant-rate phase; switch to through-air or lower flux in the falling-rate/bound-moisture phase to avoid casehardening and curl (p.383–384).

H11.2 — Set jet T from evaporative-cooling window, not peak (★★★★★). Action: Exploit the constant-rate latent sink (p.385) to run 100–350 °C jets while holding web surface below degradation/ignition; back off flux once bound moisture dominates and surface T climbs (p.385).

H11.3 — Control coating streaks on wipes (★★★★). Action: For lotion/print-coated wipes, cap jet velocity and tune nozzle-to-substrate spacing to avoid shear-driven "railroading" streaks (p.384); validate coating rheology vs impact shear in lab.

H11.4 — Place exhaust to protect cross-machine uniformity (★★★★). Action: For slot-nozzle (ASN) hoods allow ≤2 jet rows before exhaust (p.389); design exhaust ports to keep crossflow from halving local h and producing moisture stripes across the web.

H11.5 — Cost energy at 3100 kJ/kg, 75–90 % efficiency (★★★★★). Action: Use 3100 kJ/kg water and 75–90 % thermal efficiency (p.388) as the energy-cost/tonne baseline when scoping a new impingement/air-floater line; challenge designs that fall outside this band.

H11.6 — UseMartin correlations to size nozzle pitch (★★★). Action: Apply ARN/ASN Nu correlations (p.388–389) with optimal spacing ~1.40H to predict h and set nozzle pitch for a tissue or nonwoven impingement zone; correct for mass flux (≈10 % paper).

H11.7 — Add suction for falling-rate nonwoven bonding (★★★). Action: Combine impingement with through-drying suction (+10–15 % h/m, p.390) for the falling-rate period of heat-sensitive nonwoven webs — the recommended synergy for permeable sheets.
