# Academic Chapter Analysis — Chapter 33: Drying of Fibrous Materials

Source: Mujumdar, A.S. (ed.), Handbook of Industrial Drying, 4th ed., CRC Press, 2015 — Chapter 33, "Drying of Fibrous Materials," by Roger B. Keey.
Reader profile: Hygiene-materials R&D engineer (disposable diapers, wipes, absorbent cores, fluff-pulp procurement).
Prepared: 2026-08-13

Page numbers derived from PDF-page − 21; offset anchored on Ch.1 = p.1 and should be spot-checked against the print edition.

## 1. Overview / Chapter Scope

Chapter 33 is the single most relevant chapter in the assignment for a hygiene-materials engineer. Keey treats fibres as elongated polymeric particles and systematically develops the physics of fibre–water interaction: sorption isotherms (BET, Henderson), bound vs. free water, anisotropic swelling/shrinkage, single-fibre diffusion (Fick's laws in cylindrical coordinates), the response of a fibre mass to hygrothermal change (Henry–Nordon fronts), convective/through-circulation/impingement drying, pulp-and-paper machine drying, and superheated-steam drying. The chapter closes with the explicit statement that press-drying bonds wet wood webs when the temperature exceeds the glass-transition point of lignin (~150 °C) — a direct polymer-physics anchor.

For the diaper/wipe engineer this chapter maps onto: fluff-pulp bale moisture and redrying damage (hornification), nonwoven/web through-drying, SAP–cellulose interfibre water, and the energy–quality trade-off of thermal drying on cellulosic structures.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (with verbatim quotes + print pages)

- Fibres are polymeric, anisotropic, and bonded by lignin. "Wood consists of a large number of fibers, together with cells of other types, bonded together by lignin to form a solid and rigid structure. The fibers are hollow, cylindrical structures, typically 1 to 4 mm in length, with walls composed mainly of cellulose and its associated polysaccharides. Most woods shrink and swell with moisture content, but the dimensional change is much smaller along the fiber length compared with the changes across the fiber by a factor of 50 to 100." (p. 754)
- Water = swelling; its loss = shrinkage; stresses = permanent set. "Water imbibed by the cell walls of fibers causes swelling and moisture loss causes shrinkage. Loading, including the development of drying stresses, introduces a creep strain that may not fully recover when the load is released. This is known as permanent set. With hydrophilic materials, moisture is found to reduce stiffness and increase creep, possibly as a result of plasticization." (p. 756)
- Bound water is energetically distinct from free water. "For most woods the differential heat of sorption is about half that of vaporization, falling to about one quarter of that value when a complete monolayer is formed at about 4 to 5% moisture content." (p. 756)
- Loose fibrous assemblies are NOT simple capillary-porous bodies. "a mass of loose fibers do not constitute a simple capillary-porous body, and the mechanisms that give rise to a constant-rate period in a porous network do not arise in a touching fibrous assembly. Recent experiments on the transport of water and n-propanol through samples of Kraft pulp and filter paper confirm lack of importance of capillarity." (p. 762)
- Single-fibre diffusion is fast; the mass is slow. "The moisture diffusion coefficient within a single wool fiber is of the order 10⁻¹¹ m² s⁻¹... for fiber diameters, typically 20 µm for wool, the fiber will respond to a change in moisture conditions very quickly, being about 98% complete within 2.5 s." (p. 759) — yet a bale penetrates only 150 mm/day (p. 761).
- Press-drying is polymer-bonding above Tg. "The main effect of press drying is the thermal softening of lignin and cellulose components. If temperatures 25 to 50°C above the glass-transition point of lignin is reached, then bonding can take place. This condition entails a temperature in the order of 150°C or above in the wet web." (p. 773)
## 3. Research Method / Approach

The chapter is a theory-led review. Keey builds from molecular (sorption isotherm thermodynamics, §33.2) → single-fibre (Fickian diffusion in a cylinder, §33.3) → fibre-mass (coupled heat–mass fronts, §33.4) → equipment-scale (convective, through-circulation, impingement, cylinder, steam, §33.5–33.10). The mathematical spine is Fick's first/second law recast in cylindrical coordinates with a concentration-dependent diffusion coefficient to absorb shrinkage, plus Henry's linearized two-front model for a hygroscopic bed. Experimental validation is cited throughout (King & Cassie vacuum uptake; Nordon wool-wad fronts; Nilsson pulp vapour diffusion; Keey/Wu suction-drum data).

## 4. Argumentation / Logical Structure

- What is a fibre? Elongated, polymeric, anisotropic; lignin-bonded; swelling/shrinking with MC.
- How does it hold water? Sorption isotherms (monolayer BET → multilayer → capillary condensation above fibre saturation point); bound water has heat-of-sorption ≈ ½–¼ of vaporization.
- How does water move in one fibre? Chemical-potential gradient → Fick's law; at low MC, sorptive (activated-site) diffusion dominates because water bonds strongly to hydrophilic sites.
- How does a fibre mass behave? Two fronts (fast thermal, slow moisture); local equilibrium usually holds at the single-fibre scale; the mass is limited by interfibre void transport and packing density.
- How is it dried industrially? Convective (no true constant-rate period for touching fibres), through-circulation (3 stages, airflow reversal evens MC), impingement (jet spacing rules), multicylinder paper machines (80% evaporation in free draws), superheated steam (inversion temperature ~160–230 °C; Tg-dependent thermal damage).
- What is the material cost? Shrinkage, permanent set (irreversible), lignin-bond formation above Tg.
## 5. Role in the Book / Volume Architecture

Ch33 is the definitive "fibrous/porous polymeric" anchor of the Handbook's materials block, directly complementing Ch31 (ceramics: rigid porous, shrinkage-cracking) and Ch28/29 (food/pharma). It is the only chapter that makes the polymer-physics link explicit (lignin Tg, plasticization). In the hygiene context it is the parent reference for fluff-pulp behaviour; Ch30 (nanosize) and Ch32 (peat) are peripheral by comparison.

## 6. Strengths and Weaknesses

Strengths: Unifies molecular sorption, single-fibre transport, and equipment-scale drying; explicitly denies the capillary-porous assumption for touching fibres (critical for nonwoven/web modelling); the Tg/press-drying statement is a rare, crisp polymer anchor. Weaknesses: Equations are OCR-degraded (Eq. 33.1, 33.2, 33.7 show stray symbols; symbol definitions scattered in nomenclature); no treatment of superabsorbent polymers or of interfibre liquid water held by SAP (the actual diaper core); "permanent set" is named but not linked to hornification vocabulary; kinetic models are empirical (n, K coefficients) rather than mechanistic for the falling-rate period.

## 7. Key Terms and Definitions

- Fibre saturation point (FSP): max hygroscopic MC when cell walls fully saturated; above RH≈0.98 lumens fill by capillary condensation (p. 755).
- Bound vs. free water: bound = sorbed in cell wall (heat of sorption < vaporization); free = liquid in lumens/interfibre voids.
- Hygroscopic regime: MC below FSP; activity ≠ concentration; sorptive (activated) diffusion.
- Permanent set: irreversible creep strain under drying stress (p. 756) — the viscoelastic precursor of hornification.
- Characteristic drying curve: normalized rate f = N/N_V vs F = (X−X_e)/(X_cr−X_e); falling-rate period f = A·Fⁿ (p. 762–763).
- Critical moisture content X_cr: transition unhindered→hindered drying; textile fabric ~0.41 kg/kg, paper tissue 2.6–4.0 kg/kg (p. 763).
- Inversion temperature (superheated steam): where evaporation into pure steam exceeds that into dry air; 160–230 °C experimentally, point-inversion 155–300 °C (p. 774).
- Glass-transition (lignin): bonding in wet web at Tg+25–50 °C ≈ 150 °C (p. 773).
## 8. Micro close-reading (verbatim excerpts)

- p. 754: "Pulping, by chemical or thermomechanical means, delignifies the structure, releasing individual fibers." → delignification = removing the Tg-bonded matrix; this is exactly what makes fluff pulp a loose, swellable mass.
- p. 755 Table 33.1: Woodpulp equilibrium MC at RH 0.2/0.5/1.0 = 0.034 / 0.062 / 0.25 kg/kg; cotton 0.0305/0.0565/0.23; viscose 0.0555/0.101/0.46. → quantitative hygroscopicity ranking for procurement specs.
- p. 756: "Wood fibers shrink anisotropically on drying below their saturation point. Walker cites... microfibrils in the cell wall restraint the cell wall matrix of lignin and hemicelluloses." → anisotropic shrinkage = oriented semicrystalline polymer composite.
- p. 763: "Dry paper fiber consists of about 100 lamellae, but is not porous. Water can diffuse into the fiber and dissolve within the cellulose and hemicellulose, causing swelling across the fiber but not along it. Completely wet paper consists of moisture between fibers and bound water within the solid matrix." → the canonical intrafibre(vs interfibre) water split.
- p. 773: press-drying Tg statement (quoted above) → the polymer-physics keystone.
## 9. Concept Deep-Dive — Fibre–Water Binding and the Hygiene Transfer

Bound vs. free water (the core hygiene distinction). Keey's FSP framing (p. 755) maps directly onto fluff pulp: below FSP, water is bound in the cell wall (heat of sorption ≈ ½–¼ ΔH_vap, p. 756) and cannot be pressed out; above FSP it is free liquid removable by mechanical pressing. Procurement and converting should therefore specify bale MC against this split — pressing removes free water cheaply; evaporative drying only attacks bound water at steep energy cost (cf. Ch32's marginal-returns cliff).

Sorptive (activated) diffusion at low MC. "at low moisture contents, sorbed water may form strong bonds with a hydrophilic fiber, so that simple diffusion can no longer occur. The concept of sorptive diffusion has been introduced... Only those molecules with kinetic energies greater than the activation energy of the moisture–fiber bonds can shift from one site to another" (p. 757). This is the molecular basis of hornification: each redrying cycle re-forms strong H-bonds at sorption sites, progressively locking swelling capacity. "Permanent set" (p. 756) is the macroscopic analogue — irreversible viscoelastic strain under drying stress.

Anisotropic swelling = oriented polymer composite. Shrinkage 50–100× greater across than along the fibre (p. 754), restrained by cellulose microfibrils in a lignin/hemicellulose matrix (p. 756), is precisely semicrystalline-polymer-composite behaviour. For airlaid/woodpulp webs this predicts in-plane vs through-thickness dimensional change — relevant to core dimensional stability in diapers.

Lignin Tg as the bonding switch. Press-drying bonds at lignin Tg+25–50 °C ≈ 150 °C (p. 773). This is the PoP-anchor: thermal softening of an amorphous polymer above Tg enables flow and interfibre bonding. For hygiene, the inverse lesson holds — avoid approaching lignin Tg in fluffy/non-bonded fluff where you want maximum swellability; reserve heat above ~150 °C for deliberately bonded structures (e.g., wet-laid cores, paper).

Superheated-steam inversion & thermal damage. Above the inversion temperature (160–230 °C) steam drying outpaces air; but "thermal damage to wool fibers... increased with temperature, exposure time, and pH... Presence of water in the material enhanced the degradation" (p. 773). Water acts as a degradative plasticizer at high T — a caution for steam-drying cellulosic hygiene stock.

## 10. Critical Assessment

Ch33 is rigorous, well-sourced, and unusually honest about the limits of capillary-porous theory for fibres. Its one structural gap for the hygiene reader is the absence of superabsorbent polymer and interfibre liquid held by SAP — the real physics of a diaper core. Nevertheless, every mechanism it develops (sorption, bound/free split, anisotropic swelling, permanent set, Tg bonding, steam inversion) transfers to fluff pulp and cellulosic nonwovens. The OCR-degraded equations require print verification before direct reuse in modelling.

## 11. Hygiene-Materials Relevance & Action Cards

Overall relevance: ★★★★★ (core chapter).

Action Card H33-1 — Specify bale MC against the FSP bound/free split. Insight: bound water (heat of sorption ½–¼ ΔH_vap) cannot be pressed out; free water can (p. 755–756). Action: Set fluff-pulp acceptance MC so that incoming water is predominantly free (above interpolated FSP for that pulp); reject "dry but bound-water-rich" claims by measuring equilibrium MC, not just oven MC. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Prevents paying evaporative energy for water that pressing/equilibration already removes; protects converting yield.

Action Card H33-2 — Quantify hygroscopicity from Table 33.1 for fibre selection. Insight: woodpulp 0.034/0.062/0.25 vs viscose 0.0555/0.101/0.46 at RH 0.2/0.5/1.0 (p. 755). Action: Use these equilibrium values to predict in-package MC drift and SAP–pulp partitioning under storage RH. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Lets you size SAP vs pulp ratio from sorption equilibrium rather than guesswork.

Action Card H33-3 — Treat every redry as hornification risk. Insight: sorptive (activated) diffusion + strong bonds at low MC (p. 757); "permanent set" irreversible creep (p. 756). Action: Minimise pulp re-wetting/redrying cycles in procurement and broke recovery; audit supplier drying history as a quality attribute. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Directly preserves pulp swellability = core absorbent capacity.

Action Card H33-4 — Keep fluffy fluff below lignin Tg; reserve ≥150 °C for bonded structures. Insight: press-drying bonds at lignin Tg+25–50 °C ≈ 150 °C (p. 773). Action: Cap thermal exposure of non-bonded airlaid/woodpulp at 150 °C deliberately only for wet-laid/bonded cores or paper. → 卫材相关性★★★★★｜Controls the swellability-vs-bonding trade-off at the process-design level.

Action Card H33-5 — Model webs as non-capillary; use characteristic drying curve. Insight: touching fibrous assemblies lack a true constant-rate period (p. 762); normalized f = A·Fⁿ with n≈0.6 (wool), n≈2/3 (thin wood) (p. 763); internal resistance negligible  mm thickness. Action: Build through-dryer/impingement models on the characteristic-curve form, not capillary-porous assumptions; exploit airflow reversal to even MC (p. 768). → 卫材相关性★★★★☆｜Improves nonwoven/airlaid dryer design and reduces over-drying.

Action Card H33-6 — Steam-drying caution: water enhances degradation above inversion. Insight: inversion 160–230 °C; water present accelerates thermal damage (p. 773). Action: If trialling superheated-steam drying of cellulosic stock, stay below inversion or below lignin-Tg-sensitive thresholds and verify brightness/strength. → 卫材相关性★★★★☆｜Avoids hidden strength/yellowness loss in hygiene-grade cellulose.

Action Card H33-7 — Pulp-machine lesson: 70% water removed by pressing, not heat. Insight: press section removes ~70% of water before drying; sheet emerges ~8% MC (p. 770); ~⅔ of multicylinder heat goes to evaporation, 22% lost from cylinders (p. 771). Action: In any converting line, maximise mechanical dewatering ahead of thermal drying. → 卫材相关性★★★★☆｜Energy-cost discipline directly portable to wet-laid hygiene lines.

## 12. Book-Map Quotes (positioning within the Handbook)

- Vs Ch31 (Ceramics): Ch31 fears shrinkage stress-cracking in a rigid porous body; Ch33 frames shrinkage as anisotropic, polymer-composite (microfibril-restrained) and adds permanent set — same phenomenon, different mechanism vocabulary.
- Vs Ch30 (Nanosize): Ch30's agglomeration/redispersibility of colloidal particles has no analogue here; Ch33's "sorptive diffusion / strong bonds at low MC" is the macroscopic counterpart to Ch30's interparticle bridging.
- Vs Ch32 (Peat/Biofuels): Ch32 states hygroscopicity is "of rather limited importance" (p. 750); Ch33 places hygroscopicity and bound-water energetics at the very centre. Opposite regimes, same Handbook.
- Vs Ch29 (Pharma): both use desorption isotherms; Ch29 for GMP residual-moisture/collapse, Ch33 for fibre-equilibrium and drying kinetics.
