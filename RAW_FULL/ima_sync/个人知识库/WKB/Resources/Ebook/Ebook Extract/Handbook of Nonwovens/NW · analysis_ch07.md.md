# Chapter 7 — Chemical bonding

## 1. Chapter Overview

Chapter 7 (R.A. Chapman) systematically treats chemical (latex/binder) bonding—the dominant alternative to thermal consolidation and the route of choice when a broad property palette (softness, waterproofness, breathability, flame resistance, recyclability) must be engineered into the fabric. It begins by defining the binder and its multiple roles, then surveys the polymer chemistry of binders: vinyl, acrylic, butadiene (SBR/NBR/natural rubber), and specialty systems (PU, phenolic, epoxy). A rigorous subsection explains emulsion polymerisation, the Fox equation for copolymer Tg, minimum film-forming temperature (MFFT), and the functionality of coagulation/migration and crosslinking. The chapter then analyses the mechanism of chemical bonding—wetting, binder cohesion, adhesion, distribution, and binder:fibre ratio—including the transition to a fibre-reinforced-polymer composite at high add-on. Application methods (saturation, foam, spray, print, coating, solution) and dryers (convection/through-air, conduction, radiation) follow. The chapter closes with explicit hygiene/medical and wipes applications, including flushable-wipe binder chemistry—making it one of the most directly applicable chapters for the target expert.

## 2. Key Viewpoints

### Viewpoint 1 — A binder does far more than hold fibres together

- Page: p.330
- Quote: > "A binder not only 'holds fibres together' but also affects the final properties of the nonwoven fabric including its strength (both tensile and compressive), stiffness, softness, waterproofness, breathability and flammability. The choice of binder also influences the capability of the fabric to be recycled or biochemically degraded at the end of its useful life."
- Interpretation: Establishes that binder selection is a multi-objective design problem—central to formulating wipe and hygiene substrates.
### Viewpoint 2 — Latices (emulsion polymers) are the workhorse binder

- Page: p.331
- Quote: > "Binder polymers can be dissolved in a solvent including water or they can be dispersions or emulsions. The most important binders are latices (also called latexes) of emulsion polymers. These are fine dispersions of specific polymers in water."
- Interpretation: Grounds the whole chapter in emulsion-polymer science; the water-borne latex is what makes low-viscosity penetration and energy-efficient drying possible.
### Viewpoint 3 — Copolymer Tg (Fox equation) governs handle and wet stability

- Page: p.334–335
- Quote: > "A calculated estimate of the Tg of any copolymer can be obtained using the Fox equation: 1/Tg = Wx/Tgx + Wy/Tgy … the wet stability of the binder is a consideration in some applications such as disposable wipes and incontinence products."
- Interpretation: Provides a quantitative handle-design tool: softer monomers (low Tg) → softer fabric; hydrophilicity of monomers sets wet stability—directly relevant to wipe/incontinence performance.
### Viewpoint 4 — Binder migration during drying is structural and can be beneficial or detrimental

- Page: p.343
- Quote: > "As an impregnated nonwoven dries, the temperature difference through the cross-section of the fabric can cause the binder polymer to migrate to the higher temperature regions. This differential migration results in a non-uniform distribution of binder where the surfaces tend to have a higher concentration of binder than the core of the fabric, which is depleted. This can lead to problems such as fabric delamination…"
- Interpretation: A key process–structure link; migration causes binder-rich surfaces / starved core, affecting delamination and handle—must be managed via thickeners or heat-sensitisation.
### Viewpoint 5 — Wettability is governed by fibre surface energy; PP is hard to bond

- Page: p.347
- Quote: > "Polypropylene fibres have a low surface energy (about 23 mN/m) and are difficult to bond. Corona and plasma treatments are sometimes used to change the chemical nature of the surface and improve wetting. Polyester fibres have a higher surface energy of about 42 mN/m. Cellulose fibres not only have a higher surface energy than both polypropylene and polyester synthetic fibres, they are also relatively porous enabling liquid to penetrate…"
- Interpretation: Quantifies why cellulose/viscose wipes wet easily while PP needs treatment—foundational for hygiene-web bonding and rewet behaviour.
### Viewpoint 6 — Hygiene/wipes applications are explicit and chemistry-specific

- Page: p.361–362
- Quote: > "Incontinence products are often chemically bonded and can be based on carded or airlaid webs. These products must have high absorption and softness and so binder technologies include soft SBR and self-crosslinking butyl acrylates."
- Interpretation: Maps binder chemistry directly onto the absorbent hygiene product requirement set (absorption + softness + sterilisation resistance).

## 4. Argumentation Process

The logic flows: (1) define binder and its multi-property role; (2) enumerate binder polymer families and their chemistry; (3) explain how latices form (emulsion polymerisation) and why Tg/MFFT control film formation and handle; (4) introduce functionality—coagulation/migration and crosslinking—as levers; (5) analyse the bonding mechanism at the fibre level (wetting, cohesion, adhesion, distribution, FRP transition); (6) enumerate application and drying methods; (7) close with wipes and hygiene/medical applications that concretise the chemistry. The argument is deductive from polymer physics to formulation, repeatedly anchored to disposable/hygiene examples.

## 5. Role in the Book

Chapter 7 is the counterpart to Chapter 6 (thermal bonding) and the bridge to Chapter 8 (finishing/lamination, which reuses coating/padding/print methods and often applies binder+active chemistries). Chapter 9 (§9.2.5) characterises chemically bonded structures and §7.6 hygiene chemistry is the substantive content behind "hygiene and medical" testing in Table 9.1. Together 6–8 form the consolidation-and-finishing spine of the Handbook.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths:
- Excellent polymer-chemistry grounding (emulsion polymerisation, Fox Tg, MFFT, crosslinking classification) usable by formulators.
- Explicit, chemistry-specific hygiene/wipes/flushable sections (§7.6.2, §7.6.4).
- Surface-energy data (PP 23, PET 42 mN/m; cellulose porous) is directly actionable for bonding/wetting design.

⚠️ Limitations / contestable points:
- Binder add-on "between about 5% and 150% on the dry weight of fabric" (p.331) is a very wide range with little guidance on where hygiene products sit within it.
- Crosslinking/formaldehyde discussion notes health concerns but the book predates later stringent VOC/PFAS-era regulations.
- Mechanism section is qualitative on the quantitative adhesion thresholds; wetting is asserted via surface-energy ranking rather than measured contact angles per system.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Binder: (p.330) "an adhesive material used to hold together the fibres in a nonwoven structure."
- Latex/emulsion polymer: (p.331) "fine dispersions of specific polymers in water… viscosity is close to that of water, they can easily penetrate thick or dense nonwoven structures by simple immersion."
- Fox equation (copolymer Tg): (p.334) "1/Tg = Wx/Tgx + Wy/Tgy."
- Minimum film forming temperature (MFFT): (p.341) "the lowest temperature at which an emulsion polymer can form a continuous film. It is usually several degrees above the glass transition temperature (Tg)."
- Binder migration: (p.343) differential drying drives binder "to the higher temperature regions… surfaces tend to have a higher concentration of binder than the core."
- Crosslinking: (p.344) "providing covalent bonds between polymer chains, which reduce their mobility" → increased stiffness and waterproofness.
- Fibre-reinforced polymer (FRP) transition: (p.348) at high binder:fibre ratio the nonwoven "effectively becomes a fibre-reinforced polymer (FRP) composite."
## 8. Micro close-reading

Paragraph (p.343, binder migration during drying):
> "As an impregnated nonwoven dries, the temperature difference through the cross-section of the fabric can cause the binder polymer to migrate to the higher temperature regions. This differential migration results in a non-uniform distribution of binder where the surfaces tend to have a higher concentration of binder than the core of the fabric, which is depleted. This can lead to problems such as fabric delamination but can be beneficial in some applications such as in the manufacture of synthetic leather. In this process, the impregnated nonwoven is split through its thickness, as is natural leather, and it is important that each 'split' component has similar properties."

- Sentence 1 ("As an impregnated nonwoven dries… migrate to the higher temperature regions"): Identifies the driver—through-thickness temperature gradient during drying. Technique: causal statement.
- Sentence 2 ("This differential migration results in a non-uniform distribution… core… depleted"): States the structural outcome—binder-rich skin, starved core. Keyword: "depleted."
- Sentence 3 ("This can lead to problems such as fabric delamination…"): Negative consequence. Logic: effect of sentence 2.
- Sentence 4 ("but can be beneficial… manufacture of synthetic leather"): Contrarian qualification—same phenomenon is exploited deliberately elsewhere. Technique: dialectical counterpoint.
- Sentence 5 ("In this process… each 'split' component has similar properties"): Explains the beneficial case requires uniform splits. Keyword: "similar properties."
💡 Standout line: "the surfaces tend to have a higher concentration of binder than the core of the fabric, which is depleted"—the single sentence that explains most cosmetic/delamination defects in chemically bonded hygiene substrates.

## 9. Concept deep-dive

Core concept 1 — Copolymer glass-transition temperature (Fox equation) as a handle-design tool.
- Definition (p.334): "A calculated estimate of the Tg of any copolymer can be obtained using the Fox equation: 1/Tg = Wx/Tgx + Wy/Tgy" where Tgx, Tgy are homopolymer Tg and Wx, Wy weight fractions.
- Operational definition? Yes—Table 7.2 gives monomer Tg values (e.g., 2-ethylhexyl acrylate –85 °C, vinyl acetate +30 °C, styrene +105 °C) enabling quantitative formulation.
- Usage in chapter: introduced in §7.2.2, then tied to handle (p.335), MFFT (p.341), and hydrophilicity/wet stability for wipes & incontinence (p.335).
- Relation: links monomer selection → Tg → film formation → fabric softness/water stability.
- Academic origin: standard polymer-physics relation (Fox & Flory); tables from binder suppliers.

Core concept 2 — Crosslinking classification and its effect.
- Definition (p.344): "Crosslinking the binder polymer can increase stiffness and waterproofness… by providing covalent bonds between polymer chains, which reduce their mobility." Classes: non-crosslinking, crosslinkable, self-crosslinking, thermosetting.
- Operational definition? Yes—functional groups (carboxyl, hydroxyl, N-methylol, amine, epoxy) and external resins (melamine/urea formaldehyde) specified.
- Usage: pervades §7.2.7 and the hygiene binder lists (self-crosslinking butyl acrylates, p.362).
- Relation: connects monomer functionality → cure → waterproofness/durability, and to formaldehyde concerns.

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- The chapter is strong on chemistry but weak on quantitative property prediction: no model links monomer recipe → add-on → final tensile/softness for a given web.
- Open question 1: For flushable wipes (starches, CMC, PEO, PVA, PLA+superabsorbent, water-soluble sheath, p.361), what is the rate/extent of in-sewer disintegration versus the on-body wet-strength required during use—an unresolved design conflict?
- Open question 2: How do modern formaldehyde-free / low-VOC crosslinkers (beyond the acetoacetamide scavengers mentioned, p.344) trade off cure temperature and hydrolytic stability in incontinence products?
- Scope: little on bio-based binder mechanical limits vs. petrochemical SBR/acrylic benchmarks.
## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Relevance: ★★★★★ — Chemical bonding supplies the binder chemistry for wipes, airlaid absorbent cores, incontinence products, and flushable wipes; §7.6 is essentially a hygiene-formulation guide.

ACTION CARD 1 — Formulate a soft, wet-stable wipe binder
- Situation: Designing a disposable wipe needing wet strength + softness (p.335, p.338, p.361).
- How to apply: Use viscose rayon + small polyester for wet strength; choose a soft acrylic or EVA binder (low-Tg monomers via Fox eq., p.334); for crosslinkable water resistance use carboxylated/acrylic systems; nitrile rubber (Tg < –18 °C) for cold-flexibility.
- Expected effect: Wipe with high wet strength, soft handle, and controlled hydrophobicity.

ACTION CARD 2 — Prevent binder migration in absorbent cores
- Situation: Airlaid/carded incontinence web shows surface-hardening or delamination (p.343).
- How to apply: Use heat-sensitisable latex (coagulates on fibres) or thickeners; manage dryer temperature profile so coagulation T is ≥5 °C below wet-bulb T (Troesch & Hoffman, p.343).
- Expected effect: Uniform through-thickness binder, less delamination, softer core.

ACTION CARD 3 — Select flushable-wipe binder chemistry
- Situation: Product must be dispersible in water yet strong in use (p.361).
- How to apply: Choose soluble polymer binders—starches, CMC, PEO, PVA, polyacrylates, PLA+superabsorbent blends, or bicomponent water-soluble sheath fibres; avoid permanent crosslinked SBR.
- Expected effect: In-use strength with sewer/dispersal compatibility.
