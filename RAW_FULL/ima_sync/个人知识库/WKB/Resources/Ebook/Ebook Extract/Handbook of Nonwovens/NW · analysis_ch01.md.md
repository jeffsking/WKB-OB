# Chapter 1 — Development of the nonwovens industry

## 1. Chapter Overview

Chapter 1 (by A. Wilson) establishes the conceptual and industrial scaffolding of the nonwovens field before the technology chapters that follow. It opens by acknowledging that any definition of "nonwoven" carries exceptions, because the industry parasitically drew on textile, paper and polymer-processing know-how. The chapter then fixes the EDANA and INDA definitions, classifies nonwovens into drylaid, wetlaid and polymer-laid (spunmelt) families, surveys raw materials (with polypropylene dominant), maps global market structure and growth, and closes with the hygiene-disposables sector as the single largest end-use. The narrative logic runs from taxonomy → process families → materials → market → hygiene focus, deliberately positioning nonwovens as a sophisticated, high-margin, R&D-intensive industry rather than a cheap textile substitute. For a hygiene-materials R&D reader this is the map that locates every later chapter (drylaid, wetlaid, polymer-laid, mechanical bonding) inside the diaper/wipe value chain.

## 2. Key Viewpoints

### Viewpoint 1 — Nonwovens resist a single clean definition

- Page: p.1
- Quote: > "In defining what a nonwoven is, there is always at least one exception that breaks the rule. This is perhaps fitting, since while being now recognised in its own right, the nonwovens industry has drawn on the practices and know-how of many other more well-established fields of polymer and materials manufacturing with a piratical disregard…"
- Interpretation: The author frames nonwovens as a convergent, boundary-crossing industry. For R&D this warns that process choice (textile/paper/polymer) determines material behaviour — relevant when selecting a topsheet or ADL route.
### Viewpoint 2 — EDANA and INDA definitions

- Page: p.1–2
- Quote: > "EDANA… defines a nonwoven as 'a manufactured sheet, web or batt of directionally or randomly orientated fibres, bonded by friction, and/or cohesion and/or adhesion'… INDA… describes nonwoven fabrics as 'sheet or web structures bonded together by entangling fibres or filaments, by various mechanical, thermal and/or chemical processes. These are made directly from separate fibres or from molten plastic or plastic film.'"
- Interpretation: Two authoritative definitions anchor the whole book; the ISO/EN 29092 fibre length-to-diameter (>300) and density ( g/cm³) thresholds (p.1–2) are the legal/standards basis that later chapters (wetlaid especially) invoke.
### Viewpoint 3 — Hygiene is the dominant end-use

- Page: p.4
- Quote: > "Hygiene is by far the largest of these categories, accounting for over 33% of European nonwovens production, followed by civil engineering/construction and building materials with 17.9%."
- Interpretation: This single statistic justifies the book's (and this analysis's) hygiene-centred lens. Wipes add a further 14.8% (p.4), confirming the domain's centrality.
### Viewpoint 4 — Three process families trace to three mother industries

- Page: p.5
- Quote: > "…drylaid materials have their origins in textiles, wetlaid materials in papermaking, and polymer-laid products in polymer extrusion and plastics (remembering that there is always at least one exception to the rule)."
- Interpretation: A clean taxonomy that the entire handbook is built around; it previews Ch2 (drylaid), Ch3 (wetlaid), Ch4 (polymer-laid), Ch5 (bonding).
### Viewpoint 5 — Polymer-laid (spunmelt) is overwhelmingly hygiene-driven

- Page: p.7
- Quote: > "Hygiene product components such as coverstocks, backs, distribution layers and leg-cuffs account for around 62% of spunmelt production, of which spunmelt materials account for around 65% of hygiene product components, and this is expected to rise still further to at least 72% in the coming years."
- Interpretation: Quantifies the tight coupling between spunbond/meltblown and diaper architecture (coverstock, ADL, backs, cuffs) — the spine of Ch4's relevance.
### Viewpoint 6 — Man-made fibres, especially PP, dominate

- Page: p.10
- Quote: > "Man-made fibres completely dominate nonwovens production, accounting for over 90% of total output… ∑ polypropylene 63% ∑ polyester 23% ∑ viscose rayon 8%…"
- Interpretation: Polymerchemistry choice is the master variable. PP's 63% share sets up Ch4's detailed treatment of PP resin grade / MFR / stereoregularity for spunbond & meltblown.

## 4. Argumentation Process

The author builds the case by (a) destabilising the definition to show the field's hybrid nature; (b) imposing order via EDANA/INDA definitions and the dry/wet/polymer-laid split; (c) demonstrating scale and maturity with market data (Tables 1.2, 1.3, 1.4); (d) narrowing to hygiene as the largest, fastest, most capital-intensive segment. Rhetorical moves: use of the "exception that breaks the rule" leitmotif, and repeated quantification to convert a qualitative narrative into an evidence-backed industrial map.

## 5. Role in the Book

Chapter 1 is the orienting frame. It introduces the three web-formation families and the bonding concept that Ch2–Ch5 each develop technically, and it seeds the hygiene dominance motif that makes later polymer/fibre detail meaningful. It links forward to Ch2 (drylaid carding/airlaid), Ch3 (wetlaid), Ch4 (spunmelt), Ch5 (bonding), and to Ch1's raw-material table that reappears in process-specific fibre choices.

✅ Strengths: Authoritative, well-sourced market data; clear taxonomy that structures the whole handbook; explicit hygiene focus valuable to the target reader. ⚠️ Limitations / contestable: Data are circa 2004–2005 and now dated (e.g., China capacity, spunmelt share); the chapter is descriptive with no physical/chemical mechanism, so it cannot by itself inform formulation work. ## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Nonwoven: (p.1) "a manufactured sheet, web or batt of directionally or randomly orientated fibres, bonded by friction, and/or cohesion and/or adhesion".
- Drylaid / Wetlaid / Polymer-laid: (p.5) origins in textiles / papermaking / polymer extrusion respectively.
- Spunmelt: (p.7) encompasses spunbond, meltblown, flash-spun.
- MD:CD ratio: (p.8) machine-direction/cross-direction property ratio; a proxy for fibre orientation / isotropy.
- Web formation vs. bonding: (p.8–9) two stages; bonding degree "is a primary factor in determining fabric mechanical properties (particularly strength), porosity, flexibility, softness, and density".
## 8. Micro close-reading (ONE key paragraph)

Most important paragraph — p.7 (spunmelt hygiene share):
> "Most of the first spunbonding systems were originated by fibre producers such as DuPont in the USA, Rhone-Poulenc in France and Freudenberg in Germany. DuPont is regarded as the first to successfully commercialise spunbonding with its Typar product… Global spunmelt demand has grown on average by 11% per annum in recent years and it now has an estimated 25% share of the global nonwovens industry. Hygiene product components such as coverstocks, backs, distribution layers and leg-cuffs account for around 62% of spunmelt production…"

Sentence-by-sentence:
1. Function: Historical attribution — roots spunbond in fibre producers, not textile mills. Technique: proper-noun authority (DuPont/Freudenberg). Keywords: "originated by fibre producers".
2. Function: Concrete commercialisation milestone (Typar, mid-1960s). Technique: dated exemplar.
3. Function: Scale claim — 11% p.a. growth, 25% global share. Technique: quantitative trend. Logic: establishes momentum.
4. Function: Links scale to hygiene — 62% of spunmelt = coverstock/ADL/backs/cuffs. Technique: component enumeration. Logic: this is the hinge sentence connecting Ch4 technology to diaper design.
💡 Standout: "coverstocks, backs, distribution layers and leg-cuffs account for around 62% of spunmelt production" — the sentence that makes every later PP spunbond discussion matter to a diaper engineer.

## 9. Concept deep-dive

Core concept — the dry/wet/polymer-laid taxonomy (p.5).
- Definition (p.5): quoted above; origins trace to textiles, papermaking, polymer extrusion.
- Operational definition? Yes — used throughout the book as the chapter-organising principle (Ch2 drylaid, Ch3 wetlaid, Ch4 polymer-laid).
- Usage in THIS chapter: frames market tables (p.5–9), raw-material discussion (p.10), and the hygiene-share statistic (p.7).
- Relation to other concepts: web formation (p.8) + web bonding (p.9) are the two transformation stages; MD:CD ratio (p.8) measures anisotropy produced by each route.
- Academic origin: EDANA / ISO 9092:1988 / EN 29092 standards (p.1–2).

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

The chapter is a snapshot, so its limitations are temporal and methodological rather than theoretical: (1) all market figures pre-date ~2005 Chinese capacity explosion; (2) no causal mechanism for why PP dominates beyond a bullet list (p.9–10) — the polymer-science "why" is deferred to Ch4 and to polymer chemistry. Open question: how has the dry/wet/spunmelt split shifted with the rise of carded-spunlaced (hydroentangled) wipes and airlaid cores (see Ch2, Ch5)?
