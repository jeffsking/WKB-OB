# Chapter 4 — Polymer-laid web formation (spunbond & meltblown)

## 1. Chapter Overview

Chapter 4 is the technical core of the book's "web formation" triptych (dry-laid, wet-laid, polymer-laid). It treats polymer-laid nonwovens — spunbond (spunlaid) and meltblown — as extensions of conventional melt-spinning in which extrusion, drawing, lay-down and bonding are integrated into one continuous line. The chapter opens (§4.2) with polymer science: polyolefin resin architecture (isotactic/syndiotactic/atactic PP; HDPE/LDPE/LLDPE; PET; PA; TPU; rayon; bicomponents), the resin properties that govern spinnability (melting point, thermal-bonding window, molecular-weight distribution, melt viscosity, cleanliness), and the single-site metallocene catalyst revolution. It then describes spunbond process engineering (§4.3–4.7), meltblown process engineering and its extensive parametric/modelling literature (§4.9–4.13), and finally composites and future trends (§4.14–4.15). The argument is cumulative: resin molecular structure sets the processing window, the processing window sets fibre diameter and web architecture, and fibre architecture sets the end-use performance (strength, barrier, filtration, absorption) that makes spunmelt the dominant family in hygiene and medical disposables.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (3–6, each with page + verbatim quote + interpretation)

### Viewpoint 1 — Isotactic PP stereochemistry and the MFR window define spinnability

- Page: p.146–147
- Quote: > "Isotactic PP is a stereo specific polymer because the propylene units are added head to tail so that their methyl groups are all on the same side of the plane of the polymeric backbone. It crystallizes in a helical form and exhibits good mechanical properties, such as stiffness and tensile strength. … Spunbonding usually requires a narrow molecular weight distribution and high melt flow rate (MFR) resins, typically of 30 to 80 MFR. Melt blowing is compatible with a variety of narrow molecular weight melt flow rate resins, typically these are 30 to 1500 MFR."
- Interpretation: The chapter roots spunmelt processing in polymer stereochemistry. Isotacticity gives PP its drawability and stiffness; the MFR bands (30–80 for spunbond, 30–1500 for meltblown) are the resin-selection hard constraints that let the same polymer serve both coarse structural webs and sub-micron filtration media — the backbone of hygiene nonwovens.
### Viewpoint 2 — The thermal-bonding window separates PP from PE and sets composite design

- Page: p.160
- Quote: > "Most PP resins melt at around 165 °C whereas PE resins melt at around 120 to 140 °C. … The thermal bonding range for PP is from 125 to 155 °C and for PE it ranges from 90 to 110 °C."
- Interpretation: The ~25–45 °C separation between PP and PE melting/softening ranges is the physical basis for sheath–core and through-air thermal bonding of bicomponent webs. For hygiene R&D this is why a PP/PE bicomponent can bond at PE's low temperature while the PP core preserves strength — the mechanism behind soft, hole-free topsheets and ADL structures.
### Viewpoint 3 — Narrow molecular-weight distribution is required to avoid draw resonance

- Page: p.173–174
- Quote: > "Both spunbonding and meltblowing require relatively narrow molecular weight resins. The narrow molecular weight distribution reduces the melt elasticity and melt strength of the resin so that the melt stream can be drawn into fine denier filaments without excessive draw force. The broad MWD increases melt elasticity and melt strength, which prohibit fiber drawing therefore a broad MWD resin is prone to produce fiber breaks due to draw resonance (melt instability) phenomena."
- Interpretation: This is a causal claim linking resin polydispersity to process stability. A broad MWD raises melt elasticity → fibre breaks via draw resonance. For a disposable producer, resin MWD/PDI is not a cosmetic spec but a defect-rate driver in fine-denier spunbond and meltblown lines.
### Viewpoint 4 — Metallocene single-site catalysts widen the design space

- Page: p.147–148
- Quote: > "The most commonly used single-site catalyst to manufacture polyolefin resins is the metallocene catalyst. … The fiber grade metallocene based polyolefin resin used in spunbonding and meltblowing offer the following advantages over conventional resin: finer denier fibers than conventional resins; the optimum bonding temperatures in spunbonding are lower because of the lower melting point; fabric strengths are comparable; excellent spinning continuity; can be spun at higher draw force; substantial reduction in volatile deposits; a broader MFR range, especially for meltblowing."
- Interpretation: Metallocene control of molecular structure, MWD and comonomer placement lets producers push finer deniers, lower bond temperatures and broader MFR — all levers for softer, more permeable, lower-energy hygiene fabrics versus Ziegler–Natta resins.
### Viewpoint 5 — Spunbond draw and electrostatic separation set web uniformity

- Page: p.166–167
- Quote: > "In spunbond production, filament bundles are partially oriented by pneumatic acceleration speeds of up to about 6,000 m/min. … For such application, the filaments are drawn over heated rolls with a typical draw ratio of 3.5:1. … The electrostatic charge on the filaments must be at least 30,000 esu/m². The belt is usually made of an electrically grounded conductive wire. Upon deposition, the belt discharges the filaments."
- Interpretation: Two levers control spunbond fabric quality: mechanical draw (pneumatic up to 6,000 m/min; roll draw 3.5:1 for strength) and electrostatic filament separation (≥30,000 esu/m²) that prevents filament clumping before lay-down. Uniform, separated filaments are what give a printable, low-basis-weight topsheet its cover and softness.
### Viewpoint 6 — Spunbond/meltblown composites and Coform® are purpose-built for hygiene liquid containment

- Page: p.172, p.192–193
- Quote: > "The use of spunbond fabrics in coverstock for diapers and incontinence devices has grown dramatically. This is mainly because of the structural characteristics of spunbonds, which helps keep the skin of the user dry and comfortable." — and — "The Coform® process … produces meltblown webs containing wood pulp as a liquid absorbent. … The fabrics are composed of approximatly 60–70% wood pulp and are particularly thin given their liquid absorption properties. … These are used in liquid containment applications in the hygiene sector. Applications include wipes for domestic, hospital and nursing homes, incontinence, birthing, and nursing pads and fenestration areas on drapes."
- Interpretation: The chapter closes the hygiene loop: spunbond supplies the dry-comfort topsheet; the SMS laminate supplies barrier; and Coform® (60–70% wood pulp co-formed with meltblown) supplies a thin, high-absorption core/adlayer — the three polymer-laid building blocks of a modern diaper.

## 4. Argumentation Process

The author builds the case as a chain: (a) resin structure → processability (isotactic PP, PE branching, MWD, MFR set the extrusion/spinning envelope); (b) process → fibre (draw ratio, air attenuation, electrostatic separation fix filament diameter and orientation); (c) fibre → web property (random vs oriented lay-down, bond geometry set strength, cover, barrier); (d) web property → end-use (filtration, absorption, liquid containment in hygiene/medical). Rhetorically the chapter leans on authoritative enumeration ("The main resin characteristics affecting the extrusion and spinning processes … are as follows:") and on consolidated empirical findings (the meltblown parametric literature) rather than on a single novel proof. The SMS/Coform® section is the payoff that ties polymer-laid technology back to the hygiene market established in Chapter 1.

## 5. Role in the Book

Chapter 4 is the third web-formation chapter and the most polymer-science intensive. It complements Ch.2 (dry-laid, mechanical fibre handling) and Ch.3 (wet-laid, slurry/fluid mechanics) by focussing on melt as the carrier — i.e. the resin itself becomes the feedstock. It feeds directly into Ch.5 (mechanical bonding) and Ch.6+ (chemical/thermal bonding), since most spunmelt webs are self-bonded or thermal/calender bonded. Its hygiene framing (coverstock, SMS, Coform®) also answers the market data of Ch.1 (spunmelt ≈ 25% of nonwovens, ~62% of hygiene).

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths: Exceptional breadth on resin–process–property causality; the MWD/draw-resonance explanation (p.173–174) and the electrostatic-separation threshold (p.167) are concrete, actionable engineering facts. The meltblown parametric survey (p.174–185) is a rare consolidated view given industry secrecy.
> "The broad MWD increases melt elasticity and melt strength, which prohibit fiber drawing therefore a broad MWD resin is prone to produce fiber breaks due to draw resonance (melt instability) phenomena." (p.174)

⚠️ Limitations / contestable points:
- The chapter is descriptive on why metallocene lowers bonding temperature (cites "lower melting point" without mechanism) — a polymer-thermodynamics gap.
- Meltblown fibre diameters are quoted in "mm" throughout (e.g. "1–5 mm", "0.5 and 30 mm", p.173, p.185) where the intended unit is unambiguously µm (the text itself calls them "microfibers" / "submicron", and meltblown fibres are by definition ~1–5 µm). This is a unit-transcription error in the source; [inference] the conventional values are 1–5 µm (typical 2–7 µm).
- Heavy reliance on patented/commercial know-how means quantitative process–property models (eqns 4.3–4.11) are presented but their fitted constants are vendor-specific.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Melt flow rate (MFR): (p.147) "Spunbonding usually requires … high melt flow rate (MFR) resins, typically of 30 to 80 MFR. Melt blowing is compatible with … 30 to 1500 MFR."
- Thermal bonding window: (p.160) "The thermal bonding range for PP is from 125 to 155 °C and for PE it ranges from 90 to 110 °C."
- Molecular-weight distribution (MWD): (p.174) "narrow molecular weight distribution reduces the melt elasticity and melt strength … broad MWD … prone to produce fiber breaks due to draw resonance."
- Metallocene / single-site catalyst: (p.147) "all sites are identical. This in turn gives identical polymers and minimizes the variability of polymers."
- Meltblowing: (p.173) "a thermoplastic fiber forming polymer is extruded through a linear die … Convergent streams of hot air … rapidly attenuate the extruded polymer streams to form extremely fine diameter fibers."
- SMS composite: (p.192) "the spunbond fabric provides the strength and the abrasion resistance and the meltblown component provides the liquid and particulates barrier."
- Coform®: (p.193) "meltblown webs containing wood pulp as a liquid absorbent … composed of approximatly 60–70% wood pulp."
- Draw resonance: (p.174) fibre-break instability from broad MWD melt elasticity.
## 8. Micro close-reading (ONE key paragraph)

Source paragraph (p.193, Coform®):
> "The Coform® process is operated by Kimberly Clark and produces meltblown webs containing wood pulp as a liquid absorbent. During the process, the wood pulp in sheet form is fiberized and the separated pulp is injected from one side onto the still tacky meltblown filaments as they travel from the die to the collector. In this way, the wood pulp adheres to the filaments as they cool. The fabrics are composed of approximatly 60–70% wood pulp and are particularly thin given their liquid absorption properties. Once the web is laid down, a preformed spunbond or meltblown fabric or film is thermally laminated to at least one side to form an absorbent composite. By changing the number and composition of these layers, a variety of products can be produced. These are used in liquid containment applications in the hygiene sector. Applications include wipes for domestic, hospital and nursing homes, incontinence, birthing, and nursing pads and fenestration areas on drapes."

Sentence-by-sentence breakdown:
1. "operated by Kimberly Clark … produces meltblown webs containing wood pulp as a liquid absorbent" — names the owner and the design intent: meltblown is the binder matrix, wood pulp the absorbent phase. Technique: co-formulation in-line.
2. "the wood pulp in sheet form is fiberized … injected … onto the still tacky meltblown filaments" — describes the in-flight integration; the key word is "still tacky": bonding is autoadhesive, no extra binder needed. Logic: tackiness ⇒ adhesion ⇒ composite.
3. "the wood pulp adheres to the filaments as they cool" — cooling freezes the structure; causality from thermal state to final architecture.
4. "composed of approximatly 60–70% wood pulp and are particularly thin given their liquid absorption properties" — the quantitative claim (60–70% pulp) and the value proposition (thin + high absorption) — keywords: thin, liquid absorption.
5. "a preformed spunbond or meltblown fabric or film is thermally laminated to at least one side" — closure/layer adds strength or barrier; technique: thermal lamination.
6. "By changing the number and composition of these layers, a variety of products can be produced" — design rule: layer engineering = product variety.
7. "used in liquid containment applications in the hygiene sector. Applications include wipes … incontinence, birthing, and nursing pads" — end-use mapping; keywords: liquid containment, hygiene sector.

💡 Standout line: "The fabrics are composed of approximatly 60–70% wood pulp and are particularly thin given their liquid absorption properties" — the entire art of the thin absorbent core is captured in one number (60–70% pulp) and one contradiction resolved (thin yet highly absorbent).

## 9. Concept deep-dive

Concept A — Polymer-laid (spunmelt) web formation.
- Definition (p.146, p.173): spunbond = "an integrated and continuous production line" combining filament extrusion, drawing, lay-down and bonding; meltblowing = "a thermoplastic fiber forming polymer is extruded through a linear die containing several hundred small orifices … Convergent streams of hot air … rapidly attenuate the extruded polymer streams."
- Operational definition? Yes — both are defined by process geometry (die, attenuating air, collector), not by a final property.
- Usage in this chapter: spunbond used for coverstock/strength (p.172); meltblown for filtration/barrier (p.173, p.186); composites (SMS, Coform®) for hygiene (p.192–193).
- Academic origin: extension of conventional melt-spinning (Ziabicki, Fundamentals of fibre formation, cited ref. 13); meltblowing pioneered by Wente/Naval Research Laboratory (ref. 67, 74).

Concept B — Melt flow rate (MFR) and molecular-weight distribution (MWD).
- Definition (p.147, p.174): MFR = processability index; MWD narrowness required to "draw into fine denier filaments without excessive draw force."
- Operational definition? Yes — MFR bands (30–80 spunbond; 30–1500 meltblown) are direct resin-spec targets.
- Relation: MFR ↑ ⇒ lower viscosity ⇒ finer fibre; MWD narrow ⇒ lower melt elasticity ⇒ stable draw; broad MWD ⇒ draw resonance ⇒ breaks (p.174).
- Academic origin: polymer rheology / melt-spinning stability (Ziabicki; melt-spinning force balance eqns 4.3–4.11).

Concept C — Electrostatic filament separation (spunbond).
- Definition (p.167): charge "at least 30,000 esu/m²" induced triboelectrically or by high voltage, discharged on a grounded belt.
- Operational definition? Yes — a measurable minimum surface-charge threshold for web uniformity.
- Usage: prevents filament clumping before lay-down → uniform low-basis-weight coverstock.

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- Theoretical: The melt-spinning force balance (eqns 4.3–4.11) is single-filament; the chapter admits multi-filament spunbond/meltblown equations are "quite complex" and out of scope (p.188). Real lines are multi-jet, turbulent, interacting — a real gap between model and plant.
- Methodological: Meltblown parametric literature is contradictory on orifice size's effect on final diameter (Wente/Malkan: "very little effect"; Wadsworth & Jones / Khan: significant effect, p.1446–1467). The chapter reports the conflict but offers no resolution — likely die-geometry/air-regime dependent.
- Scope: "mm" vs "µm" unit error (see §6) undermines precise fibre-diameter reading; a hygiene engineer must mentally convert every quoted diameter.
- Open questions: (1) Can metallocene-controlled MWD push meltblown below 1 µm at commercial throughput without shot? (2) How does Coform® pulp loading trade off against web tensile once pulp >70%? (3) What is the ageing/stability of electret charge in PP meltblown under humid, warm hygiene use?
## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Relevance to diaper / wipe / absorbent-core R&D: ★★★★★ (spunmelt is the dominant hygiene nonwoven family; the chapter directly covers coverstock, SMS barrier, Coform® core, and meltblown wipes).

ACTION CARD 1 — Resin spec for a new topsheet line
- Situation: Selecting PP resin for a fine-denier spunbond coverstock.
- How to apply: Specify narrow-MWD, high-MFR (30–80) isotactic homopolymer; consider metallocene grade for lower bond temp + finer denier (p.147–148, p.174).
- Expected effect: Fewer draw-resonance breaks, softer/lower-basis-weight topsheet, lower thermal-bond energy. (p.146–147, p.174)

ACTION CARD 2 — Thin absorbent adlayer
- Situation: Need a thin, high-uptake layer between topsheet and core.
- How to apply: Evaluate Coform®-type co-forming (60–70% wood pulp + meltblown binder) or an SMS/SSMMS laminate for barrier (p.192–193).
- Expected effect: Thin yet high-liquid-containment structure; pulp gives uptake, meltblown gives integrity + particulate barrier. (p.193)

ACTION CARD 3 — Meltblown filter / mask media
- Situation: Designing a barrier layer for wipes or protective hygiene articles.
- How to apply: Use PP meltblown and apply electret charging; expect filtration efficiency to rise from 20–40% (uncharged) to 80–99% (charged) (p.182). Keep fibre diameter low via high air mass-flow rate (p.1551).
- Expected effect: Order-of-magnitude barrier improvement at the same basis weight. (p.182)
