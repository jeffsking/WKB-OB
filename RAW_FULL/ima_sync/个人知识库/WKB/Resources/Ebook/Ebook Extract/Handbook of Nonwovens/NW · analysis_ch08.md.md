# Chapter 8 — Nonwoven fabric finishing (including lamination)

## 1. Chapter Overview

Chapter 8 (A. Ahmed) addresses the finishing stage in which nonwoven substrates are transformed from commodity webs into value-added, fitness-for-purpose products. It opens by framing finishing as either wet (washing, impregnation, dyeing, coating) or dry (calendering, embossing, emerising, microcreeping), and stresses that there is no standard routine—process selection is end-use driven. The chapter then details wet operations (scouring, coloration by dope dye/pigment/dyestuff, printing), the application of chemical finishes (antistatic, antimicrobial, lubricant, flameproof, waterproof, softener, stiffener, UV), and the methods of applying them (padding, a large catalogue of coating techniques, and lamination). Mechanical finishing follows (splitting, perforating, drying/relaxation, compressive finishes including the Micrex® microcreeping, calendering, and surface treatments such as singeing/shearing/flocking/raising/polishing/softening). The chapter closes with "developing technologies" (plasma, microencapsulation, laser etching, biomimetic lotus-leaf finishes, electrochemical) and on-line fabric inspection. Throughout, hygiene and medical end-uses are repeatedly foregrounded—impregnation with lotions, coverstock softening, rewetters, hydrophilic PP via plasma, and perforation for vertical liquid transfer.

## 2. Key Viewpoints

### Viewpoint 1 — No standard finishing routine; end-use dictates the process

- Page: p.368
- Quote: > "Accordingly, there is no standard finishing routine for nonwoven fabrics; the selection of processes and the finishing effects introduced depend on the particular end-use application."
- Interpretation: Sets the chapter's organising principle—finishing is a design space, not a fixed sequence; essential framing for R&D targeting specific hygiene performances.
### Viewpoint 2 — Impregnation with lotions is a hygiene/medical core activity

- Page: p.368
- Quote: > "The impregnation of nonwoven fabrics with cosmetics, detergents, cleaning agents, medicaments and other lotions is a major activity, particularly in the hygiene and medical industries, and is frequently undertaken as part of converting prior to packaging."
- Interpretation: Positions nonwovens as delivery vehicles for actives—directly the basis of lotion wipes, medicated pads, and converting-line impregnation.
### Viewpoint 3 — Hydrophilic softeners act as rewetters that move liquid away from concentration zones

- Page: p.378
- Quote: > "Hydrophilic softeners have the additional effect of increasing wettability so that liquids can be distributed away from areas of high liquid concentration. Hydrophilic finishes are often known as rewetters."
- Interpretation: A concise statement of the rewetting mechanism central to topsheet/ADL fluid management—spreading liquid laterally to keep the skin surface dry.
### Viewpoint 4 — Lamination is fundamentally about engineering barrier/permeability properties

- Page: p.385
- Quote: > "The lamination of films to nonwovens, for example, is often intended to modify barrier properties (including liquids, particles and microbes and pathogens), permeability (gases, liquids and biological fluids), surface properties (abrasives, friction, appearance), dimensional stability and other mechanical properties."
- Interpretation: Defines backsheet/laminate design intent—fluid and microbe barrier with controlled breathability—the essence of diaper backsheet engineering.
### Viewpoint 5 — Plasma makes PP hydrophilic for hygiene without wet chemistry

- Page: p.399
- Quote: > "Among the various applications of plasma processes in nonwovens, increasing the hydrophilicity of synthetic polymer surfaces particularly with respect to polypropylene fabrics for hygiene applications is well known. … Oxygen plasma treated PP blood filters have been produced with much higher flow rates because of improved wetting properties."
- Interpretation: Offers a dry, low-effluent route to fix PP's poor wettability (cf. Ch7's 23 mN/m surface energy)—directly relevant to coverstock and filter wetting.
### Viewpoint 6 — Microcreping improves wipe cleaning efficiency

- Page: p.393
- Quote: > "The textural effects, and increased elasticity that are achieved have found applications in the wipes industry, particularly in relation to staple fibre hydroentangled fabrics. The surface texture after microcreping is claimed to improve the cleaning efficiency of wipes."
- Interpretation: Links a mechanical finish (3-D crepe) to a measurable wipe-performance benefit—useful for wipe substrate design.

## 4. Argumentation Process

The author proceeds: (1) classify finishing (wet/dry) and state end-use dependence; (2) treat wet processes (scour, colour, print) as web-preparation/appearance steps; (3) enumerate chemical finishes and their application (padding, coating family); (4) devote a major block to lamination as barrier/permeability engineering; (5) cover mechanical finishing (perforation, drying, compaction/Micrex, calendering, surface treatments); (6) spotlight emerging technologies (plasma, microencapsulation, biomimetic, electrochemical); (7) close with on-line inspection. The rhetorical strategy is comprehensive enumeration, repeatedly returning to hygiene/medical examples to justify each finish.

## 5. Role in the Book

Chapter 8 completes the bonding-and-finishing block begun in Ch6 (thermal) and Ch7 (chemical): it assumes bonded substrates and adds functionality/lamination. It is referenced by Ch9 (§9.3–9.11 testing of finished properties such as thickness, permeability, wetting) and complements Ch7 (coating/padding/print methods are shared). It is the chapter where "hygiene and medical" ceases to be a sub-application and becomes a recurring design driver.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths:
- Broad, well-organised catalogue of finishes and equipment—excellent reference for process selection.
- Strong, repeated hygiene anchoring (impregnation, rewetters, coverstock softening, PP plasma hydrophilicity, perforation for liquid transfer).
- Emerging-tech section (plasma, microencapsulation, biomimetic) is forward-looking and relevant to smart hygiene products.

⚠️ Limitations / contestable points:
- Coating/lamination subsections are largely descriptive; little quantitative guidance on bond-strength vs. breathability trade-offs for backsheets.
- Claims such as microcreping "improving the cleaning efficiency of wipes" (p.393) are presented as vendor claims ("is claimed") rather than validated data.
- Antimicrobial/biocidal finishes (p.377) predate later biocide-regulation tightening (e.g., silver/isothiazolinone restrictions).

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Wet vs dry finishing: (p.368) "finishing is classified as either wet finishing, for example washing, chemical impregnation, dyeing and coating or dry finishing, for example calendering, embossing, emerising and microcreping."
- Rewetter: (p.378) "Hydrophilic finishes are often known as rewetters" (increase wettability, distribute liquid away from high-concentration areas).
- Lamination: (p.385) joining fabrics/films to "modify barrier properties… permeability… surface properties… dimensional stability."
- Hot-melt coating adhesives: (p.383) "copolyamides, polyesters, copolyesters, polyurethanes, coPVCs and EVA (ethylene vinyl acetate) polymers."
- Plasma surface modification: (p.399) "increasing the hydrophilicity of synthetic polymer surfaces particularly with respect to polypropylene fabrics for hygiene applications."
- Microencapsulation: (p.399) delivery of "Perfumes, cosmetic lotions, thermoactive phase change materials (PCMs) and anti-microbial chemicals… in the form of microcapsules, usually in association with a binder resin."
- Micrex® microcreping: (p.393) 3-D crepe giving elasticity; Compaction C(%) = (L₀–L_c)/L₀ × 100.
## 8. Micro close-reading

Paragraph (p.399, plasma for hygiene):
> "Among the various applications of plasma processes in nonwovens, increasing the hydrophilicity of synthetic polymer surfaces particularly with respect to polypropylene fabrics for hygiene applications is well known. It has been shown that wetting out of nonwoven fabrics and the interaction of dyestuffs, pigments and chemical finishes can be improved by plasma treatments. Oxygen plasma treated PP blood filters have been produced with much higher flow rates because of improved wetting properties. Nonwovens containing synthetic fibres such as polyester and polypropylene can be treated to further increase hydrophobicity to obviate the need for a fluorochemical treatment. Environmental advantages are claimed due to low energy consumption, dry processing and no waste or effluent disposal problems."

- Sentence 1 ("increasing the hydrophilicity… polypropylene fabrics for hygiene applications is well known"): States the primary hygiene use—making PP wettable. Technique: topic sentence.
- Sentence 2 ("wetting out… dyestuffs, pigments and chemical finishes can be improved"): Extends benefit to downstream finishing. Logic: consequence.
- Sentence 3 ("Oxygen plasma treated PP blood filters… much higher flow rates because of improved wetting"): Concrete evidence case. Keyword: "improved wetting."
- Sentence 4 ("can be treated to further increase hydrophobicity to obviate the need for a fluorochemical treatment"): Counter-direction—plasma can also boost hydrophobicity, avoiding PFAS-type chemistry. Technique: dialectical contrast.
- Sentence 5 ("Environmental advantages… low energy, dry processing, no effluent"): Justifies via sustainability. Keyword: "no waste or effluent."
💡 Standout line: "increasing the hydrophilicity of synthetic polymer surfaces particularly with respect to polypropylene fabrics for hygiene applications is well known"—a one-line validation that dry plasma treatment is the established fix for PP's intrinsic poor wettability.

## 9. Concept deep-dive

Core concept 1 — Rewetting / hydrophilic finishing for fluid management.
- Definition (p.378): "Hydrophilic softeners have the additional effect of increasing wettability so that liquids can be distributed away from areas of high liquid concentration. Hydrophilic finishes are often known as rewetters."
- Operational definition? Yes—behaviour (liquid distribution away from concentration zones) is specified; mechanism is surface-energy lowering.
- Usage in chapter: appears under softeners (§8.3.1) and is the functional basis for coverstock/ADL performance.
- Relation: connects to Ch7's fibre surface-energy discussion (PP 23 mN/m) and Ch9's wettability/contact-angle section (§9.11.1).
- Academic origin: standard absorbent-hygiene finishing practice.

Core concept 2 — Lamination as barrier/permeability engineering (backsheet).
- Definition (p.385): film-to-nonwoven lamination to "modify barrier properties (including liquids, particles and microbes and pathogens), permeability (gases, liquids and biological fluids)…"
- Operational definition? Partly—wet vs dry lamination and adhesive-web/Tg mechanisms are described; quantitative breathability/barrier trade-off is not modelled.
- Usage: §8.4 covers calender, extrusion, flat-bed, flame, hot-melt spray lamination with PE/LDPE and TPU/coPET adhesives.
- Relation: to Ch6 (calender, through-air ovens) and Ch7 (coating/padding); underpins diaper backsheet and fascial films.

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- The chapter is encyclopaedic but thin on quantitative structure–property relationships for finished goods (e.g., how coating add-on maps to MVTR/barrier).
- Open question 1: Can plasma-induced PP hydrophilicity (p.399) be made durable through repeated liquid contact and conversion, or does it decay—and how does that compare to chemical rewetters (p.378)?
- Open question 2: For biomimetic lotus-leaf (Nanosphere®, p.399) self-cleaning finishes, what is the trade-off between hydrophobic self-cleaning and the wettability/absorption required in hygiene absorbents?
- Scope: microencapsulation (fragrance/PCM/antimicrobial) is described without release-kinetics data relevant to product shelf-life.
## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Relevance: ★★★★★ — Finishing/lamination is where hygiene products acquire their functional surfaces: coverstock softening & rewetting, backsheet barrier lamination, lotion impregnation, PP plasma treatment, and perforation for liquid transfer.

ACTION CARD 1 — Engineer a rewetting coverstock
- Situation: Topsheet must pull fluid away from skin (p.378).
- How to apply: Apply a hydrophilic softener/rewetter finish; combine with perforation (p.389) for vertical transfer; if substrate is PP, consider plasma hydrophilicity (p.399) to avoid heavy chemical loading.
- Expected effect: Faster liquid acquisition, drier skin contact, softer handle.

ACTION CARD 2 — Design a breathable backsheet laminate
- Situation: Need fluid/microbe barrier with vapour transmission (p.385).
- How to apply: Dry-laminate a PE/LDPE or TPU film to the nonwoven using a low-melt adhesive web (coPET/TPU, Tg-driven, p.386); choose extrusion lamination for robust bond; verify breathability via MVTR (Ch9 §9.10).
- Expected effect: Leak-proof yet breathable backsheet.

ACTION CARD 3 — Add actives via microencapsulation or lotion impregnation
- Situation: Product must deliver fragrance, skin-care or antimicrobial actives (p.368, p.399).
- How to apply: Impregnate during converting (p.368) or apply microcapsules with a binder resin (p.399); for sustained release use β-cyclodextrin attachment.
- Expected effect: Value-added wipe/pad with controlled active delivery.
