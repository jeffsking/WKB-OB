# Chapter 2 — Dry-laid web formation

## 1. Chapter Overview

Chapter 2 (author not named in the slice) is the handbook's deepest treatment of drylaid web formation — the textile-derived branch of nonwovens. It progresses from the physics of carding (worker/stripper/licker-in/doffer interactions, metallic wire geometry, aerodynamic control of fibre in the card), through automatic feed and weight-control systems, into cross-lapping and perpendicular-laid structures, and culminates in airlaying (aerodynamic web formation) and its raw-material science — wood pulp, man-made fibres, superabsorbents (SAP), and the bicomponent blends that define modern hygiene cores. For the hygiene R&D expert the chapter is pivotal: it is where fluff pulp, SAP and the "liquid acquisition layer for nappies" (p.78) are explicitly named, where the MD:CD isotropy advantage of airlaid vs. carded webs is stated (p.76), and where the PP/PE bicomponent 35/65 optimum (p.79) and the M&J 3 mm/4 mm fibre-length rule (p.79) are given. The carding and cross-lapping sections, though apparently distant from hygiene, govern the uniformity and bath-tub weight profile that later needlepunching/hydroentanglement must correct.

## 2. Key Viewpoints

### Viewpoint 1 — Airlaid webs are isotropic; carded webs are not

- Page: p.76
- Quote: > "A characteristic feature of airlaid webs is their isotropicity. In contrast to carded webs, MD:CD ratios approaching 1 may be obtained depending on fibre specifications and machine parameters."
- Interpretation: This is the single most important structural claim for absorbent hygiene. An isotropic (MD:CD ≈ 1) web gives uniform acquisition and wetting in every direction — critical for a diaper ADL. Carded webs are inherently MD-oriented and must be cross-lapped (and still show anisotropy) to approach isotropy.
### Viewpoint 2 — Hydrophobic synthetics preserve wet bulk; blends with pulp + SAP form the nappy acquisition layer

- Page: p.78
- Quote: > "The synthetic polymer fibres, specifically PET, PA, PP and PE are hydrophobic and are particularly effective in maintaining the bulkiness of airlaid fabrics in wet conditions. Such fibres are used in blends with wood pulp and sometimes SAP in liquid acquisition layers for nappies as well as other absorptive materials."
- Interpretation: The chapter names the modern ADL recipe: a hydrophobic synthetic (PET/PP/PE) blended with hydrophilic pulp and optionally SAP. The synthetics supply wet resiliency (bulk when saturated) while pulp/SAP supply capacity — a division of labour the core-formulator must respect.
### Viewpoint 3 — SAP (cross-linked hydrogel) immobilises fluid under compression

- Page: p.79
- Quote: > "The capacity of superabsorbents (cross-linked hydrogels) to absorb fluid is several times higher than wood pulp fibres and their function is to immobilise as much fluid as possible without releasing it even when the fabric structure is compressed."
- Interpretation: This is the functional definition of SAP in an airlaid context. The phrase "without releasing it even when compressed" is the design rationale for the core: under buttock pressure the gel must not give fluid back (no rewet). The cross-linked network is what delivers pressure-resistant retention — a polymer-chemistry point developed in the PoP cross-reference.
### Viewpoint 4 — Optimised fibre length and bicomponent ratio are quantifiable

- Page: p.79
- Quote: > "in the M&J system, selection of 3 mm fibre length optimises the production capacity whereas, 4 mm fibre length optimises the fabric tensile strength." … "The optimum proportion of PP and PE in the bicomponent fibre was claimed to be 35/65 for a concentric sheath-core bicomponent fibre…"
- Interpretation: Two concrete, transferable rules of thumb: (a) 3 mm → throughput, 4 mm → strength (machine-system-dependent); (b) PP/PE sheath-core BICO optimum 35/65. The 35/65 ratio places the low-melting PE sheath mass high enough to bond yet preserves PP-core integrity — directly usable in ADL/airlaid-core design.
### Viewpoint 5 — High-speed cards serve coverstock; web weight for hygiene is coefficient-limited

- Page: p.54 / p.56
- Quote: > "Commercial nonwoven carding installations vary from small low-production machines … to high-speed units for the manufacture of coverstock and high-volume roll goods for consumer products." (p.54)
- Interpretation: Confirms carding's role in spunlace/thermal coverstock. The companion formula (p.56) "L = K n^2.1" with K = 12–25 "for a card producing a lightweight web for hygiene operating with condenser rollers" is an operational design constraint: the lightest achievable carded web weight scales with fibre fineness (denier) and the condenser-roller coefficient K.
### Viewpoint 6 — Cross-lapping creates a 'bath-tub' profile that needlepunching amplifies

- Page: p.71
- Quote: > "heavy edges are an inherent quality issue in cross-lapping; the weight profile across the width of the batt therefore 'smiles' or is said to exhibit a 'bath-tub' profile." … "needlepunching can accentuate the heavy-edge effect, a reverse smile profile is frequently pre-programmed…"
- Interpretation: A process–quality chain: cross-lapping → heavy edges → needlepunch accentuates → solution is a pre-programmed reverse-smile. Shows why web-formation choices propagate into bonding and must be co-designed (relevant to needlepunched hygiene substrates and wipes, Ch.5).

## 4. Argumentation Process

The chapter builds bottom-up:
1. Carding primitives (wire geometry, roller interactions, aerodynamics) → uniform opened web.
2. Control systems (feed weight, micro-weigh, X-ray/weigh-belt) → uniformity at high speed.
3. Web geometry manipulation (cross-lapping, drafting, perpendicular-laid) → target MD/CD and loft.
4. Airlaying as an alternative drylaid route giving isotropy (p.76) and compatibility with pulp/SAP (p.78–79).
5. Synthesis: airlaid is the route of choice for high-basis-weight, isotropic, pulp/SAP-containing absorbent structures (nappy cores/ADLs).

The rhetorical move is machine-led: each process limitation (e.g. bath-tub profile, line speed) is met with a machine or control innovation, implying continuous incremental optimisation rather than paradigm shift.

## 5. Role in the Book

Ch.2 is the drylaid pillar of the three-pillar taxonomy from Ch.1. It pairs with Ch.3 (wetlaid) and Ch.4 (polymer-laid) and feeds Ch.5 (mechanical bonding — needlepunch/hydroentangle act on the webs formed here). Its airlaid/SAP/ADL content is the material bridge to the absorbent core, while carding/cross-lapping underpin the uniformity problems Ch.5 must solve.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths: Unusually concrete hygiene-relevant data (PP/PE 35/65, 3 vs 4 mm, K = 12–25, SAP "immobilise under compression"); clear machine taxonomy; good treatment of isotropy as a design variable.
⚠️ Limitations: Heavily machinery-vendor oriented (NSC, Fehrer, Laroche, Spinnbau) — process economics and polymer-science depth are thin; SAP is treated only as a blend additive, not via its gelation/cross-link kinetics (left to PoP cross-reference); the "bath-tub profile" is described but not quantified with a model.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Airlaid / aerodynamic web formation: (p.76) webs from individualised fibres carried in an airstream and randomly deposited; "isotropicity," MD:CD ≈ 1.
- Card clothing (metallic wire): (p.45) tooth profile (depth D, front/back angle, point density) controlling fibre capture/release.
- Cross-lapping: (p.66) layered side-to-side deposition giving CD-peaked orientation; source of bath-tub profile.
- Superabsorbent (SAP): (p.79) "cross-linked hydrogels" that absorb several× pulp capacity and immobilise fluid under compression.
- Bicomponent (BICO) PP/PE: (p.79) sheath-core, optimum 35/65, low-melt sheath bonds.
- Web-weight coefficient K: (p.56) L = K·n^2.1 for hygiene lightweight webs, K = 12–25.
## 8. Micro close-reading (ONE key paragraph)

"The synthetic polymer fibres, specifically PET, PA, PP and PE are hydrophobic and are particularly effective in maintaining the bulkiness of airlaid fabrics in wet conditions. Such fibres are used in blends with wood pulp and sometimes SAP in liquid acquisition layers for nappies as well as other absorptive materials. Synthetic fibres have a high wet strength as compared to viscose rayon (which decreases in strength when wet) and can markedly increase the durability and strength of the fabric in use." (p.78)

- Function: States the materials architecture of the nappy liquid-acquisition layer.
- Technique: Contrastive pairing — hydrophobic synthetic (bulk/wet-strength) vs. hydrophilic pulp/viscose (capacity, but weak when wet).
- Keywords: "hydrophobic," "maintaining the bulkiness … in wet conditions," "liquid acquisition layers for nappies," "high wet strength."
- Logic relation: The sentence resolves the apparent paradox that a hydrophobic fibre is desirable in an absorbent product: it is there for structure (bulk + wet strength), not for uptake.
- 💡 Standout line: "liquid acquisition layers for nappies" — the chapter explicitly locates the blend discussion inside the diaper, not generic wadding.
## 9. Concept deep-dive

Core concept: airlaid web isotropy and its raw-material compatibility.
- Definition (p.76): airlaid webs are "random-laid," MD:CD ≈ 1, with 3-D loft above ~50 g/m²; compatible with natural/synthetic/ceramic/metal fibres.
- Operational definition? Yes — measurable as MD:CD ratio; the chapter treats isotropy as a machine-parameter outcome (fibre specification + airflow dynamics, p.81–82).
- Usage in THIS chapter: Used to justify airlaying for absorbent hygiene vs. carding (anisotropic). Contrasted with carded webs' "preferential orientation in the machine direction" (p.82).
- Relation to other concepts: Isotropy + pulp/SAP compatibility (p.77–79) + bicomponent thermal bonding (p.79) together define the airlaid core; this is the materials pipeline that Ch.5 bonding (needlepunch/hydroentangle) later consolidates.
- Academic origin: Airlaying traced to Kellner (1892) and Rando/Curlator (1940s); pulp-airlay to Kroyer/M&J/Dan-Web (p.92–93).

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- SAP as black box. The chapter states what SAP does (immobilise under compression) but not how (gelation, cross-link density, Flory network). A hygiene formulator needs the latter — supplied via the PoP cross-reference (Ch.4/Ch.9 gelation, 86% ceiling).
- Machine-centrism. Optimisation is framed as choosing a vendor line (M&J vs Dan-Web, p.79) rather than a fundamental process limit; the 3 mm/4 mm and 35/65 rules are empirical, not derived.
- Open questions: (1) Can airlaid isotropy (MD:CD≈1) be reconciled with the need for machine-direction tensile in converted rolls? (2) How does SAP particle-size distribution interact with the hammermill/disc-refiner fibre individualisation step to avoid gel-blockage in the airstream?
## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Relevance rating: ★★★★★ — this chapter names the absorbent core and ADL directly (p.78 "liquid acquisition layers for nappies," p.79 SAP, p.79 PP/PE 35/65). It is the drylaid materials handbook for diaper/core R&D.

ACTION CARD 1 — ADL blend recipe.
- Situation: Designing or troubleshooting a nappy ADL.
- Apply: Per p.78, blend a hydrophobic synthetic (PET/PP/PE) with wood pulp (capacity) ± SAP; keep synthetics for wet bulk/resilience, pulp/SAP for uptake. Respect p.79 PP/PE BICO 35/65 for any thermally bonded component.
- Effect: Avoids the classic failure of an all-cellulosic ADL that collapses when wet.

ACTION CARD 2 — Airlaid vs carded for the core.
- Situation: Choosing web-formation route for an acquisition/distribution layer.
- Apply: Choose airlaid when omnidirectional isotropy matters (p.76, MD:CD≈1); choose carded+cross-lap when very high web weight/specific fibre types dominate, but anticipate the bath-tub profile (p.71) and pre-program a reverse-smile.
- Effect: Matches structure to in-use wetting geometry.

ACTION CARD 3 — Core-fibre length & throughput trade-off.
- Situation: Balancing line throughput against fabric strength on an M&J airlay.
- Apply: Use 3 mm for capacity, 4 mm for tensile (p.79); tune crimp to control throughput (p.78).
- Effect: Hits both OEE and strength targets without re-specifying the line.
