## Chapter 1 — Development of the nonwovens industry

### 概述

Chapter 1 (by A. Wilson) establishes the conceptual and industrial scaffolding of the nonwovens field before the technology chapters that follow. It opens by acknowledging that any definition of "nonwoven" carries exceptions, because the industry parasitically drew on textile, paper and polymer-processing know-how. The chapter then fixes the EDANA and INDA definitions, classifies nonwovens into drylaid, wetlaid and polymer-laid (spunmelt) families, surveys raw materials (with polypropylene dominant), maps global market structure and growth, and closes with the hygiene-disposables sector as the single largest end-use. The narrative logic runs from taxonomy → process families → materials → market → hygiene focus, deliberately positioning nonwovens as a sophisticated, high-margin, R&D-intensive industry rather than a cheap textile substitute. For a hygiene-materials R&D reader this is the map that locates every later chapter (drylaid, wetlaid, polymer-laid, mechanical bonding) inside the diaper/wipe value chain.

### 核心观点标题

Nonwovens resist a single clean definition

EDANA and INDA definitions

Hygiene is the dominant end-use

Three process families trace to three mother industries

Polymer-laid (spunmelt) is overwhelmingly hygiene-driven

- 

Man-made fibres, especially PP, dominate

### 在书中的作用

Chapter 1 is the orienting frame. It introduces the three web-formation families and the bonding concept that Ch2–Ch5 each develop technically, and it seeds the hygiene dominance motif that makes later polymer/fibre detail meaningful. It links forward to Ch2 (drylaid carding/airlaid), Ch3 (wetlaid), Ch4 (spunmelt), Ch5 (bonding), and to Ch1's raw-material table that reappears in process-specific fibre choices.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance: ★★★★★ (sets the entire hygiene value chain context).
ACTION CARD 1 — Material choice screening

Situation: selecting a nonwoven route for a new diaper component.

How to apply: use p.7 (spunmelt = 62% of hygiene components) and p.10 (PP 63%, PET 23%, viscose 8%) as the first-pass material/process priors.

Expected effect: faster route selection; avoids over-investing in a process family misaligned with hygiene volumes.
ACTION CARD 2 — Anisotropy spec

Situation: defining topsheet/ADL mechanical specs.

How to apply: use MD:CD ratio concept (p.8) to quantify isotropy needs; cardio web (Ch2 airlaid, p.76) approaches 1, spunbond is anisotropic.

Expected effect: align directional strength with machine vs cross direction of converting.
ACTION CARD 3 — Market-sizing sanity check

Situation: benchmarking a new hygiene nonwoven investment.

How to apply: cite p.4 (hygiene 33.1% of EU production) and p.15 (nappy maker spends 40% of revenue on raw materials) for top-down sizing.

- 

Expected effect: realistic CAPEX/raw-material cost framing.

## Chapter 2 — Dry-laid web formation

### 概述

Chapter 2 (author not named in the slice) is the handbook's deepest treatment of drylaid web formation — the textile-derived branch of nonwovens. It progresses from the physics of carding (worker/stripper/licker-in/doffer interactions, metallic wire geometry, aerodynamic control of fibre in the card), through automatic feed and weight-control systems, into cross-lapping and perpendicular-laid structures, and culminates in airlaying (aerodynamic web formation) and its raw-material science — wood pulp, man-made fibres, superabsorbents (SAP), and the bicomponent blends that define modern hygiene cores. For the hygiene R&D expert the chapter is pivotal: it is where fluff pulp, SAP and the "liquid acquisition layer for nappies" (p.78) are explicitly named, where the MD:CD isotropy advantage of airlaid vs. carded webs is stated (p.76), and where the PP/PE bicomponent 35/65 optimum (p.79) and the M&J 3 mm/4 mm fibre-length rule (p.79) are given. The carding and cross-lapping sections, though apparently distant from hygiene, govern the uniformity and bath-tub weight profile that later needlepunching/hydroentanglement must correct.

### 核心观点标题

Airlaid webs are isotropic; carded webs are not

Hydrophobic synthetics preserve wet bulk; blends with pulp + SAP form the nappy acquisition layer

SAP (cross-linked hydrogel) immobilises fluid under compression

Optimised fibre length and bicomponent ratio are quantifiable

High-speed cards serve coverstock; web weight for hygiene is coefficient-limited

- 

Cross-lapping creates a 'bath-tub' profile that needlepunching amplifies

### 在书中的作用

Ch.2 is the drylaid pillar of the three-pillar taxonomy from Ch.1. It pairs with Ch.3 (wetlaid) and Ch.4 (polymer-laid) and feeds Ch.5 (mechanical bonding — needlepunch/hydroentangle act on the webs formed here). Its airlaid/SAP/ADL content is the material bridge to the absorbent core, while carding/cross-lapping underpin the uniformity problems Ch.5 must solve.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance rating: ★★★★★ — this chapter names the absorbent core and ADL directly (p.78 "liquid acquisition layers for nappies," p.79 SAP, p.79 PP/PE 35/65). It is the drylaid materials handbook for diaper/core R&D.

ACTION CARD 1 — ADL blend recipe.

Situation: Designing or troubleshooting a nappy ADL.

Apply: Per p.78, blend a hydrophobic synthetic (PET/PP/PE) with wood pulp (capacity) ± SAP; keep synthetics for wet bulk/resilience, pulp/SAP for uptake. Respect p.79 PP/PE BICO 35/65 for any thermally bonded component.

Effect: Avoids the classic failure of an all-cellulosic ADL that collapses when wet.

ACTION CARD 2 — Airlaid vs carded for the core.

Situation: Choosing web-formation route for an acquisition/distribution layer.

Apply: Choose airlaid when omnidirectional isotropy matters (p.76, MD:CD≈1); choose carded+cross-lap when very high web weight/specific fibre types dominate, but anticipate the bath-tub profile (p.71) and pre-program a reverse-smile.

Effect: Matches structure to in-use wetting geometry.

ACTION CARD 3 — Core-fibre length & throughput trade-off.

Situation: Balancing line throughput against fabric strength on an M&J airlay.

Apply: Use 3 mm for capacity, 4 mm for tensile (p.79); tune crimp to control throughput (p.78).

- 

Effect: Hits both OEE and strength targets without re-specifying the line.

## Chapter 3 — Wet-laid web formation

### 概述

Chapter 3 (C. White) covers wetlaid nonwovens — the papermaking-derived branch of the three-pillar taxonomy (Ch.1). It opens with the standards definition (EN 29092 / ISO / EDANA) that separates wetlaid nonwovens from wetlaid papers, then develops the fluid-mechanics of slurry deposition: the Bernoulli balance giving slice velocity v = √(2gH) for an open headbox and v = √(2(ΔP/ρ + gH)) for a pressurised one, and the two deposition regimes (filtration vs. thickening). The chapter then surveys the fibre palette (cellulose, Tencel/Lyocell with fibrillatable wet strength, SWP synthetic wood pulp, deep-grooved PET, polyolefins) and the bonding toolbox (cellulose hydrogen-bonding, latex, thermal bicomponent PP/PE or PET/CoPET, PVA, hydroentanglement). Throughout, hygiene and wipes appear as named end-uses. For the hygiene R&D expert this chapter is the home of wetlaid cellulose and Tencel wipes, synthetic wood-pulp binders, and the deep-grooved PET fibre whose spontaneous fluid transport is "particularly interesting in webs used in absorbent products" (p.122).

### 核心观点标题

The wetlaid/paper legal boundary

Slice velocity is set by head (or head + pressure)

Two deposition regimes govern uniformity

Tencel/Lyocell brings wet strength + fibrillation

Deep-grooved PET spontaneously transports fluid in absorbent products

- 

Thermal bonding via bicomponent PP/PE or PET/CoPET, with no hole

### 在书中的作用

Ch.3 is the wetlaid pillar of the Ch.1 taxonomy, the papermaking sibling of Ch.2 (drylaid) and the slurry sibling of Ch.4 (extrusion). Its bonding section (latex/thermal/hydroentangle, p.135–137) hands directly to Ch.5 (hydroentanglement as a bonding method). The deep-grooved PET and SWP fibres reappear as hygiene-relevant structures later.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance rating: ★★★★★ — wetlaid cellulose/Tencel wipes, SWP binders, deep-grooved PET for absorbent products, and thermal-BICO bonding are all directly hygiene-relevant; the chapter names "Hygiene products," "Kitchen wipes," and "surgical clothing and drapes" (p.139–140).

ACTION CARD 1 — Wetlaid vs drylaid wipe fibre choice.

Situation: Specifying a cellulose/Tencel wetlaid or airlaid wipe.

Apply: Use Tencel/Lyocell for high wet strength + fibrillation + hydroentangle compatibility (p.121); use SWP as a thermally bondable synthetic wood-pulp analogue (p.122).

Effect: Strong, absorbent, latex-free wipes.

ACTION CARD 2 — Fibre-level fluid transport.

Situation: Designing an acquisition/distribution layer.

Apply: Consider deep-grooved PET (p.122) for spontaneous in-web fluid transport instead of relying solely on web porosity.

Effect: Faster, more uniform acquisition without extra chemical treatment.

ACTION CARD 3 — Latex-free bonding.

Situation: Regulatory/skin-contact pressure to remove latex.

Apply: Switch to bicomponent PP/PE or PET/CoPET thermal bonding (p.136), which leaves no hole and prevents wet-web collapse.

- 

Effect: Bonded web with intact bulk and no latex allergen.

## Chapter 4 — Polymer-laid web formation (spunbond & meltblown)

### 概述

Chapter 4 is the technical core of the book's "web formation" triptych (dry-laid, wet-laid, polymer-laid). It treats polymer-laid nonwovens — spunbond (spunlaid) and meltblown — as extensions of conventional melt-spinning in which extrusion, drawing, lay-down and bonding are integrated into one continuous line. The chapter opens (§4.2) with polymer science: polyolefin resin architecture (isotactic/syndiotactic/atactic PP; HDPE/LDPE/LLDPE; PET; PA; TPU; rayon; bicomponents), the resin properties that govern spinnability (melting point, thermal-bonding window, molecular-weight distribution, melt viscosity, cleanliness), and the single-site metallocene catalyst revolution. It then describes spunbond process engineering (§4.3–4.7), meltblown process engineering and its extensive parametric/modelling literature (§4.9–4.13), and finally composites and future trends (§4.14–4.15). The argument is cumulative: resin molecular structure sets the processing window, the processing window sets fibre diameter and web architecture, and fibre architecture sets the end-use performance (strength, barrier, filtration, absorption) that makes spunmelt the dominant family in hygiene and medical disposables.

### 核心观点标题

Isotactic PP stereochemistry and the MFR window define spinnability

The thermal-bonding window separates PP from PE and sets composite design

Narrow molecular-weight distribution is required to avoid draw resonance

Metallocene single-site catalysts widen the design space

Spunbond draw and electrostatic separation set web uniformity

- 

Spunbond/meltblown composites and Coform® are purpose-built for hygiene liquid containment

### 在书中的作用

Chapter 4 is the third web-formation chapter and the most polymer-science intensive. It complements Ch.2 (dry-laid, mechanical fibre handling) and Ch.3 (wet-laid, slurry/fluid mechanics) by focussing on melt as the carrier — i.e. the resin itself becomes the feedstock. It feeds directly into Ch.5 (mechanical bonding) and Ch.6+ (chemical/thermal bonding), since most spunmelt webs are self-bonded or thermal/calender bonded. Its hygiene framing (coverstock, SMS, Coform®) also answers the market data of Ch.1 (spunmelt ≈ 25% of nonwovens, ~62% of hygiene).

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance to diaper / wipe / absorbent-core R&D: ★★★★★ (spunmelt is the dominant hygiene nonwoven family; the chapter directly covers coverstock, SMS barrier, Coform® core, and meltblown wipes).

ACTION CARD 1 — Resin spec for a new topsheet line

Situation: Selecting PP resin for a fine-denier spunbond coverstock.

How to apply: Specify narrow-MWD, high-MFR (30–80) isotactic homopolymer; consider metallocene grade for lower bond temp + finer denier (p.147–148, p.174).

Expected effect: Fewer draw-resonance breaks, softer/lower-basis-weight topsheet, lower thermal-bond energy. (p.146–147, p.174)

ACTION CARD 2 — Thin absorbent adlayer

Situation: Need a thin, high-uptake layer between topsheet and core.

How to apply: Evaluate Coform®-type co-forming (60–70% wood pulp + meltblown binder) or an SMS/SSMMS laminate for barrier (p.192–193).

Expected effect: Thin yet high-liquid-containment structure; pulp gives uptake, meltblown gives integrity + particulate barrier. (p.193)

ACTION CARD 3 — Meltblown filter / mask media

Situation: Designing a barrier layer for wipes or protective hygiene articles.

How to apply: Use PP meltblown and apply electret charging; expect filtration efficiency to rise from 20–40% (uncharged) to 80–99% (charged) (p.182). Keep fibre diameter low via high air mass-flow rate (p.1551).

- 

Expected effect: Order-of-magnitude barrier improvement at the same basis weight. (p.182)

## Chapter 5 — Mechanical bonding (needlepunch, stitch-bonding & hydroentanglement)

### 概述

Chapter 5 completes the web-formation side of the book by treating mechanical bonding — the family of consolidation methods that interlock fibres without adhesive, thermal or chemical binders. It is organised into three technologies: stitch-bonding (Maliwatt/Malimo/Malipol, §5.2–5.8), needlepunching (§5.9–5.13) and hydroentanglement (§5.14–5.18). The first two are mature, capital-intensive routes aimed at durable/industrial goods (geotextiles, automotive, synthetic leather, filtration); the third — hydroentanglement or spunlacing — is the chapter's growth engine and the one most relevant to disposables. The narrative arc is consistent across all three: fibre/web geometry → bonding mechanism (needle barb, stitch yarn, or water jet) → structural property (entanglement, anisotropy, strength) → end-use. For a hygiene-materials reader the hydroentanglement section is the payload: it supplies the equations (specific energy K, specific-energy coefficient SE_c, jet impact force F ∝ p·d²), the fibre-selection rules (stiffness, wettability, dimensions), and the in-situ microfibre routes (splittable bicomponents, Lyocell fibrillation) that define modern wipes, topsheets and medical fabrics.

### 核心观点标题

Hydroentanglement is high-velocity water-jet mechanical bonding

Specific energy K and the specific-energy coefficient SE_c govern cost and strength

Jet impact force scales with pressure and nozzle area (F ∝ p·d²)

Fibre selection is governed by stiffness, wettability and dimensions

In-situ microfibres via splittable bicomponents and Lyocell fibrillation

- 

Needlepunching is barb-needle mechanical entanglement (the thick-web route)

### 在书中的作用

Chapter 5 is the "bonding" counterpart to the formation chapters (2–4) and the bridge to the chemical/thermal bonding chapters that follow. It shows that a web, once formed, can be consolidated mechanically — important because many hygiene webs (airlaid pulp + carded staple, spunlaid bicomponent) are finished by hydroentanglement or needlepunch rather than by binder. Its hydroentanglement section also answers Ch.1's hygiene-market data (wipes growth) and Ch.4's spunlaid-hydroentanglement route (Evolon®, Spunjet, p.2646–2668).

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance to diaper / wipe / absorbent-core R&D: ★★★★★ (hydroentanglement is the dominant binder-free route for wipes, medical fabrics and soft topsheets; needlepunch underpins thick absorbent/component webs).

ACTION CARD 1 — Binder-free wipe via hydroentanglement

Situation: Designing a soft, lint-free, lotion-compatible wipe.

How to apply: Use viscose or Lyocell (low wet modulus → easy entanglement at low K); if PP/PET, apply hydrophilic finish pre-bonding and re-apply post-bonding (p.2334–2337). Target SE_c minimisation, not just K.

Expected effect: Binder-free, high wet-strength wipe (Lyocell gives "excellent pattern definition", p.2392) at lower energy cost. (p.2303–2307, p.2390–2392)

ACTION CARD 2 — Soft microfibre topsheet / premium wipe

Situation: Need an ultra-soft surface from a segmented-pie BICO or Lyocell web.

How to apply: Run hydroentanglement at high impact force (F ∝ p·d², p.259); for BICO set pressure 50–100 bar minimum, up to 250–400 bar for heavy webs, with hollow-core/rectangular segments for efficiency (p.2442–2445). Control F/V_b to avoid perforation.

Expected effect: In-situ 0.05–0.3 denier microfibres → softer handle, no chemical split step. (p.268–269)

ACTION CARD 3 — Anisotropy control for cross-laid webs

Situation: Web must be isotropic for uniform convertibility.

How to apply: Use cross-laid precursor and hydroentangle so "MD/CD ratio of tensile strength … can sometimes approach unity" (p.261); prefer rotary/low-tension systems to avoid peel-induced MD orientation (p.2078–2081).

- 

Expected effect: Near-isotropic fabric → stable converting, even tensile in all directions. (p.2069–2071)

## Chapter 6 — Thermal bonding

### 概述

Chapter 6 (A. Pourmohammadi) presents thermal bonding as a family of web-consolidation processes in which a thermoplastic component of a web is heated to its softening/melting point and then cooled to form bonds—eliminating the need for an externally applied chemical adhesive in many cases. The chapter opens with the historical origin of the concept (Reed, 1942) and the early reliance on latex bonding, then explains why energy cost and environmental concerns drove a shift toward thermal methods (Fig. 6.1). It surveys the raw materials that make thermal bonding possible—conventional thermoplastic binder fibres, bicomponent (conjugate) fibres, and polymeric powder binders—and tabulates the glass-transition and melting temperatures of common binder polymers. The core of the chapter is a systematic treatment of the four principal heat-transfer routes: calender (conduction) bonding subdivided into area and point bonding, through-air/air-impingement (convection) bonding, and thermal-radiation/infra-red plus ultrasonic bonding. For calender bonding the author develops the process-parameter relationships (temperature, nip pressure, contact time, engraving) and the heat-transfer physics (conduction equation, deformation-induced heating, Clapeyron effect). The chapter closes with fabric-structure consequences (bond-point failure modes, fibre morphology effects) and a broad application survey that explicitly foregrounds hygiene disposables.

### 核心观点标题

Thermal bonding bonds via softening/melting a thermoplastic component, then cooling

Energy and environmental drivers displaced latex bonding

Bicomponent fibres enabled through-thickness bonding that chemical bonding could not achieve

Over-bonding causes "popping" failure; sintering not melting is the target

- 

Hygiene is a "major application" of thermally bonded fabrics

### 在书中的作用

Chapter 6 is the first of the bonding-mechanism trilogy (thermal → chemical → finishing) within the "web bonding and finishing" block of the Handbook. It pairs with Chapter 7 (chemical bonding) as the two dominant consolidation routes and is referenced by Chapter 8 (finishing/lamination, which uses calenders and through-air ovens) and Chapter 9 (characterisation, which treats thermally bonded bond structures in §9.2.4). It supplies the materials and process vocabulary (bond point, sintering, sheath-core, contact time) that later chapters assume.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance: ★★★★★ — Thermal bonding is the dominant consolidation route for diaper topsheet (coverstock), acquisition/distribution layers (ADL), and many wipes; the chapter's polymer tables and process windows are directly usable.

ACTION CARD 1 — Coverstock polymer/bond selection

Situation: Designing a 10–30 g/m² calender point-bonded PP coverstock (p.327).

How to apply: Use Table 6.1 (PP melt 160–175 °C, Tg –18 °C) and target surface sintering (p.309) at 10–40% bond area (p.307); if a softer handle is needed, blend in sheath-core PP/PE bicomponent (sheath 125–135 °C) so the core survives (p.305).

Expected effect: Soft, breathable, permeable coverstock without film-forming over-bonding/popping.

ACTION CARD 2 — Wipes basis-weight & method choice

Situation: Selecting a thermal route for airlaid/through-air wipes (p.327).

How to apply: Choose through-air bonding (25–150 g/m² airlaid; ~100–250 g/m² carded) to maximise bulk; reserve calender bonding for spunbond/meltblown medical gowns/drapes.

Expected effect: Bulkier, more absorbent wipes; calender for strength-critical medical disposables.

ACTION CARD 3 — Avoid over-bonding in converted parts

Situation: Coverstock fails at bond points during conversion/stretch.

How to apply: Keep inter-bond fibres below melt (differential roller temps, p.309); monitor for "popping" (p.310) and reduce temperature/pressure if present.

- 

Expected effect: Higher tensile conversion yield, retained softness.

## Chapter 7 — Chemical bonding

### 概述

Chapter 7 (R.A. Chapman) systematically treats chemical (latex/binder) bonding—the dominant alternative to thermal consolidation and the route of choice when a broad property palette (softness, waterproofness, breathability, flame resistance, recyclability) must be engineered into the fabric. It begins by defining the binder and its multiple roles, then surveys the polymer chemistry of binders: vinyl, acrylic, butadiene (SBR/NBR/natural rubber), and specialty systems (PU, phenolic, epoxy). A rigorous subsection explains emulsion polymerisation, the Fox equation for copolymer Tg, minimum film-forming temperature (MFFT), and the functionality of coagulation/migration and crosslinking. The chapter then analyses the mechanism of chemical bonding—wetting, binder cohesion, adhesion, distribution, and binder:fibre ratio—including the transition to a fibre-reinforced-polymer composite at high add-on. Application methods (saturation, foam, spray, print, coating, solution) and dryers (convection/through-air, conduction, radiation) follow. The chapter closes with explicit hygiene/medical and wipes applications, including flushable-wipe binder chemistry—making it one of the most directly applicable chapters for the target expert.

### 核心观点标题

A binder does far more than hold fibres together

Latices (emulsion polymers) are the workhorse binder

Copolymer Tg (Fox equation) governs handle and wet stability

Binder migration during drying is structural and can be beneficial or detrimental

Wettability is governed by fibre surface energy; PP is hard to bond

- 

Hygiene/wipes applications are explicit and chemistry-specific

### 在书中的作用

Chapter 7 is the counterpart to Chapter 6 (thermal bonding) and the bridge to Chapter 8 (finishing/lamination, which reuses coating/padding/print methods and often applies binder+active chemistries). Chapter 9 (§9.2.5) characterises chemically bonded structures and §7.6 hygiene chemistry is the substantive content behind "hygiene and medical" testing in Table 9.1. Together 6–8 form the consolidation-and-finishing spine of the Handbook.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance: ★★★★★ — Chemical bonding supplies the binder chemistry for wipes, airlaid absorbent cores, incontinence products, and flushable wipes; §7.6 is essentially a hygiene-formulation guide.

ACTION CARD 1 — Formulate a soft, wet-stable wipe binder

Situation: Designing a disposable wipe needing wet strength + softness (p.335, p.338, p.361).

How to apply: Use viscose rayon + small polyester for wet strength; choose a soft acrylic or EVA binder (low-Tg monomers via Fox eq., p.334); for crosslinkable water resistance use carboxylated/acrylic systems; nitrile rubber (Tg < –18 °C) for cold-flexibility.

Expected effect: Wipe with high wet strength, soft handle, and controlled hydrophobicity.

ACTION CARD 2 — Prevent binder migration in absorbent cores

Situation: Airlaid/carded incontinence web shows surface-hardening or delamination (p.343).

How to apply: Use heat-sensitisable latex (coagulates on fibres) or thickeners; manage dryer temperature profile so coagulation T is ≥5 °C below wet-bulb T (Troesch & Hoffman, p.343).

Expected effect: Uniform through-thickness binder, less delamination, softer core.

ACTION CARD 3 — Select flushable-wipe binder chemistry

Situation: Product must be dispersible in water yet strong in use (p.361).

How to apply: Choose soluble polymer binders—starches, CMC, PEO, PVA, polyacrylates, PLA+superabsorbent blends, or bicomponent water-soluble sheath fibres; avoid permanent crosslinked SBR.

- 

Expected effect: In-use strength with sewer/dispersal compatibility.

## Chapter 8 — Nonwoven fabric finishing (including lamination)

### 概述

Chapter 8 (A. Ahmed) addresses the finishing stage in which nonwoven substrates are transformed from commodity webs into value-added, fitness-for-purpose products. It opens by framing finishing as either wet (washing, impregnation, dyeing, coating) or dry (calendering, embossing, emerising, microcreping), and stresses that there is no standard routine—process selection is end-use driven. The chapter then details wet operations (scouring, coloration by dope dye/pigment/dyestuff, printing), the application of chemical finishes (antistatic, antimicrobial, lubricant, flameproof, waterproof, softener, stiffener, UV), and the methods of applying them (padding, a large catalogue of coating techniques, and lamination). Mechanical finishing follows (splitting, perforating, drying/relaxation, compressive finishes including the Micrex® microcreping, calendering, and surface treatments such as singeing/shearing/flocking/raising/polishing/softening). The chapter closes with "developing technologies" (plasma, microencapsulation, laser etching, biomimetic lotus-leaf finishes, electrochemical) and on-line fabric inspection. Throughout, hygiene and medical end-uses are repeatedly foregrounded—impregnation with lotions, coverstock softening, rewetters, hydrophilic PP via plasma, and perforation for vertical liquid transfer.

### 核心观点标题

No standard finishing routine; end-use dictates the process

Impregnation with lotions is a hygiene/medical core activity

Hydrophilic softeners act as rewetters that move liquid away from concentration zones

Lamination is fundamentally about engineering barrier/permeability properties

Plasma makes PP hydrophilic for hygiene without wet chemistry

- 

Microcreping improves wipe cleaning efficiency

### 在书中的作用

Chapter 8 completes the bonding-and-finishing block begun in Ch6 (thermal) and Ch7 (chemical): it assumes bonded substrates and adds functionality/lamination. It is referenced by Ch9 (§9.3–9.11 testing of finished properties such as thickness, permeability, wetting) and complements Ch7 (coating/padding/print methods are shared). It is the chapter where "hygiene and medical" ceases to be a sub-application and becomes a recurring design driver.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance: ★★★★★ — Finishing/lamination is where hygiene products acquire their functional surfaces: coverstock softening & rewetting, backsheet barrier lamination, lotion impregnation, PP plasma treatment, and perforation for liquid transfer.

ACTION CARD 1 — Engineer a rewetting coverstock

Situation: Topsheet must pull fluid away from skin (p.378).

How to apply: Apply a hydrophilic softener/rewetter finish; combine with perforation (p.389) for vertical transfer; if substrate is PP, consider plasma hydrophilicity (p.399) to avoid heavy chemical loading.

Expected effect: Faster liquid acquisition, drier skin contact, softer handle.

ACTION CARD 2 — Design a breathable backsheet laminate

Situation: Need fluid/microbe barrier with vapour transmission (p.385).

How to apply: Dry-laminate a PE/LDPE or TPU film to the nonwoven using a low-melt adhesive web (coPET/TPU, Tg-driven, p.386); choose extrusion lamination for robust bond; verify breathability via MVTR (Ch9 §9.10).

Expected effect: Leak-proof yet breathable backsheet.

ACTION CARD 3 — Add actives via microencapsulation or lotion impregnation

Situation: Product must deliver fragrance, skin-care or antimicrobial actives (p.368, p.399).

How to apply: Impregnate during converting (p.368) or apply microcapsules with a binder resin (p.399); for sustained release use β-cyclodextrin attachment.

- 

Expected effect: Value-added wipe/pad with controlled active delivery.

## Chapter 9 — Characterisation, testing and modelling of nonwoven fabrics

### 概述

Chapter 9 (N. Mao & S.J. Russell, with §9.22 by B. Pourdeyhimi) is the measurement-and-modelling capstone of the Handbook. It asserts that nonwoven performance is governed jointly by composition (fibre, binder, filler, finish) and structure, then organises the field into: (1) characterisation of bond structures for each bonding route (needlepunch, hydroentangle, stitch-bond, thermal, chemical); (2) dimensional parameters (weight, thickness, density, uniformity, fibre orientation distribution/FOD, porosity and pore size); (3) a large compendium of test standards (general, medical, air-filtration, and—critically—absorbent hygiene products, lamination strength, wiping efficiency); (4) measurement techniques for basic parameters, FOD (image analysis), pore size (sieving, bubble point, porometry, mercury intrusion, BET); (5) mechanical (tensile), gas/liquid permeability (D'Arcy), water-vapour transmission, and wetting/liquid-absorption testing (wettability/contact angle, wicking vs. demand absorbency, strike time, GATS, drainage); and (6) a substantial modelling section translating structure into properties (thermal conductivity, pore-size, tensile, bending, specific permeability, absorbency, capillary wicking via Lucas–Washburn and the directional Mao–Russell model, thermal resistance, acoustic impedance, filtration). The chapter is the quantitative backbone that lets the bonding/finishing chapters (6–8) be verified and optimised.

### 在书中的作用

Chapter 9 is the evaluative counterpart to the making chapters (6–8). It is where claims in those chapters become measurable: thermal-bond softness (Ch6) is tested by thickness/tensile/Mao–Russell models; chemical-binder migration (Ch7) by resin-distribution tests (WSP 150.1, §9.5.1); finishing barrier/rewet (Ch8) by permeability, MVTR, contact-angle and wiping-efficiency standards. It also introduces the FOD and pore-size concepts that later sections (and Pourdeyhimi's §9.22 image analysis) build upon. The chapter closes the Handbook's technical core before applications/material-specific chapters.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance: ★★★★★ — This chapter is the measurement and prediction toolkit for every hygiene metric: strike time, wicking rate, demand absorbency, re-wet, barrier, MVTR, lamination strength, and wiping efficiency; plus models to design ADL and core uptake.

ACTION CARD 1 — Specify and verify coverstock acquisition metrics

Situation: Defining incoming-spec for a topsheet/ADL (p.402 fluid-handling list; p.423 wiping efficiency ASTM D6650/D6702).

How to apply: Use §9.11.4 wicking-rate tests (upward strip; GATS/MLDA; BS EN ISO 9073-6, WSP 10.1) for in-plane spread and §9.11 demand-absorbency for through-plane uptake; track strike time and re-wet.

Expected effect: Objective, standards-based acquisition/rewet spec instead of ad-hoc feel tests.

ACTION CARD 2 — Model ADL vs. core wicking with Lucas–Washburn / Mao–Russell

Situation: Optimising fluid distribution in the absorbent assembly (p.442, p.453–454).

How to apply: Treat ADL as in-plane wicking (Lucas–Washburn, eq. 9.43–9.48) and core uptake as through-plane demand absorbency; use the directional Mao–Russell model (9.50–9.54) for anisotropic sheets; input pore radius/contact angle from §9.7 and §9.11.1.

Expected effect: Predict wicking distance vs. time; reduce wet-back by tuning pore structure and hydrophilicity.

ACTION CARD 3 — Characterise barrier/breathability of backsheet laminate

Situation: Validating a film–nonwoven backsheet (cf. Ch8 lamination, p.385).

How to apply: Measure specific permeability by D'Arcy (§9.16, eq. 9.35–9.36), MVTR (§9.10), and pore-throat via bubble point (§9.7.5, Laplace eq. 9.9); use polymer thermal conductivity (Table 9.7: PP 0.12, PE 0.34) if thermal modelling needed.

Expected effect: Quantified leak-barrier vs. breathability trade-off for compliant backsheet.
