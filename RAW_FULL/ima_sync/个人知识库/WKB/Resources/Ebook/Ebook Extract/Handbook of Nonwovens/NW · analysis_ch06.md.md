# Chapter 6 — Thermal bonding

## 1. Chapter Overview

Chapter 6 (A. Pourmohammadi) presents thermal bonding as a family of web-consolidation processes in which a thermoplastic component of a web is heated to its softening/melting point and then cooled to form bonds—eliminating the need for an externally applied chemical adhesive in many cases. The chapter opens with the historical origin of the concept (Reed, 1942) and the early reliance on latex bonding, then explains why energy cost and environmental concerns drove a shift toward thermal methods (Fig. 6.1). It surveys the raw materials that make thermal bonding possible—conventional thermoplastic binder fibres, bicomponent (conjugate) fibres, and polymeric powder binders—and tabulates the glass-transition and melting temperatures of common binder polymers. The core of the chapter is a systematic treatment of the four principal heat-transfer routes: calender (conduction) bonding subdivided into area and point bonding, through-air/air-impingement (convection) bonding, and thermal-radiation/infra-red plus ultrasonic bonding. For calender bonding the author develops the process-parameter relationships (temperature, nip pressure, contact time, engraving) and the heat-transfer physics (conduction equation, deformation-induced heating, Clapeyron effect). The chapter closes with fabric-structure consequences (bond-point failure modes, fibre morphology effects) and a broad application survey that explicitly foregrounds hygiene disposables.

## 2. Key Viewpoints

### Viewpoint 1 — Thermal bonding bonds via softening/melting a thermoplastic component, then cooling

- Page: p.298
- Quote: > "The basic concept of thermal bonding was introduced by Reed in 1942. He described a process in which a web consisting of thermoplastic and non-thermoplastic fibres was made and then heated to the melting or softening temperature of the constituent thermoplastic fibres followed by cooling to solidify the bonding area."
- Interpretation: This is the chapter's operative definition. For hygiene R&D it establishes that the bond is intrinsic to the fibre (or powder/film) polymer, not an added adhesive—central to topsheet and ADL design.
### Viewpoint 2 — Energy and environmental drivers displaced latex bonding

- Page: p.298
- Quote: > "The rising cost of energy and greater awareness of the environmental impact of latex bonding led to a change in direction. A comparison of energy consumption by various web-bonding processes is given in Fig. 6.1 which shows a considerable energy saving for the thermal bonding process."
- Interpretation: The author frames thermal bonding as a sustainability- and cost-led substitution. Relevant to the diaper industry's drive to reduce binder-chemical loading and drying energy.
### Viewpoint 3 — Bicomponent fibres enabled through-thickness bonding that chemical bonding could not achieve

- Page: p.302
- Quote: > "Use of bicomponent fibres accelerated dramatically in the early 1990s partly because of the need to uniformly bond the entire thickness of nonwoven fabrics, which in heavy weight per unit area structures could not be satisfactorily accomplished by chemical bonding."
- Interpretation: Explains why sheath-core PP/PE and CoPET/PET bicomponents dominate hygiene webs: a low-melt sheath fuses while the core preserves tensile integrity—the key to soft, strong coverstock.
### Viewpoint 4 — Over-bonding causes "popping" failure; sintering not melting is the target

- Page: p.309–310
- Quote: > "In bonding areas where fibres are flattened but not completely melted to a film-like structure, fabrics with high strength are produced. Therefore it is important that the temperature of the roller surfaces is adjusted in a way that the so-called sintering of the fibre surfaces can be achieved while avoiding complete fibre melting and film formation."
- Quote (cont.): > "Over-bonding of this kind leads to 'popping' of the structure under tensile load as the fabric fails at the point bond locations."
- Interpretation: A critical process window. Hygiene coverstock must stay soft/permeable, so operators target surface sintering; excessive temperature embrittles and causes bond-point popping—a direct failure mode in converted products.
### Viewpoint 5 — Hygiene is a "major application" of thermally bonded fabrics

- Page: p.327
- Quote: > "A major application is in hygiene, for example PP coverstock found in sanitary and incontinence products of 10–30 g/m2, based on calender point bonded dry-laid or spunbond webs, the former using bicomponent fibres. Other disposables include wipes produced from airlaid short fibres, which are through-air bonded to make products in the 25–150 g/m2 weight range and through-air bonded carded wipes products of about 100–250 g/m2."
- Interpretation: The chapter explicitly maps its technology onto the diaper/wipe value chain (coverstock, incontinence, wipes), confirming high relevance to the target expert.

## 4. Argumentation Process

The author builds the case as: (1) define thermal bonding and its historical motivation (energy/environment vs. latex); (2) establish the material precondition—thermoplastic binder fibres, bicomponents, powders, with quantitative Tg/Tm tables; (3) classify heat-transfer mechanisms (conduction/calender, convection/through-air, radiation/IR, mechanical/ultrasonic) and for each give apparatus, advantages, drawbacks; (4) deepen calender bonding with the physics of temperature, pressure, contact time, and engraving (including DIH and Clapeyron effects); (5) connect process–structure–property via bond-point failure modes and fibre-morphology effects; (6) conclude with applications, foregrounding hygiene. The rhetorical move is inductive-from-physics-to-application, repeatedly returning to the trade-off between bond strength and softness/permeability.

## 5. Role in the Book

Chapter 6 is the first of the bonding-mechanism trilogy (thermal → chemical → finishing) within the "web bonding and finishing" block of the Handbook. It pairs with Chapter 7 (chemical bonding) as the two dominant consolidation routes and is referenced by Chapter 8 (finishing/lamination, which uses calenders and through-air ovens) and Chapter 9 (characterisation, which treats thermally bonded bond structures in §9.2.4). It supplies the materials and process vocabulary (bond point, sintering, sheath-core, contact time) that later chapters assume.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths:
- Quantitative anchor via thermal-transition tables (Table 6.1) lets the reader select polymers against bonding temperature windows—directly actionable.
- Clear mechanism taxonomy (conduction/convection/radiation/mechanical) aids process selection.
- Explicit hygiene application mapping (p.327) ties theory to the diaper/wipe sector.

⚠️ Limitations / contestable points:
- "Light-weight fabrics are calendered at very short contact times (about 1/1000 sec) compared to heavier web weights (about 0.7 sec)" (p.312) is presented as typical; real coverstock lines vary and the figure is illustrative rather than bounded by a cited source.
- Through-air and ultrasonics sections are comparatively thin on quantitative process windows versus calender bonding.
- The chapter predates later developments in extensible/elastic thermal-point bonding for stretchable hygiene films and elastomeric bicomponents.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Thermal bonding: (p.298) "a web consisting of thermoplastic and non-thermoplastic fibres was made and then heated to the melting or softening temperature of the constituent thermoplastic fibres followed by cooling to solidify the bonding area."
- Bicomponent (conjugate) fibre: (p.303) "commonly classified by the structure of their cross-section as side-by-side, sheath-core, island in the sea or segmented pie."
- Point bonding: (p.307) "The degree of bonding in the fabric depends on the frequency, the size and the pattern of the bond points and it is normal to have a bonded area of 10–40%."
- Sintering (vs. melting): (p.309) achieving fibre-surface fusion "while avoiding complete fibre melting and film formation."
- Deformation-induced heating (DIH): (p.311) compressive nip forces raise polymer temperature "as much as 30–35 °C in the case of polypropylene fibres."
- Clapeyron effect: (p.311) "Polymer molecules subjected to compression require more thermal energy to melt… pressure could increase the melting point temperature of polypropylene by 38K/Kbar."
## 8. Micro close-reading

Paragraph (p.309–310, calender bonding temperature window):
> "Bonding temperature determines the structure of fibres at the bonding points. In bonding areas where fibres are flattened but not completely melted to a film-like structure, fabrics with high strength are produced. Therefore it is important that the temperature of the roller surfaces is adjusted in a way that the so-called sintering of the fibre surfaces can be achieved while avoiding complete fibre melting and film formation. Fabric softness can also be maintained by keeping the fibres between bonding points at a temperature below the melting point. This may be accomplished by using different temperatures for the smooth and engraved rollers. In this way fibre characteristics in the area between the raised points can be maintained. … Over-bonding of this kind leads to 'popping' of the structure under tensile load as the fabric fails at the point bond locations."

- Sentence 1 ("Bonding temperature determines…"): States the dependent variable—bond-point fibre structure is set by roller temperature. Technique: causal framing.
- Sentence 2 ("In bonding areas where fibres are flattened…"): Operational definition of the desirable bond—surface fusion without film formation. Keyword: "flattened but not completely melted."
- Sentence 3 ("Therefore it is important… sintering… while avoiding complete fibre melting"): Prescribes the target state (sintering) and the failure to avoid (film). Logic: conclusion from sentence 2.
- Sentence 4–5 ("Fabric softness can also be maintained… different temperatures for the smooth and engraved rollers"): Offers a control lever—differential roller temperatures protect inter-bond fibres. Technique: process prescription.
- Sentence 6 ("In this way fibre characteristics… between the raised points can be maintained"): Restates the benefit.
- Final sentence ("Over-bonding… leads to 'popping'…"): Names the penalty of exceeding the window. Keyword: "popping"—a vivid, specific failure mode.
💡 Standout line: "the so-called sintering of the fibre surfaces can be achieved while avoiding complete fibre melting and film formation"—this single clause is the entire process-control philosophy for soft, strong point-bonded coverstock.

## 9. Concept deep-dive

Core concept 1 — Sheath-core bicomponent fibre as a self-limiting thermal binder.
- Definition (p.303–305): "In sheath-core bicomponent fibres one of the components (the core) is fully surrounded by another component (the sheath)… If high fabric strength is required, the concentric form is selected whereas if bulk is required, the eccentric type is employed." Core melts at 250–265 °C (PET) or 175 °C (PP); sheath at 110–130 °C (CoPET/PE).
- Operational definition? Yes—concrete melt-temperature windows and blend ratios (10–50%) are given.
- Usage in this chapter: introduced as the solution to through-thickness bonding (p.302), then detailed as the mechanism behind hygiene coverstock (p.327) and wipes.
- Relation to other concepts: bridges polymer Tg/Tm (Table 6.1) to the calender process window (§6.4.3) and to application weight ranges.
- Academic origin: commercialised via Neumag (1986) and early DuPont Monsanto work; see §6.3.3.

Core concept 2 — Process window of calender point bonding (temperature/pressure/contact time).
- Definition (p.307, 309–312): bonded area 10–40%; bond strength peaks then declines with temperature; contact time milliseconds for coverstock.
- Operational definition? Yes—quantified with example (D=450 mm, T_in=1.8 mm, T_out=0.2 mm → contact time 6–18 ms; coverstock ~0.001 s).
- Usage: pervades §6.4 and §6.7 (fabric structure).
- Relation: links conduction heat transfer (eq. 6.1), DIH, Clapeyron effect, and engraving to final mechanical properties.

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- The chapter is descriptive/process-oriented; it lacks a unified predictive model linking polymer morphology → bond-point microstructure →宏观 mechanical properties (the author concedes: "a clear understanding of the properties of point bonded fabrics has yet to be established," p.326).
- Open question 1: How do elastomeric/extensible bicomponent sheaths behave in point bonding when stretch (not just softness) is required for form-fitting hygiene products?
- Open question 2: Can the "popping" failure under tensile load be predicted from bond-point morphology and bridging-fibre stress–strain (cf. Mi et al. model) for next-generation low-basis-weight coverstocks?
- Scope limitation: powder-bonding and ultrasonic sections are thinner; no discussion of recyclability of mixed-polymer thermally bonded laminates.
## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Relevance: ★★★★★ — Thermal bonding is the dominant consolidation route for diaper topsheet (coverstock), acquisition/distribution layers (ADL), and many wipes; the chapter's polymer tables and process windows are directly usable.

ACTION CARD 1 — Coverstock polymer/bond selection
- Situation: Designing a 10–30 g/m² calender point-bonded PP coverstock (p.327).
- How to apply: Use Table 6.1 (PP melt 160–175 °C, Tg –18 °C) and target surface sintering (p.309) at 10–40% bond area (p.307); if a softer handle is needed, blend in sheath-core PP/PE bicomponent (sheath 125–135 °C) so the core survives (p.305).
- Expected effect: Soft, breathable, permeable coverstock without film-forming over-bonding/popping.

ACTION CARD 2 — Wipes basis-weight & method choice
- Situation: Selecting a thermal route for airlaid/through-air wipes (p.327).
- How to apply: Choose through-air bonding (25–150 g/m² airlaid; ~100–250 g/m² carded) to maximise bulk; reserve calender bonding for spunbond/meltblown medical gowns/drapes.
- Expected effect: Bulkier, more absorbent wipes; calender for strength-critical medical disposables.

ACTION CARD 3 — Avoid over-bonding in converted parts
- Situation: Coverstock fails at bond points during conversion/stretch.
- How to apply: Keep inter-bond fibres below melt (differential roller temps, p.309); monitor for "popping" (p.310) and reduce temperature/pressure if present.
- Expected effect: Higher tensile conversion yield, retained softness.
