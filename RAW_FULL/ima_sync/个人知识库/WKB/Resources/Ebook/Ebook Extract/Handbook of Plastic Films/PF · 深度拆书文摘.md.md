## Chapter 1 — Technology of Polyolefin Film Production

### 概述

Chapter 1 surveys the polyolefin family used in film manufacture and the technologies that convert these semicrystalline polymers into thin, high-performance films. The author (Robert Shanks) opens with a film definition grounded in dimensional form (large surface-area-to-volume ratio) and the bulk/barrier/mechanical properties required of films (p.5). The chapter then proceeds systematically: it catalogues the polyolefin types (LDPE, HDPE, LLDPE, VLDPE/ULDPE, iPP, PP copolymers) and their structure–property–rheology relationships (§1.2–1.4); describes the two dominant conversion routes — blown (tubular) and cast (flat-die, chilled-roll) extrusion, plus orientation and coextrusion/lamination (§1.5–1.7); treats surface properties and surface modification (gloss, haze, surface energy, slip, blocking, corona, antiblock, slip agents, lubricants, antistatics) (§1.8–1.9); details internal additives (antioxidants, UV stabilisers) (§1.10); and closes with mechanical/thermal/IR characterisation and applications (packaging, laminates, heat sealing, agriculture) (§1.11–1.15). The logical thread is: molecular architecture (branching, crystallinity, tacticity) → melt rheology → processing stability and orientation → surface and additive engineering → final functional film. The chapter is a review of established industrial practice rather than new research, providing the foundational vocabulary for the later, more specialised chapters on PE (Ch2), PP durability (Ch3), and additives (Ch4).

### 核心观点标题

A film is defined by its dimensional form and barrier function

Long-chain branching is what makes LDPE processable in blown film

Catalyst control of isotacticity determines PP film quality

Surface inertness of polyolefins must be modified by oxidation

Erucamide is the most effective polyolefin slip agent

- 

Antioxidants act by radical/peroxide removal during processing and life

### 在书中的作用

Chapter 1 is the foundational overview. It defines the materials (polyolefins) and processes (blown/cast extrusion, orientation, surface treatment) that the rest of the handbook elaborates: Ch2 (PE processing specifics), Ch3 (PP durability and UV degradation), Ch4 (additive solubility in these same polyolefins), and Ch5–Ch7 (degradation, flame retardancy, pollutant interactions). It supplies the shared vocabulary (melt strength, shear-thinning, haze, blocking, corona, antiblock) used throughout.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance: ★★★★☆ (4/5) — Polyolefin films (esp. cast PP, PE) are directly used as diaper backsheets, hygiene wrappers, and component layers; surface treatment, slip/antiblock, and antioxidants are daily formulation concerns.

ACTION CARD 1 — Backsheet film selection (situation → apply → effect): Situation: choosing a diaper/chux backsheet film. → Apply p.8/Table 1.1 and p.15: HDPE/LLDPE blown film for moisture barrier, cast PP for gloss/stiffness; match MWD/MFI to extruder. → Effect: balance of moisture barrier (HDPE, p.35 "most resistant to water vapour") and processability.

ACTION CARD 2 — Solve blocking & slip (situation → apply → effect): Situation: rolls block or web jams on converting line. → Apply p.21–23: add silica/talc antiblock (p.22) and erucamide slip agent (p.23 "most effective slip agent for polyolefins"); control chill-roll temp. → Effect: stable roll winding and web handling.

ACTION CARD 3 — Printability/lamination (situation → apply → effect): Situation: printed or laminated hygiene film. → Apply p.21–22: corona discharge to raise surface energy via polar groups, or use EVA/MAH-grafted tie layers (p.21, p.37). → Effect: ink/adhesive wetting and bond strength.

Note: Lower relevance only for absorbent-core chemistry (SAP) which is non-polyolefin; otherwise highly relevant.

## Chapter 2 — Processing of Polyethylene Films

### 概述

Chapter 2 (Amin Al-Robaidi) drills into polyethylene film processing, complementing Ch1's broad polyolefin survey with quantitative resin parameters, melt rheology, and the two principal conversion routes (blown and cast). The chapter opens by situating PE among commodity polymers and noting the two synthesis families — free-radical (high-pressure, up to 3500 atm / 300 °C) and Ziegler–Natta/metallocene (milder) (p.41). It then defines the three intrinsic resin parameters — molecular-weight distribution (MWD), melt index (MI), and density (branching/rigidity) (p.42–p.44), and decomposes the physics of branching (LCB vs SCB), intrinsic viscosity, melting/heat-of-fusion, and shear/elongational rheology including the "reptation/tube" model and "strain hardening" (p.44–p.50). The second half is applied: blown-film extrusion (≈85% of film) and cast-film extrusion, with detailed process variables (cooling, blow ratio, frost-line, chill-roll), optical/impact/blocking property effects, troubleshooting tables, and a long section on shrink film (orientation, balanced shrinkage, MD/TD) (p.50–p.72). The through-line is: resin molecular architecture and rheology determine the processing window, which in turn sets optical, mechanical and shrink properties — a quantitative bridge between polymer science and the converting line.

### 核心观点标题

PE synthesis: free-radical (high-pressure) vs Ziegler–Natta/metallocene

Melt index is an inverse proxy for molecular weight and strength

Density encodes crystallinity, which is set by branching

Long-chain branching, not short-chain branching, controls melt rheology

Repletion/rheology explained by the reptation "tube" model

Blown film dominates; cooling is the rate-limiting step

- 

Shrink film requires balanced MD/TD orientation

### 在书中的作用

Chapter 2 is the PE-specific deepening of Ch1. Where Ch1 catalogued all polyolefins, Ch2 focuses on PE and supplies the rheological and processing machinery that Ch1 lacked. It feeds Ch3 (PP processing/durability), Ch4 (additive solubility in PE), and supports Ch5–Ch7 (degradation/flame/pollutant chemistry occur in these same PE films). The reptation model and LCB/SCB distinction introduced here reappear conceptually in Ch4 (orientation drawing reduces solubility) and Ch7 (PE double-bond nitration).

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance: ★★★★☆ (4/5) — PE films (HDPE/LLDPE/LDPE) are the workhorse of hygiene backsheets, liner films and wrapping; MI/MWD/density selection and blown-vs-cast choice are daily decisions.

ACTION CARD 1 — Resin grade selection (situation → apply → effect): Situation: specify PE for a diaper backsheet. → Apply p.42–44: choose density (HDPE for barrier, p.35/Ch1) and MI per process (blown 0.5–2, cast 2–6, coating 5–12 g/10 min, p.41). → Effect: right flow/strength balance and extruder compatibility.

ACTION CARD 2 — Shrink wrap for multipacks (situation → apply → effect): Situation: collation-wrap hygiene multipacks. → Apply p.68: set MDSR = BUR for balanced shrinkage; use low-MI resin for heavy-duty shrink (p.69). → Effect: tight, balanced shrink with high shrink strength.

ACTION CARD 3 — Control blocking on the line (situation → apply → effect): Situation: blown film blocks at wind-up. → Apply p.63 troubleshooting: lower melt temp, reduce cooling-air temp, increase airflow, add antiblock (cf. Ch1 p.22). → Effect: higher line speed without blocking.

- 

Relevance slightly below Ch1/Ch3 because Ch2 is PE-specific and less directly about the absorbent core; still high for backsheet/wrap films.

## Chapter 3 — Processing Conditions and Durability of Polypropylene Films

### 概述

Chapter 3 (H. Aglan and Y.X. Gan) surveys the structure, synthesis, film-processing and — most centrally — the durability of semicrystalline polypropylene (PP) films under tensile loading and ultraviolet (UV) irradiation. The chapter opens with the commercial history of PP (Ziegler–Natta catalysis, 1954 Milan discovery, 1968 MgCl₂-supported high-yield catalysts) and its stereochemical architecture (isotactic / atactic / syndiotactic). It then treats film manufacture (chill-roll cast, tenter-frame oriented, blown), the functional additive package (antiblock, slip, phenolic/phosphite stabilisers), and the free-radical UV-oxidation mechanism in three steps (initiation → propagation → termination). The empirical core is a case study correlating durability (defined as strain-to-failure) with microstructure across processing stages (PP1 → PP7) and additive systems. The overriding conclusion is that PP durability is highly sensitive to UV ageing and to stretching/ orientation, but largely insensitive to UV-stabiliser/antioxidant addition within the tested ranges — a finding the authors themselves flag as surprising and worth deeper engineering study.

### 核心观点标题

UV/ageing durability is a defining end-use property of PP film

Stereoregularity (isotacticity) is what makes PP crystallisable and useful

Ziegler–Natta catalysis made stereoregular PP possible

UV degradation proceeds by a free-radical autoxidation chain; oxidation is a surface phenomenon

Stretching/knifing raises strength but collapses durability

- 

Additives did NOT measurably change durability in the case study (contestable)

### 在书中的作用

Ch.3 is the PP-specific durability deep-dive that complements Ch.1 (polyolefin film technology) and Ch.2 (PE processing). Where Ch.1–2 emphasise making film (extrusion, orientation, rheology), Ch.3 emphasises surviving use (UV, stabilisers, microstructural durability) and is the only chapter to publish a full tensile/SEM durability case study. It also supplies the book's most explicit hygiene junction: cast PP film as the diaper outer layer (p.76) and PP nonwovens for surgical masks/gowns (p.75), bridging film science to the absorbent-hygiene reader.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance rating: ★★★★★ (PP is the dominant backsheet/nonwoven polyolefin in diapers, wipes, surgical hygiene).

ACTION CARD 1 — Diaper cast-PP backsheet specification

Situation: Specifying the outer moisture barrier of a diaper/nappy.

How to apply: Cite p.76 — "Cast PP films are also often used as the outer protective layer in diaper (nappy) construction. The purpose of using PP films is to keep the moisture inside the diaper." Require UV-stabilised + antioxidant grade even if stored shaded; treat thin backsheet as a surface-oxidation risk (p.88).

Expected effect: Prevents brittle microcrack failure (p.92 "totally lost their load-bearing capability") during warehouse/retail UV exposure.

ACTION CARD 2 — Stabiliser package & yellowing control

Situation: White/printed hygiene film discolours over shelf life.

How to apply: Per p.89, hindered-phenol stabilisers "give a distinct yellow colour to PP" and interact with catalyst residues / NOₓ, SOₓ to intensify colour centres. Prefer hindered-amine (HA) + phosphite secondary synergistic system (p.90) for low-colour films; verify base resin is already stabilised before blaming additive.

Expected effect: Retains whiteness while meeting UV-durability requirement.

ACTION CARD 3 — Stretch/orientation vs tear-durability trade-off

Situation: Converting PP tape/laminate for elastic or high-strength hygiene components.

How to apply: p.94 shows stretching lifts tensile strength (37→220 MPa) but collapses durability (500%→33%). For a backsheet you need retained elongation, not just peak load — avoid over-orienting the moisture barrier.

- 

Expected effect: Avoids catastrophic low-elongation failure in the finished article.

## Chapter 4 — Solubility of Additives in Polymers

### 概述

Chapter 4 (Alexander Mar'in) is a physically grounded treatise on where additives go inside a polymer — the thermodynamics and morphology of additive dissolution, diffusion, blooming and wash-out. It opens by noting that real polymer articles are multi-component (stabilisers, plasticisers, dyes, gases, impurities) and that additives redistribute between polymer and surroundings via sorption/diffusion. The core theory is built in §4.3–4.4: Henry's-law (ideal) isotherms, Flory–Huggins χ (regular-solution) deviations, and Langmuir-type sorption onto discrete "centres" (free volume, chain entanglements, polar groups). §4.5 catalogues the factors shifting solubility — crystallinity, polymer orientation/drawing, polar groups, co-additives, plasticisers, high-MW additive dissolution, and polymer oxidation. §4.6 closes with additive loss: sweating-out/blooming, evaporation, and wash-out. The unifying message for the hygiene reader is stark: stabiliser solubility in polyolefins at room temperature is often below the 0.1–0.5 wt% normal dosage, so excess blooms or washes out — a direct skin-contact and fluid-migration safety issue.

### 核心观点标题

Real polymers are multi-component; additives migrate in and out

Additive sits in amorphous free volume / sorption centres, not the crystal

Sorption is often Langmuir-type (finite centres), not ideal Henry

Normal dosage exceeds room-temperature solubility → blooming is routine

Orientation/drawing lowers solubility and diffusion

- 

For fluid-contact articles, wash-out is the dominant loss mechanism

### 在书中的作用

Ch.4 is the additive-physics companion to Ch.3's additive-chemistry (UV stabilisers) and Ch.1–2's additive-function (slip/antiblock/antioxidant in processing). It supplies the missing mechanistic layer: why a stabiliser stays, blooms, or migrates. For the hygiene reader it is the bridge between "we add 0.3% antioxidant" (Ch.3) and "it ends up on the film surface / leaches into fluid" (safety & performance). It also underpins Ch.5–7 (PVC, flame retardants, NOₓ) where additive stability/migration matters.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance rating: ★★★★★ (every hygiene film/additive decision — backsheet, nonwoven, SAP package, skin-contact safety — hinges on solubility, bloom and migration).

ACTION CARD 1 — Choose stabilisers by solubility, not just by activity

Situation: Selecting antioxidant/UV stabilizer for a PP/LDPE backsheet.

How to apply: Per p.115 and Table 4.1, normal 0.1–0.5 wt% dose often exceeds room-T solubility (e.g. Irganox 1010 in iPP only 0.15% at 50–100 °C, p.117). Prefer higher-solubility grades (phenolics > aromatic amines at equal MW, p.115) or raise let-down temperature; otherwise expect bloom.

Expected effect: Avoids surface powder/drops that abrade or transfer to skin; retains in-film stabiliser.

ACTION CARD 2 — Blooming/bloom control on skin-contact film

Situation: White/printed film that must not transfer additive to wearer skin.

How to apply: p.125 — "Any excess … blooms or sweats out." Size the dose to ≤ equilibrium solubility at use temperature; for oriented (drawn) film note solubility drops with drawing (p.119), so re-check post-orientation.

Expected effect: Pass skin-contact/extractables limits; no greasy/chalky surface.

ACTION CARD 3 — Wash-out risk for fluid-contact components

Situation: Film or nonwoven wetted by urine/water over hours (diaper, wipe).

How to apply: p.125 — for fluid-contact, "washing out is the principal cause of undesired loss." High-MW, low-solubility additives in a swollen/plasticised matrix migrate fastest when solvent solubility in polymer is high (p.126). Minimize extractable by choosing high-MW, low-mobility stabilisers and amorphous-compatible grades.

- 

Expected effect: Sustained stabilization through use; lower leachate to wearer/fluid.

## Chapter 5 — Polyvinyl Chloride: Degradation and Stabilisation

### 概述

Chapter 5 (Minsker, Zaikov & Zaikov) is a mechanistic monograph on why PVC is intrinsically unstable and how to stabilise it, centred on a decisive revision of degradation theory. The authors reject the long-accepted β-chloroallyl activation model and argue, with kinetics and model-compound data, that the true labile site is the oxovinylene (carbonyl-allyl) group –C(O)–CH=CHCl–CH₂–, whose double bond is activated by the adjacent electrophilic carbonyl. Because its elimination rate constant (~10⁻² s⁻¹) dwarfs that of normal repeat units (~10⁻⁸ s⁻¹) by ~10⁵ (KCl:Kc:Kt:Kp ≈ 1:100:100:100,000), oxovinylene accounts for ≥90% of gross dehydrochlorination. The chapter then derives "chemical stabilisation" (phosphites/Diels–Alder trapping of oxovinylene), plus three solution-phase phenomena — solvational, structural-physical, and "echo" stabilisation — and closes with a future agenda that explicitly calls for PVC free of labile backbone groups and free of toxic Ba/Cd/Pb stabilisers. For the hygiene reader this is the toxicology/regulatory backbone of why PVC films are being displaced by PP (cf. Ch.3, p.141).

### 核心观点标题

PVC's cardinal flaw is facile dehydrochlorination + coloration

The "weak site" was unidentified for decades (a live controversy)

The accepted β-chloroallyl theory is wrong; oxovinylene is the true labile group

Oxovinylene dominates the rate by ~10⁵ and ≥90% of loss

Chemical stabilisation = trapping oxovinylene (phosphites, dienes)

- 

Future agenda bans toxic stabilisers (Ba/Cd/Pb) — direct hygiene mandate

### 在书中的作用

Ch.5 is the degradation-mechanism anchor for the book's additive/stability trilogy (Ch.3 UV of PP, Ch.4 solubility of additives, Ch.5 PVC chemistry) and the launchpad for Ch.6 (flame-retardant ecology) and Ch.7 (NOₓ attack). It is the only chapter treating a chlorinated polymer and the only one issuing an explicit toxic-additive elimination mandate — the environmental thread that Ch.3 already touched ("environmental concerns surrounding PVC … accelerated the use of cast PP films," p.141).

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance rating: ★★★★ (PVC is being displaced from hygiene film by PP for toxicity reasons, but legacy PVC film, plastisol coatings and the Ba/Cd/Pb-elimination lesson remain directly relevant; the degradation/oxidation logic also informs plasticised TPE components).

ACTION CARD 1 — Prefer PP over PVC for skin-contact backsheet

Situation: Choosing a backsheet/outer film for diaper or wipe.

How to apply: Ch.3 (p.141) notes "environmental concerns surrounding PVC have accelerated the use of cast PP films"; Ch.5 adds the mechanism — PVC zipper-dehydrochlorinates (p.131) and traditionally used Ba/Cd/Pb stabilisers now slated for elimination (p.153).

Expected effect: Avoids HCl/colour-degradation and toxic heavy-metal extractable risk on skin.

**ACTION CARD 2 — If PVC must be used, specify non-heavy-metal stabilisation

Situation: A PVC component cannot be substituted.

How to apply: Per p.153 future agenda, demand formulations free of Ba/Cd/Pb; per p.138 use phosphite/Diels–Alder "chemical stabilisation" of oxovinylene rather than HCl-scavenging metals.

Expected effect: Meets toxics-elimination mandate; stable colour under heat.

**ACTION CARD 3 — Guard plasticised/elastic PVC-TPE against echo degradation

Situation: Elastic or plastisol hygiene part (PVC-based) stored/wetted.

How to apply: p.152 — ester-plasticiser oxidation accelerates PVC destruction >2 orders of magnitude; add antioxidant to the plasticiser ("echo stabilisation," Fig. 5.9 curve 5).

- 

Expected effect: Prevents catastrophic thermo-oxidative failure of the elastic element during shelf life.

## Chapter 6 — Ecological Issues of Polymer Flame Retardants

### 概述

Chapter 6 (Zaikov & Lomakin) surveys the ecological and mechanistic landscape of polymer flame retardants (FR), framed by the tension between life-safety benefit and environmental persistence/toxicity. It opens with the four FR families — inorganic (~50% of volume), halogenated (~25%, mainly brominated), organophosphorus (~20%), and nitrogen-based (limited) — and their gas- vs condensed-phase action: halogens trap OH/H radicals in the flame; hydroxides release cooling water; phosphorus drives condensed-phase charring. The ecological core is the brominated-FR problem: PBDE/PBB bioaccumulate, persist in sediments, and can form brominated dioxins/furans (TCDD-type toxicity, TEF scheme) on combustion. The chapter then promotes three "ecologically friendly" alternatives — intumescent systems, polymer organic char-formers (PVOH/PA-6,6 synergistic carbonisation), and polymer-layered-silicate nanocomposites — each lowering heat-release/charring in cone-calorimeter tests. For the hygiene reader the takeaway is twofold: avoid halogenated FR where skin/incineration exposure is possible, and note that nanoclay barrier technology (also a hygiene film tool) doubles as a flame retardant.

### 核心观点标题

FR saves lives but has an ecological cost; four families dominate

Halogenated FR work by gas-phase radical trapping

Brominated FR are persistent, bioaccumulative, dioxin-forming

Intumescent / char-forming systems are low-impact but hard to predict

Char yield correlates with fire resistance; synergy beats components

- 

Nanocomposites give flame retardancy + mechanical upgrade simultaneously

### 在书中的作用

Ch.6 is the ecology/regulatory capstone of the stability-and-additives arc (Ch.3 UV, Ch.4 solubility, Ch.5 PVC chemistry) and the natural sequel to Ch.5's toxic-stabiliser elimination. It generalises Ch.5's "avoid toxic additives" lesson to the whole additive class of FR, and it introduces nanocomposite/clay technology that reappears implicitly whenever hygiene barrier films use montmorillonite. It links forward to Ch.7 (NOₓ/pollutant attack on polymers).

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance rating: ★★★ (FR are uncommon in skin-contact diaper film, but the ecological/halogen-avoidance lesson, nanoclay barrier tech, and medical/Adult-incontinence fire-safety requirements make it relevant; Ch.5's toxic-elimination thread continues here).

ACTION CARD 1 — Avoid halogenated FR in skin-contact / incinerable hygiene goods

Situation: A hygiene component needs fire safety (e.g. adult-incontinence or medical nonwoven).

How to apply: Per p.161–162, brominated FR form brominated dioxins/furans on combustion and persist/bioaccumulate. Choose phosphorus/nitrogen intumescent or inorganic (ATH/magnesium hydroxide) instead; if FR is unavoidable in an incinerated product, exclude BFR.

Expected effect: Avoids dioxin emission and toxic extractable; meets ecoProcurement.

**ACTION CARD 2 — Use nanoclay for dual barrier + flame retardancy

Situation: Specifying a high-barrier hygiene film.

How to apply: p.180 — polymer-layered silicate nanocomposites give "decreased gas permeability, increased solvent resistance and decreased flammability" plus +40% tensile / +68% modulus at 5 wt%. Consider montmorillonite in cast PP/PE backsheet.

Expected effect: One additive delivers moisture barrier + mechanical upgrade + incidental flame retardancy.

**ACTION CARD 3 — Spec char-yield, not just FR presence, for thin films

Situation: Qualifying an "eco-friendly" FR film.

How to apply: p.179 — char yield and peak RHR (cone calorimeter) are the real metrics; intumescent efficacy is unpredictable (p.173), so require measured LOI/peak-RHR, not a supplier FR claim.

- 

Expected effect: Prevents over-reliance on unproven intumescent loadings in thin films.

## Chapter 7 — Interaction of Polymers with the Nitrogen Oxides in Polluted Atmospheres

### 概述

Chapter 7 (Pariiskii, Gaponova & Davydov) is a mechanistic review of how atmospheric nitrogen oxides — chiefly NO and NO₂ (with N₂O₄ dimer) — attack solid polymers, closing the book's additive/degradation trilogy by adding gaseous pollutants as an ageing driver alongside heat/UV/oxygen (Ch.3) and HCl (Ch.5). It classifies polymers into three groups (vinyl polyolefins; unsaturated rubbers; amide/urethane/imide) and shows NO₂ is the active radical: it adds to C=C double bonds (Ea 8–16 kJ/mol), abstracts labile H (Ea 56–68 kJ/mol in PE, 60 in PP), and — critically — decomposes polymer hydroperoxides, the very species UV oxidation (Ch.3) creates, via a trace-NOₓ-accelerated chain (Scheme 7.76). Polyamides/urethanes are surprisingly vulnerable through N–H nitrosation that collapses the hydrogen-bond network. The chapter's practical warning: accelerated tests use 10²–10⁴× atmospheric NOₓ and linearly extrapolate — "a priori ambiguous" (p.187) — so real-service lifetimes under pollution are uncertain. For the hygiene reader this mechanistically explains the NOₓ/SOₓ colour-centre intensification noted in Ch.3 (p.89) and flags nylon/PU elastic elements in traffic-exposed storage.

### 核心观点标题

NO₂ is the active pollutant radical; NO mostly terminates chains

NO₂ adds to C=C and abstracts H; double bonds are the easy entry

Accelerated-test extrapolation to atmospheric NOₓ is unreliable

NO₂ decomposes polymer hydroperoxides (the UV legacy) via trace-accelerated chain

Polyamides are surprisingly NO₂-sensitive via N–H nitrosation

- 

PU films lose tensile strength under NO₂

### 在书中的作用

Ch.7 is the environmental-ageing capstone that completes the degradation arc: Ch.3 (UV/O₂ of PP), Ch.4 (additive solubility/loss), Ch.5 (PVC HCl), Ch.6 (FR ecology) → Ch.7 (gaseous-pollutant attack). It is the only chapter on external atmospheric reactants and the only one using ESR to image the reaction front. It also explains the NOₓ colour-centre mechanism implicit in Ch.3's phenolic-yellowing note (p.89), tying the whole stability story together.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance rating: ★★★★ (explains the NOₓ/SOₓ colour-centre intensification of phenolic stabilisers noted in Ch.3 p.89; flags nylon/PU elastic elements and pollution-exposed storage; ties UV-hydroperoxide chemistry to atmospheric ageing).

**ACTION CARD 1 — Expect extra colour/yellowing in polluted-air storage

Situation: White hygiene film/nonwoven warehouse near traffic or industry.

How to apply: Ch.3 (p.89) notes phenolic stabiliser yellowing intensifies with NOₓ/SOₓ; Ch.7 shows NO₂ attacks C=C and decomposes hydroperoxides (p.189, p.207), generating N-containing colour centres. Specify lower phenolic load or HALS (Ch.4) and verify whiteness after polluted-air ageing, not just UV ageing.

Expected effect: Avoids surprise yellowing/claim failure in real distribution.

**ACTION CARD 2 — Protect nylon/PU elastic elements from NO₂

Situation: Elastic leg cuff (PU film) or nylon component in diaper.

How to apply: p.197 (PA N–H nitrosation collapses H-bonds) and p.200 (PU tensile loss under NO₂+air). Minimise residual unsaturation, store sealed, and add antioxidant to suppress hydroperoxide build-up that NO₂ later detonates (p.207).

Expected effect: Preserves elastic recovery/strength through shelf life.

**ACTION CARD 3 — Do not trust accelerated NOₓ ageing claims linearly

Situation: Supplier cites "passes NOₓ ageing test."

How to apply: p.187 — accelerated tests use 10³–10⁴× atmospheric NOₓ and linearly extrapolate, "a priori ambiguous." Require the test concentration and a non-linear (kinetics-based) justification, or run a real-concentration long-term check.

- 

Expected effect: Prevents over-confident shelf-life claims that fail in field.

## Chapter 8 — Modifications of Plastic Films

### 概述

Chapter 8 surveys the methods by which plastic films are deliberately modified to improve their mechanical, physical and physicochemical performance for specific end-uses. The chapter, authored by E.M. Abdel-Bary, is organised into mechanical modification (orientation, crystallisation, crosslinking), chemical surface modification (fluorination, chlorination, bromination, sulfonation, chemical etching, grafting), physical surface modification (plasma, corona, radiation), and a full battery of characterisation techniques, closing with application fields. The governing logic is that bulk polymer properties are often adequate but surface/structure tailoring unlocks barrier, adhesion, wettability and bio-functionality. The author treats modification as either bulk (orientation, crosslinking) or surface-localised (fluorination, plasma, corona, grafting), emphasising that surface treatments preserve bulk properties while altering the top nanometres — a distinction that recurs throughout. The chapter is strongly empirically oriented, drawing on several decades of polymer-surface literature rather than proposing new theory.

### 核心观点标题

Modification serves property tailoring, not synthesis

Orientation aligns chains and freezes them

Radiation crosslinking makes PE infusible and tougher

Surface fluorination sharply improves barrier

Grafting enables hydrophilic/bio-functional surfaces but is hard to industrialise

- 

Corona is the dominant industrial surface treatment

### 在书中的作用

Ch8 is the "how to change a film after you have it" chapter. It complements Ch9 (applications), Ch10 (agriculture stability), Ch12 (testing) and Ch13 (recycling). Surface modification (corona, fluorination) reappears in Ch9 (§9.8 Surface Treatment) and Ch12 (§12.5.9 Wetting Tension), forming a coherent thread: make film → modify surface → test → apply → recycle.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance: ★★★★☆ — directly informs breathable backsheets (orientation/crosslinking), printable/heat-sealable PE layers (corona/plasma), and future bio-functional topsheets (grafting).

ACTION CARD 1 — Corona for printable PE backsheet

Situation: PE film won't accept ink/adhesive.

## Chapter 9 — Applications of Plastic Films in Packaging

### 概述

Chapter 9 (Susan E. Selke) systematically maps how plastic films are used in packaging, progressing from the four basic packaging functions (containment, protection, communication, utility) to flexible package forms (wraps, bags, pouches), heat-sealing, and the major film resins with their property tables. It then treats multilayer structures (coating, lamination, coextrusion, metallisation, SiO_x), surface treatment, static, printing, and the science of barriers/permeation with full transport equations, closing on environmental/life-cycle issues. The organising argument is economic and functional: flexible packaging saves material, space and cost versus rigid, at the price of lower strength and harder reclosure. The chapter is unusually quantitative — it gives permeability coefficients, WVTR/O2 tables for LDPE, HDPE, PP, PVC, PET, PVDC, PCTFE, PVOH, EVOH, Nylon, EVA, ionomers — making it a reference data chapter for film selection. It is the most directly applicable chapter for hygiene flexible packaging (bags, pouches, backsheet laminates).

### 核心观点标题

Flexible packaging trades strength for material savings

LDPE/LLDPE dominate; metallocene narrows the heat-seal gap

PP is stiff/cheap but seals poorly unless coextruded

EVOH is an excellent but moisture-sensitive oxygen barrier

Metallised film can outperform foil laminates in distribution

- 

Permeation = solubility × diffusivity; shelf-life is calculable

### 在书中的作用

Ch9 is the application anchor for "films in service." It is referenced by Ch8 (modification goals) and Ch10 (agriculture films share LDPE/EVA base), and it informs Ch12 (test methods for packaging films) and Ch13 (film waste = mostly LDPE/LLDPE). It is the chapter a hygiene packaging engineer would consult first.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance: ★★★★★ — this is the primary chapter for diaper/wipe flexible packaging: pouch & bag forms, FFS, seal-layer resin choice (LDPE/LLDPE/ionomer), multilayer barriers, and permeation-based shelf-life of odour/moisture-sensitive products.

## Chapter 10 — Applications of Plastic Films in Agriculture

### 概述

Chapter 10 (Abdel-Bary, Yehia & Mansour) examines polyethylene-based films for protected cultivation — greenhouses, tunnels, mulch — covering production, the light/thermal/ lifetime requirements, and above all the photochemical and thermal stability of these films under real outdoor service. The central narrative is that LDPE greenhouse/mulch films fail by photooxidation, and their lifetime is governed by the interplay of UV radiation, temperature, humidity, wind/abrasion, agrochemicals and metal ions. The chapter reviews UV stabilisers (screeners, Ni quenchers, HALS), additive compatibility/diffusion, laboratory vs outdoor weathering correlation, chemical-structure changes (carbonyl index, crystallinity, MWD), and the special recycling problem of soil/pesticide-contaminated agricultural film. It is the most degradation-science-intensive chapter and is methodologically empirical (DSC, FTIR, GPC, disc-stacking diffusion). For hygiene R&D it is the closest available treatment of "how a polyolefin film ages outdoors/under stress," transferable to storage and transport stability of hygiene articles.

### 核心观点标题

Agricultural film volume and LDPE dominance

Photooxidation is multi-factor and hard to lab-predict

HALS are the modern stabiliser of choice

Sulphur/chlorine agrochemicals poison HALS

Recycled agricultural film carries soil/iron/pesticide burden

- 

Multilayer with virgin top + recycled bottom is the practical route

### 在书中的作用

Ch10 is the "durability in hostile environment" chapter; it bridges Ch8 (modification/stabilisation intent) and Ch13 (recycling of aged film). It complements Ch12 (weathering/abrasion tests) and Ch9 (barrier design). For hygiene it supplies the degradation-science vocabulary (HALS, carbonyl index, hydroperoxide, metal-ion catalysis) needed to specify stable films.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance: ★★★★☆ — hygiene films (backsheets, wraps) age under warehouse UV/heat/humidity; HALS + UV-absorber strategy, carbonyl-index QC, and metal-ion-catalysis caution all transfer directly. Lower than Ch9 because agriculture-specific stressors (pesticides, soil) are partial analogues.

ACTION CARD 1 — Stabiliser system for hygiene film storage

Situation: PE film yellows/cracks in warm warehouse.

How to apply: Use HALS + UV absorber combination (p.279 retains >94% tensile after 600 h); avoid Fe/Cu contact (p.273 metal-ion catalysis).

Expected effect: Extended shelf-life, less photoyellowing.

ACTION CARD 2 — Carbonyl-index incoming QC

How to apply: Measure CI = A_1713/d ×100 by FTIR (p.277); high CI flags brittle reprocessed resin.

Expected effect: Screen out over-oxidised recycled content before conversion.

ACTION CARD 3 — Virgin skin over recycled core

How to apply: Multilayer: virgin top + recycled-PE bottom (p.279); keeps contaminants buried.

## Chapter 11 — Physicochemical Criteria for Estimating the Efficiency of Burn Dressings

### 概述

This chapter (K. Z. Gumargalieva & G. E. Zaikov) develops, for the first time in the book's framing, a quantitative physicochemical basis for judging burn-dressing performance. The authors argue that dressing efficiency cannot be assessed from scattered clinical reports because the underlying physicochemical properties (sorption, penetrability, adhesion) are rarely tabulated. They therefore build a measurement programme — porosity, pore size, surface energy, sorption, air/vapour penetrability, adhesion — and a theoretical model in which the maximal sorption capacity is set by the free volume of the material, calculable from its density. From sorption kinetics, gas/liquid transport equations and an action model (sorption → evaporation → gas transfer), they derive four concrete first-aid dressing criteria: free-volume utilisation ≥0.9, thickness ≥0.5 cm, adhesive strength ≤20 N/m, and open-pore diameter ≤0.5 µm. The chapter is thus a bridge between polymer film/porous-material science and a wound-care application, with extensive tabulated data on cellulose, collagen, polyurethane and polyolefin dressings.

### 核心观点标题

Sorption ceiling equals free volume

A dressing must fulfil three coupled functions

Polyolefins are near-impermeable to water (great barrier, poor wicking)

Wet-state gas penetrability collapses by orders of magnitude

Quantitative first-aid efficiency criteria

- 

Adhesion is governed by surface energy and wetting angle

### 在书中的作用

As Ch.11 of a Handbook of Plastic Films, this is the most "applied/medical" chapter and the clearest demonstration that film/porous-polymer science generalises beyond packaging. It reconnects to Ch.8 (surface modification, surface energy), Ch.9 (permeation P = D·S, barrier design), Ch.12 (film testing methods), and Ch.13 (material choice & recyclability). For the hygiene expert it is the conceptual twin of topsheet/backsheet/acquisition-layer design, using wound exudate as the analogue of urine/serum.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance to diaper / wipe / absorbent-core R&D: ★★★★☆ (same functional triad — sorb/breathe/barrier — but body fluid and SAP context differ).

ACTION CARD 1 — Design the acquisition/distribution layer by free-volume utilisation

Situation: Specifying a new acquisition or distribution layer (ADL) / fluff-SAP core.

Apply: Use the chapter's rule that maximal sorption ≈ free volume (p.285) and require free-volume utilisation ≥0.9 with a thickness threshold (p.323: "Dressing thickness must be 0.5 cm or more").

Expected effect: Predictive, density-based capacity control instead of trial-and-error; quantified core-weight vs. absorbency trade-off.

ACTION CARD 2 — Choose backsheet polymer from water solubility & gas penetrability tables

Apply: Use Table 11.5 (p.299: PP 0.007, PE 0.006 g/g water solubility) and Table 11.11 (p.309: PE O₂ penetrability 0.35×10¹⁵) to position PE/PET as barriers and microporous/breathable films where vapour escape is needed.

ACTION CARD 3 — Control topsheet skin-friendliness via surface energy

Situation: Reducing topsheet cling / skin irritation.

Apply: Apply Dupré–Young W_a = γ(1 − cos θ) (p.316) and the rule γ_l >> γ_s for low adhesion; choose low-γ_s surfaces (PTFE 18.5, silicone 21.0 — Table 11.15) or corona/plasma treat to tune wetting angle.

## Chapter 12 — Testing of Plastic Films

### 概述

Chapter 12 (E. M. Abdel-Bary & G. Akovali) is a comprehensive catalog of standardized test methods for plastic films, anchored on the premise that films are defined by viscoelastic behaviour absent in metals/paper. The authors first frame why testing is hard — polymer properties swing with temperature, rate and humidity across glassy→leathery→rubbery→flow regimes within <100 °C. They then survey mechanical tests (tensile, impact, tear, flexural, DMA), physical/chemical/physicochemical tests (density, haze, ignition/OI, friction, wetting tension, shrinkage, blocking, composition by ¹³C NMR, creep, weathering, abrasion, ESCR, WVTR, OTR), and close with ASTM material specifications for PET, LDPE, MDPE, PE, OPP and crosslinkable ethylene films. The dominant lens is packaging, but nearly every method maps onto hygiene-film quality control (backsheet, topsheet, printed/breathable films, converting). The chapter is reference-oriented rather than argumentative: its value is the inventory of measurable, standardised parameters a film developer must control.

### 核心观点标题

Films are defined by viscoelasticity

A polymer changes state within <100 °C

Molecular orientation makes films anisotropic

Surface treatability is quantified by wetting tension

Detergents crack ethylene plastics (ESCR)

- 

WVTR/OTR are the barrier-defining metrics

### 在书中的作用

Ch.12 is the "how do we verify it" companion to Ch.8 (modifications), Ch.9 (packaging/applications) and Ch.13 (recycling). It translates the material science of the earlier chapters into measurable acceptance criteria. For the hygiene expert it is the QC/QA backbone: every claim about a backsheet, breathable film or printed web must ultimately be confirmed by one of these ASTM methods.

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance to diaper / wipe / absorbent-core R&D: ★★★★★ (almost every test is a QC gate for backsheet, topsheet, printed/breathable film and converting).

ACTION CARD 1 — Specify breathable vs barrier backsheet by WVTR

Situation: Choosing/claming a backsheet film.

Apply: Run ASTM E96 or F1249 (p.348–349) to set water-vapour transmission; pair with ASTM D3985 for OTR. Use the result to tier "impermeable PE" vs "breathable microporous/monolithic".

Expected effect: Evidence-based comfort-vs-leakage trade-off; rejects films that are "breathable" only by marketing claim.

Apply: ASTM D1894 (p.343) static/kinetic µ; account for slip-agent blooming time-dependence before testing.

- 

Expected effect: Avoid web jams/slip and blocking on converting lines.

## Chapter 13 — Recycling of Plastic Waste

### 概述

Chapter 13 (E. M. Abdel-Bary) surveys the recovery of plastic waste, framed by the environmental and economic pressure of ever-growing polymer landfill. It opens with the dominant waste-stream polymer mix (LDPE > HDPE > PP > PS > PET, with plastic films ~68% LDPE/LLDPE) and then lays out the four canonical routes — primary (clean in-plant), secondary (mechanical, with separation/washing), tertiary (chemical/feedstock) and quaternary (energy recovery) — each with its economics and caveats. The bulk of the chapter is devoted to collection & sorting (SPI codes, density float-sink, colour/electrification/NIR/XRF spectroscopy, selective dissolution) and to material-specific recycling of PET, PVC, PE and HDPE, including the contamination chemistry of reprocessing (gel/crosslink blow-outs, black specks, pigment pro-oxidants). It closes with radiation-technology upgrading and biodegradable polymers. For the hygiene expert the chapter is the sustainability playbook: how to design backsheet/film packaging for recycle, what degradation recycled polyolefins suffer, and which bio-based polymers can serve eco-positioned products.

### 核心观点标题

Films are overwhelmingly LDPE/LLDPE

Four recycling routes

Density float-sink is the workhorse separation

Reprocessing creates gels that burst films

Pigments can catalyse degradation

- 

Biodegradable polymers as an end-of-life option

### 在书中的作用

Ch.13 is the sustainability capstone. It consumes the material science of Ch.8–12: a recycled film's properties are governed by the same orientation, crosslinking, stabilisation and barrier principles already developed, but now complicated by contamination and degradation history. For hygiene R&D it links to Ch.9 (barrier/packaging), Ch.10 (stabilisation/UV), Ch.12 (testing of recycled film) and Ch.8 (surface treatment of recycled webs).

### 卫材行动卡

Relevance to diaper / wipe / absorbent-core R&D: ★★★★★ (film waste, recycled backsheet, eco-positioning and design-for-recycling are core sustainability levers).

ACTION CARD 1 — Build a polyolefin float-sink recovery loop for film offcuts

Situation: Backsheet/laminate converting generates LDPE/LLDPE/HDPE trim and rejects.

Apply: Use density float-sink (p.364): water separates PE/PP from PET/PVC/PS; ~930 kg/m³ liquid splits PP/LDPE from HDPE. Route PE stream back as PCR content.
