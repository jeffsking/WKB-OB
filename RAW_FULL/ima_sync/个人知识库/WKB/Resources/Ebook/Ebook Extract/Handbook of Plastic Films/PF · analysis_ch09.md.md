# Chapter 9 — Applications of Plastic Films in Packaging

## 1. Chapter Overview (100–200 words)

Chapter 9 (Susan E. Selke) systematically maps how plastic films are used in packaging, progressing from the four basic packaging functions (containment, protection, communication, utility) to flexible package forms (wraps, bags, pouches), heat-sealing, and the major film resins with their property tables. It then treats multilayer structures (coating, lamination, coextrusion, metallisation, SiO_x), surface treatment, static, printing, and the science of barriers/permeation with full transport equations, closing on environmental/life-cycle issues. The organising argument is economic and functional: flexible packaging saves material, space and cost versus rigid, at the price of lower strength and harder reclosure. The chapter is unusually quantitative — it gives permeability coefficients, WVTR/O2 tables for LDPE, HDPE, PP, PVC, PET, PVDC, PCTFE, PVOH, EVOH, Nylon, EVA, ionomers — making it a reference data chapter for film selection. It is the most directly applicable chapter for hygiene flexible packaging (bags, pouches, backsheet laminates).

## 2. Key Viewpoints (3–6, each with page + verbatim quote + interpretation)

### Viewpoint 1 — Flexible packaging trades strength for material savings

- Page: p.235
- Quote: > "Flexible packages generally take up much less space than the rigid structures they replace, especially before they are filled with product. They commonly require less material, as well. Therefore, switching from rigid to flexible packaging can provide significant economic savings in warehouse space and transportation, as well as in package cost."
- Interpretation: Core economic justification for pouch/roll stock; relevant to diaper bag-in-box and refills.
### Viewpoint 2 — LDPE/LLDPE dominate; metallocene narrows the heat-seal gap

- Page: p.242
- Quote: > "LDPE has better heat-seal properties than LLDPE. It seals at lower temperatures, seals over a wider temperature range, and has better hot tack, all of which result, to a great extent, from its long-chain branching. Metallocene LLDPE containing higher alpha-olefins was designed, in part, to remedy this disadvantage of LLDPE."
- Interpretation: Explains why classic LDPE remains preferred for seal layers; metallocene LLDPE is the modern fix — useful when selecting sealant resins.
### Viewpoint 3 — PP is stiff/cheap but seals poorly unless coextruded

- Page: p.244
- Quote: > "PP, especially BOPP, does not heat-seal well. Therefore, it is commonly coated or coextruded with sealants to make heat-sealable films."
- Interpretation: BOPP is the clarity/ stiffness workhorse but needs a seal layer — exactly the coextruded structure of many hygiene wraps.
### Viewpoint 4 — EVOH is an excellent but moisture-sensitive oxygen barrier

- Page: p.248
- Quote: > "The combination of strong intermolecular forces and crystallinity makes it an excellent barrier to gases, odours and flavours. However, the hydrogen bonds also make it a moisture-sensitive material, and high humidity decreases its barrier capability."
- Interpretation: Critical design rule — EVOH must be a buried inner layer behind a moisture barrier (parallels hygiene barrier laminates).
### Viewpoint 5 — Metallised film can outperform foil laminates in distribution

- Page: p.254
- Quote: > "Metallised foil, since it retains the flexibility and other mechanical characteristics of the film substrate, is not usually subject to flex cracking. Therefore, the barrier characteristics of metallised foil are sometimes superior to those of foil laminations, at later points in distribution."
- Interpretation: Flex-crack avoidance is why metallised PET is chosen over aluminium foil in many high-barrier packs.
### Viewpoint 6 — Permeation = solubility × diffusivity; shelf-life is calculable

- Page: p.258
- Quote: > "It can be shown that the permeability coefficient, as defined by equation (9.1), is equal to the product of the Fick's law diffusion coefficient, D, and the Henry's law solubility coefficient, S… P = DS"
- Interpretation: Gives the engineer a first-principles shelf-life model (Eq. 9.1–9.10) — directly usable for odour/moisture-sensitive hygiene products.
## 3. Research Method & Data Sources

[纯理论/综述章节，未使用实证方法] — a synthesis/review with extensive property tables (Tables 9.1–9.6) drawn from references [1–7]. The permeation section derives transport equations analytically (Fick + Henry). No new experimental data; authority comes from cited handbooks (Hernandez/Selke, Selke 1997) and Wiley Packaging Encyclopedia.

## 4. Argumentation Process

The author argues by functional decomposition:
1. Why packages exist → four functions.
2. What flexible packaging is → wraps vs bags/pouches; unitising (stretch/shrink); FFS.
3. How it is closed → heat-sealing (thermal, impulse, hot-wire, band).
4. What resins fit → for each, a property table + barrier/ sealing narrative.
5. How to combine → multilayer (coating, lamination, coextrusion, metallisation, SiO_x, inorganic).
6. How to finish → surface treatment, static, printing.
7. How to predict performance → permeation science with equations.
8. How to judge responsibly → LCA/environment.
Rhetorical move: each resin is presented with both a strength and a limitation, building a selection logic rather than advocacy.

## 5. Role in the Book

Ch9 is the application anchor for "films in service." It is referenced by Ch8 (modification goals) and Ch10 (agriculture films share LDPE/EVA base), and it informs Ch12 (test methods for packaging films) and Ch13 (film waste = mostly LDPE/LLDPE). It is the chapter a hygiene packaging engineer would consult first.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths: Quantitative property tables and closed-form permeation equations; balanced resin-by-resin treatment; clear multilayer logic.
> "Since LLDPE often permits considerable down-gauging, it can be the lowest cost alternative on a per-use basis." (p.242)

⚠️ Limitations / contestable points:
- Tables are single-source snapshot values; real films vary widely with grade/additives.
- Environmental section is brief and acknowledges scope limit:
> "thorough discussion of such issues is beyond the scope of this chapter." (p.261)
- Little on sustainable/recyclable mono-material structures (pre-dates recent mono-PE pouch trend).

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- WVTR: (p.259) "Water vapour transmission rates (WVTR), rather than permeability coefficients… WVTR = P_water / Δp."
- Permeability coefficient P: (p.258) "P = Ql / (A t Δp)" (steady-state mass transfer).
- EVOH: (p.248) "Ethylene-vinyl alcohol resins are produced by hydrolysis (alcoholysis) of ethylene-vinyl acetate random copolymer."
- Ionomer: (p.251) "ionic bonds function as reversible crosslinks in the polymer, readily disrupted by heat, but reforming on cooling."
- Metallisation: (p.253) "vacuum metallising, involves evaporation of aluminium inside a vacuum chamber, and deposition of the aluminium vapour on a plastic film."
## 8. Micro close-reading (ONE key paragraph)

Source paragraph (p.258, §9.11 Barriers and Permeation):
> "The barrier performance of the film is generally expressed in terms of its permeability coefficient. For one-dimensional steady-state mass transfer, the permeability coefficient is related to the quantity of permeant, which is transferred through the film as represented by the equation: P = Ql / (A t Δp) where P is the permeability coefficient, Q is the mass of permeant passing through the material, l is the thickness of the plastic film, A is the surface area available for mass transfer, t is time, and Δp is the change in permeant partial pressure across the film. It can be shown that the permeability coefficient, as defined by equation (9.1), is equal to the product of the Fick's law diffusion coefficient, D, and the Henry's law solubility coefficient, S… P = DS"

Sentence-by-sentence breakdown:
- S1–S2: Define barrier metric = permeability coefficient; scope = 1-D steady state.
- S3–S5: State Eq. 9.1 and define every symbol (Q, l, A, t, Δp) — operational definition ready for lab use.
- S6: Key decomposition: P = D·S (Fick × Henry), valid for dilute, concentration-independent diffusion. Technique: linking macroscopic flux to molecular transport.
- S7: Implies P depends on T but not thickness/ concentration — the design lever.

💡 Standout line: "P = DS" — the entire barrier-design problem reduces to controlling diffusivity (free volume/structure) and solubility (polarity/affinity).

## 9. Concept deep-dive

Core concept — Multilayer film structures (§9.7).
- Definition (p.252): "plastic packaging films are often combined with one another or with other materials such as paper, aluminium… through processes such as coating, lamination, coextrusion and metallisation."
- Operational definition? Yes — four distinct processes, each with mechanism: coating (solution/suspension/melt on substrate); lamination (adhesive, often LDPE extrusion laminating); coextrusion (layered melt, no separate web, enables ultra-thin barrier layers, p.253); metallisation (vacuum Al); SiO_x (transparent high-barrier, microwave-safe, p.254–255).
- Usage in this chapter: enabling high-barrier snack/pharma packs; buried EVOH; printed-buried webs.
- Relation: coextrusion preferred to lamination unless buried printing needed (p.253).
- Academic origin: flexible-packaging engineering handbooks (refs 1–7).

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- Property tables are generic; grade-specific behaviour (e.g., metallocene nuances) under-developed.
- Open question 1: How to design mono-material (all-PE) high-barrier pouches that remain recyclable (the chapter's metallisation/EVOH/foil multilayers are hard to recycle — see Ch13).
- Open question 2: The Arrhenius extrapolation warning (p.260) — can shelf-life be predicted across Tg spans without lab validation?
## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Relevance: ★★★★★ — this is the primary chapter for diaper/wipe flexible packaging: pouch & bag forms, FFS, seal-layer resin choice (LDPE/LLDPE/ionomer), multilayer barriers, and permeation-based shelf-life of odour/moisture-sensitive products.

ACTION CARD 1 — Select sealant resin
- Situation: Choose a heat-seal layer for a wipe pouch.
- How to apply: Use LDPE for wide seal window/hot-tack (p.242), or metallocene LLDPE to combine strength + seal; for through-grease sealing use ionomer (p.251).
- Expected effect: Reliable seals at high line speed; fewer leakers.

ACTION CARD 2 — Bury EVOH behind a polyolefin moisture barrier
- Situation: Need oxygen barrier for a sensitive hygiene product.
- How to apply: Coextrude EVOH as inner layer with PE/PP outer (p.248–249); avoid exposing EVOH to humidity.
- Expected effect: Maintained O2 barrier; protected from humidity loss.

ACTION CARD 3 — Calculate moisture shelf-life
- Situation: Predict WVTR-driven caking/wetting of a powdered hygiene product.
- How to apply: Use P = WVTR/Δp (p.259) and Eq. 9.3–9.7 with sorption isotherm; verify film thickness.
- Expected effect: Quantitative shelf-life estimate before trial.
