# Chapter 8 — Modifications of Plastic Films

## 1. Chapter Overview (100–200 words)

Chapter 8 surveys the methods by which plastic films are deliberately modified to improve their mechanical, physical and physicochemical performance for specific end-uses. The chapter, authored by E.M. Abdel-Bary, is organised into mechanical modification (orientation, crystallisation, crosslinking), chemical surface modification (fluorination, chlorination, bromination, sulfonation, chemical etching, grafting), physical surface modification (plasma, corona, radiation), and a full battery of characterisation techniques, closing with application fields. The governing logic is that bulk polymer properties are often adequate but surface/structure tailoring unlocks barrier, adhesion, wettability and bio-functionality. The author treats modification as either bulk (orientation, crosslinking) or surface-localised (fluorination, plasma, corona, grafting), emphasising that surface treatments preserve bulk properties while altering the top nanometres — a distinction that recurs throughout. The chapter is strongly empirically oriented, drawing on several decades of polymer-surface literature rather than proposing new theory.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (3–6, each with page + verbatim quote + interpretation)

### Viewpoint 1 — Modification serves property tailoring, not synthesis

- Page: p.213
- Quote: > "Modifications of plastic films are generally used to improve mechanical or physical properties so that the films are suitable for certain applications. This can be achieved by subjecting the films to mechanical or chemical treatments."
- Interpretation: The chapter frames modification as a post-hoc engineering step applied to already-commercial polymers. For hygiene R&D this validates "tune the film, not the resin" strategies (e.g., corona-treat a PE backsheet rather than change polymer).
### Viewpoint 2 — Orientation aligns chains and freezes them

- Page: p.214
- Quote: > "This orientation of the molecules in thermoplastics is essentially a stretching process, which tends to align the molecules in the direction of the stretching force. Once the molecules have been aligned, the ordered arrangement is frozen in, giving rise to a strained condition."
- Interpretation: Explains why biaxially oriented PP/PET gain tensile strength and barrier. Directly relevant to breathable film and backsheet orientation in disposables.
### Viewpoint 3 — Radiation crosslinking makes PE infusible and tougher

- Page: p.215
- Quote: > "Crosslinked polyethylene film can be achieved by subjecting the film to high-energy radiation. When polyethylene film is irradiated, hydrogen and smaller amounts of methane, ethane and propane gases are liberated, and the polymer becomes increasingly insoluble as a result of crosslinking of the molecules via C–C bonds."
- Interpretation: Crosslinking trades solubility for thermal/tear resistance — the same principle used in crosslinked PE foam backsheets for diapers.
### Viewpoint 4 — Surface fluorination sharply improves barrier

- Page: p.215
- Quote: > "Surface fluorination is used to improve the barrier properties of the inner surface of polyethylene during the blow moulding process for formation of bottles. Polyethylene is nonpolar and therefore a poor barrier to nonpolar hydrocarbons."
- Interpretation: A thin polar fluorinated skin converts a poor nonpolar barrier into a good one — a prototype for layered barrier films.
### Viewpoint 5 — Grafting enables hydrophilic/bio-functional surfaces but is hard to industrialise

- Page: p.220
- Quote: > "Among the technical problems, which still remain to a considerable extent, are the concurrent formation of homopolymer in most cases and the lack of reproducibility in these largely heterogeneous reactions. In addition, there is the difficulty of controlling the grafted side chains in the molecular weight (molar mass) distribution."
- Interpretation: Grafting's promise (hydrophilic, ion-exchange, biomedical) is undercut by homopolymer by-product and poor reproducibility — a caveat for any grafted-fibre absorbent concept.
### Viewpoint 6 — Corona is the dominant industrial surface treatment

- Page: p.224
- Quote: > "The corona treatment functions at atmospheric pressure and relatively high temperature. In this case, very significant surface oxidation occurs."
- Interpretation: Corona is cheap, in-line, and oxidises the surface (introducing polar groups) — the standard route to printable/sealable polyolefin films.

This is a [纯理论/综述章节，未使用实证方法] — a literature review chapter. No original experiments; data are cited from ~75 references (journal papers, patents, books). ## 4. Argumentation Process

The author builds the case as a taxonomy rather than a proof chain:
1. Premise: film properties often need adjustment for specific applications.
2. First branch (mechanical): orientation → crystallisation → crosslinking, each illustrated with material examples (PP, PET, Nylon; LDPE/HDPE).
3. Second branch (chemical surface): gas-phase reactions (F, Cl, Br, SO3) then etching then grafting, with mechanistic equations (e.g., bromination radical chain, Eq. 8.2–8.5).
4. Third branch (physical surface): plasma, corona, UV/γ radiation.
5. Characterisation: gravimetric, thermal (DSC), SEM, swelling, MW/MWD, dielectric, surface spectroscopy (AES/XPS/SIMS/IR/XFS/ESCA).
6. Applications: ion-exchange, biomedical, photostabilisation.
Rhetorical move: contrast bulk vs surface effect repeatedly; use "in contrast / however / whereas" to delimit where each method applies.

## 5. Role in the Book

Ch8 is the "how to change a film after you have it" chapter. It complements Ch9 (applications), Ch10 (agriculture stability), Ch12 (testing) and Ch13 (recycling). Surface modification (corona, fluorination) reappears in Ch9 (§9.8 Surface Treatment) and Ch12 (§12.5.9 Wetting Tension), forming a coherent thread: make film → modify surface → test → apply → recycle.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths: Broad, well-referenced coverage of both bulk and surface routes; clear bulk-vs-surface framing; useful mechanistic equations for fluorination depth and grafting yield.
> "Fluorination of polymers by fluorine may be divided into two types: bulk fluorination and surface fluorination." (p.215)

⚠️ Limitations / contestable points:
- Heavy reliance on older (pre-2000) literature; limited treatment of modern metallocene/nanocomposite barriers.
- Grafting section candidly notes industrialisation failure:
> "there has been comparatively little commercialisation of the grafting process." (p.220)
- No quantitative cost/benefit or lifecycle comparison between methods.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Orientation: (p.214) "stretching a film above its glass transition temperature (Tg)… uniaxial orientation or… biaxial orientation."
- Crosslinking: (p.215) "used to improve the mechanical properties and to obtain an infusible film."
- Surface fluorination: (p.215) "Surface fluorination of polyethylene (PE) film by 10% F2 … results in a depth of fluorination ranging between 0 and 50 Å."
- Grafting percentage (GP): (p.224) "grafting percentage (GP) = (A−B)/B ×100" where A,B = extracted graft product, substrate weights.
- Plasma implantation: (p.222) "implants atomic residues at the surface… the polymers change from hydrophobic to hydrophilic."
## 8. Micro close-reading (ONE key paragraph)

Source paragraph (p.215, §8.3 Crosslinking):
> "Crosslinking is used to improve the mechanical properties and to obtain an infusible film. Crosslinked polyethylene film can be achieved by subjecting the film to high-energy radiation. When polyethylene film is irradiated, hydrogen and smaller amounts of methane, ethane and propane gases are liberated, and the polymer becomes increasingly insoluble as a result of crosslinking of the molecules via C–C bonds. This process slightly improves the gas and water vapour barrier properties, and the film has good clarity. The tear strength of the film becomes good, and the resistance to tear initiation and to tear propagation becomes high."

Sentence-by-sentence breakdown:
- S1: States purpose — mechanical upgrade + infusibility (no melt flow).
- S2: Identifies means — high-energy (ionising) radiation.
- S3: Mechanistic detail — radiolysis liberates H2/CH4/C2H6/C3H8; "insoluble" signals gel formation. Technique: free-radical recombination into C–C crosslinks.
- S4: Barrier slightly improves (crosslinks reduce free volume). Keyword: "slightly" — crosslinking is not a primary barrier strategy.
- S5–S6: Tear (initiation + propagation) resistance rises — the key hygiene-relevant gain (toughness of backsheet).

💡 Standout line: "the resistance to tear initiation and to tear propagation becomes high" — crosslinking buys tear toughness, the single most field-relevant property for disposable backsheets.

## 9. Concept deep-dive

Core concept — Graft copolymerisation onto preformed films (§8.3.6).
- Definition (p.220): "Graft copolymerisation of vinyl monomers on to polymeric materials has been the subject of extensive studies… effected, generally, through an initiation reaction involving attack by a macroradical on the monomer."
- Operational definition? Yes — initiation via (1) weak-bond decomposition, (2) chain transfer, (3) redox, (4) photochemical, (5) γ-radiation.
- Usage in this chapter: grafting AA/PVP onto PE for ion-exchange membranes, water desalination, biomedical/bioactive surfaces (p.221–222, 228–229).
- Relation to other concepts: pairs with surface oxidation (corona/plasma) as routes to polar groups; contrasted with bulk crosslinking.
- Academic origin: radical grafting literature of 1950s–90s (Chapiro, Ranby, refs 41–64).

## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- The chapter is a snapshot (~2000) and omits plasma-polymerisation scale-up, atomic-layer/nanocomposite barriers, and sustainability of fluorination (PFOA-era concerns).
- Open question 1: Can grafting be made reproducible and homopolymer-free at industrial scale (e.g., ATRP/RAFT surface grafting)?
- Open question 2: Does surface fluorination survive the flexing/forming cycles of converted hygiene articles (author notes "may not maintain their barrier properties after repeated flexing," p.217)?
## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Relevance: ★★★★☆ — directly informs breathable backsheets (orientation/crosslinking), printable/heat-sealable PE layers (corona/plasma), and future bio-functional topsheets (grafting).

ACTION CARD 1 — Corona for printable PE backsheet
- Situation: PE film won't accept ink/adhesive.
- How to apply: In-line corona (atmospheric pressure, p.224) to oxidise surface; verify by wetting-tension test (Ch12 §12.5.9) ≥3.5×10⁻² N/m.
- Expected effect: Polar groups raise surface energy → ink/laminate adhesion.

ACTION CARD 2 — Crosslinked PE foam backsheet
- Situation: Need tear-tough, thermally stable backsheet.
- How to apply: Electron-beam/γ crosslink PE (p.215) to gain tear-initiation & propagation resistance.
- Expected effect: Higher tear strength, infusibility, slight barrier gain.

ACTION CARD 3 — Graft hydrophilic topsheet (caution)
- Situation: Want wettable, low-rewet surface.
- How to apply: γ-radiation graft of acrylic acid onto PE (p.221) — but control homopolymer and reproducibility (p.220 caveat).
- Expected effect: Hydrophilic, lower contact angle; risk of batch variability.

## 12. Book-map quotes (Top 1–3 representative quotes)

"Once the molecules have been aligned, the ordered arrangement is frozen in, giving rise to a strained condition." — p.214 — encapsulates the orientation principle underpinning BOPP/BOPET used in disposables.

"the polymer becomes increasingly insoluble as a result of crosslinking of the molecules via C–C bonds." — p.215 — the mechanistic essence of radiation crosslinking for tough films.

"there has been comparatively little commercialisation of the grafting process." — p.220 — a candid limits statement worth heeding before betting on grafted-fibre absorbents.
