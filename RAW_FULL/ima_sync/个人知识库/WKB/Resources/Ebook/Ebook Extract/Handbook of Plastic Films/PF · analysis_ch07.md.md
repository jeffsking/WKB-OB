# Chapter 7 — Interaction of Polymers with the Nitrogen Oxides in Polluted Atmospheres

## 1. Chapter Overview (100–200 words)

Chapter 7 (Pariiskii, Gaponova & Davydov) is a mechanistic review of how atmospheric nitrogen oxides — chiefly NO and NO₂ (with N₂O₄ dimer) — attack solid polymers, closing the book's additive/degradation trilogy by adding gaseous pollutants as an ageing driver alongside heat/UV/oxygen (Ch.3) and HCl (Ch.5). It classifies polymers into three groups (vinyl polyolefins; unsaturated rubbers; amide/urethane/imide) and shows NO₂ is the active radical: it adds to C=C double bonds (Ea 8–16 kJ/mol), abstracts labile H (Ea 56–68 kJ/mol in PE, 60 in PP), and — critically — decomposes polymer hydroperoxides, the very species UV oxidation (Ch.3) creates, via a trace-NOₓ-accelerated chain (Scheme 7.76). Polyamides/urethanes are surprisingly vulnerable through N–H nitrosation that collapses the hydrogen-bond network. The chapter's practical warning: accelerated tests use 10²–10⁴× atmospheric NOₓ and linearly extrapolate — "a priori ambiguous" (p.187) — so real-service lifetimes under pollution are uncertain. For the hygiene reader this mechanistically explains the NOₓ/SOₓ colour-centre intensification noted in Ch.3 (p.89) and flags nylon/PU elastic elements in traffic-exposed storage.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (3–6, each with page + verbatim quote + interpretation)

### Viewpoint 1 — NO₂ is the active pollutant radical; NO mostly terminates chains

- Page: p.188
- Quote: > "The paramagnetic nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) is more active compared with NO. This gas is universally present in equilibrium with its dimer molecule: 2NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄."
- Interpretation: NO₂ (not NO) does the damage; NO mostly recombines with radicals and can inhibit oxidation (p.208). This dual role is why pollutant ageing is subtle and test-conditions matter.
### Viewpoint 2 — NO₂ adds to C=C and abstracts H; double bonds are the easy entry

- Page: p.189
- Quote: > "NO₂ reacts at room temperature with the >C=C< double bonds originally contained in PE with the formation of dinitro compounds and nitronitrites … The activation energy of the NO₂ addition to the double bonds of PE is 8-16 kJ/mol. The activation energies of H atom abstraction are 56-68 kJ/mol in PE and 60 kJ/mol in PP."
- Interpretation: Unsaturation (already a UV weak point, Ch.3) is also the NOₓ entry point at near-zero barrier; H-abstraction is far harder. So a film's residual double bonds govern its pollution sensitivity.
### Viewpoint 3 — Accelerated-test extrapolation to atmospheric NOₓ is unreliable

- Page: p.187
- Quote: > "To establish the effect of a given aggressive gas on a particular polymer, the reaction is generally studied at pollutant concentrations that are much higher than those actually existing in polluted atmospheres. The results obtained by this means are then linearly extrapolated to the concentrations of reactants found in the atmosphere. This expedient is, a priori, ambiguous."
- Interpretation: Every service-life claim from accelerated NOₓ testing should be discounted; the chapter itself questions linear scaling because acceleration changes the rate-limiting step.
### Viewpoint 4 — NO₂ decomposes polymer hydroperoxides (the UV legacy) via trace-accelerated chain

- Page: p.207–208
- Quote: > "the interaction of hydroperoxide with NO₂ is more likely to proceed according to the scheme: ROOH + N₂O₃ → [ROONO] + HNO₂ … ROONO → RO• + NO₂ … The increase in the rate of hydroperoxide decomposition with time can be related to reactions proceeding with participation of such nitroso compounds."
- Interpretation: This is the mechanistic bridge to Ch.3: UV-made hydroperoxides (p.86–88) are detonated by NOₓ, accelerating chain scission. Autoacceleration comes from nitroso intermediates — a positive feedback loop.
### Viewpoint 5 — Polyamides are surprisingly NO₂-sensitive via N–H nitrosation

- Page: p.197
- Quote: > "nitrosation through the amide group is the main process of PCA transformation in an NO₂ atmosphere, which leads to disintegration of the system of hydrogen bonds."
- Interpretation: Even strong N–H bonds fail because the consequence (hydrogen-bond network collapse) destroys crystalline cohesion. Relevant to nylon hygiene components (elastic leg cuffs, nonwovens).
### Viewpoint 6 — PU films lose tensile strength under NO₂

- Page: p.200
- Quote: > "The exposure of polyurethane films to an NO₂ atmosphere or a mixture of NO₂ with air leads to the progressive reduction of the tensile strength limit."
- Interpretation: PU elastic films (a hygiene elastic element) degrade mechanically under polluted air — a storage/transport risk near traffic or industry.

## 4. Argumentation Process

The author argues by polymer class, then unifies at the radical level:
1. Framing: pollutants (SO₂/O₃/NOₓ) matter; testing-by-extrapolation is flawed (§7.1).
2. Vinyl group (§7.2.1): NO₂ adds to C=C, abstracts H; PE/PP/PVC only mildly change even at high [NO₂]+UV.
3. Unsaturated/rubber (§7.2.2): far more sensitive; ESR proves nitroxyl spin-labels; reaction front is surface (membrane regime).
4. Amide/urethane/imide (§7.2.3): N–H nitrosation collapses H-bond network; PU loses tensile strength.
5. NO chemistry (§7.3): NO as radical "counter"/spin trap; hydroperoxide decomposition by NO₂ (7.74–7.85).
6. Conclusion (§7.4): NO₂ initiates, NO can inhibit; polyamide sensitivity unexplained.
Rhetorical move: ESR evidence lends authority; the chapter is a review that qualifies (not overclaims) accelerated-test data.

## 5. Role in the Book

Ch.7 is the environmental-ageing capstone that completes the degradation arc: Ch.3 (UV/O₂ of PP), Ch.4 (additive solubility/loss), Ch.5 (PVC HCl), Ch.6 (FR ecology) → Ch.7 (gaseous-pollutant attack). It is the only chapter on external atmospheric reactants and the only one using ESR to image the reaction front. It also explains the NOₓ colour-centre mechanism implicit in Ch.3's phenolic-yellowing note (p.89), tying the whole stability story together.

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths:
- Unifies NO vs NO₂ roles (initiator vs inhibitor, p.208) with explicit energetics (C=C addition 8–16 vs H-abstraction 56–68 kJ/mol, p.189).
- ESR imaging of the reaction front (Fig. 7.1) gives rare spatial proof that nitration is a ~1 mm surface layer — explains why thin films age faster (p.190 autoacceleration stronger for thin films).
- Honest about extrapolation ambiguity (p.187) and the unresolved polyamide mechanism (p.208).

⚠️ Limitations / contestable points:
- Data are overwhelmingly on PE/PP/PS/PMMA/rubbers/PA/PU/PTFE; no SAP, cellulose or PLA — the hygiene matrices are absent except via PA/PU analogy.
- Accelerated tests at 1.3–13 kPa NO₂ (p.189) are 10³–10⁴× atmospheric (1–5 ppm, p.188) — the very extrapolation the chapter warns is ambiguous, yet its quantitative kinetics rely on it.
- Thin-film autoacceleration (p.190) is shown for P-t-BuMA/PS, not for hygiene-relevant PE/PP — direct transfer is inference.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- NO₂ / N₂O₄ equilibrium: (p.188) "2NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄ with Kp = 0.141 atm at 298 K."
- Double-bond addition: (p.189) "NO₂ reacts at room temperature with the >C=C< double bonds … formation of dinitro compounds and nitronitrites."
- Activation energies: (p.189) C=C addition 8–16 kJ/mol; H-abstraction 56–68 (PE), 60 (PP) kJ/mol.
- Hydroperoxide decomposition by NO₂: (p.207–208) ROOH + N₂O₃ → [ROONO] + HNO₂; autoaccelerated by nitroso intermediates.
- Polyamide nitrosation: (p.197) "nitrosation through the amide group … leads to disintegration of the system of hydrogen bonds."
- Diffusion-controlled, surface front: (p.196) PA degradation "depends on the degree of crystallinity"; nitration front ~1 mm, radicals absent at centre (p.192).
- Autoaccelerated degradation: (p.190) α = (P/Q)[exp(Qt)−1]; stronger for thin films.
## 8. Micro close-reading (ONE key paragraph)

Source paragraph (p.208, Conclusion):
> "Nitrogen dioxide is a more active free radical as compared with NO, and is able to break off the labile hydrogen atoms at room temperature as well as to add to the C=C bonds of macromolecules, inducing free-radical degradation of polymers. At the same time, the NO₂ radical can inhibit the free-radical reactions giving nitrogen-containing molecules by the reactions with alkyl, alkoxy and peroxy radicals. The thermal and photochemical conversions of these products also affect the aging process of polymeric materials. Nitrogen dioxide is an initiator of the free-radical degradation of polyolefins at elevated temperatures."

Sentence-by-sentence breakdown:
1. Activity ranking — NO₂ is the aggressive radical; it both H-abstracts (room T) and adds to C=C, launching radical degradation.
2. Dual role — yet NO₂ also terminates by trapping alkyl/alkoxy/peroxy radicals into N-containing products — initiator and inhibitor at once.
3. Secondary ageing — those N-products then thermally/photochemically convert, prolonging ageing.
4. Polyolefin caveat — at elevated T, NO₂ is a frank initiator of polyolefin degradation (links to Ch.3 PP case).

💡 Standout line: NO₂ is simultaneously initiator and inhibitor — the paradox that makes pollutant ageing non-linear and test-extrapolation dangerous.

## 9. Concept deep-dive

### Core concept A — NO₂ addition to unsaturated sites (low-barrier entry)

- Definition (p.189): ">C=C< + NO₂ → >C•−C(NO₂)< (R•); R• + NO₂ → dinitro; R• + ONO → nitronitrite."
- Operational definition? Yes — Ea 8–16 kJ/mol; room-T addition confirmed; H-abstraction (56–68 kJ/mol) is the higher barrier.
- Usage: explains why residual unsaturation (Ch.3 weak point) is also NOₓ entry; why PE/PP only mildly change (little C=C).
- Academic origin: Ogihara (refs [7–8]); Jellinek (ref [6]).
### Core concept B — Hydroperoxide decomposition by NO₂ (UV legacy detonation)

- Definition (p.207–208): ROOH + N₂O₃ → [ROONO] + HNO₂; ROONO → RO• + NO₂; autoaccelerated by nitroso compounds (7.81–7.83).
- Operational definition? Yes — kinetic curves Fig. 7.4 show induction then sharp acceleration; ESR confirms dialkylnitroxyl; induction period sensitive to trace higher NOₓ.
- Usage: mechanistically links Ch.3 UV oxidation (hydroperoxide formation, p.86–88) to pollutant-accelerated scission.
- Academic origin: Pariiskii/Gaponova (ref [34]); Shelton, Carlsson (refs [35–36]).
### Core concept C — Polyamide N–H nitrosation / H-bond collapse

- Definition (p.197): "~CONHCH~ + N₂O₄ → ~CON(NO)CH~ + HNO₃; nitrosation through the amide group … leads to disintegration of the system of hydrogen bonds."
- Operational definition? Yes — UV bands 390–435 nm, IR N–H (3293 cm⁻¹) drop, amide I/II drop, N=O (1504/1387 cm⁻¹) rise; rate ∝ [NO₂]ⁿ, n≈2.
- Usage: explains surprising PA/PU vulnerability; tensile loss in PU (p.200).
- Academic origin: Gaponova et al. (ref [20]); Jellinek (refs [17–18]).
## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- Theoretical: The "linear extrapolation" of accelerated tests is flagged ambiguous (p.187) but the chapter's own kinetics use 1.3–13 kPa NO₂ — the contradiction is unaddressed methodologically (no alternative scaling proposed).
- Scope: PA/PU hygiene relevance is by analogy only; SAP/cellulose/PLA untested. The thin-film autoacceleration (p.190) is shown for P-t-BuMA/PS, not polyolefins.
- Open mechanism: "The low stability of polyamides to NO₂ is quite surprising, because the N–H bond … is rather strong" (p.208) — the authors admit the H-abstraction path is not fully elucidated.
- Open question 1: For a hygiene backsheet stored near traffic (typical NOₓ ~10–100 ppb), what is the realistic service-life penalty given the chapter's warning that 1–5 ppm linear extrapolation is invalid?
- Open question 2: Does the NO₂-driven hydroperoxide detonation (p.207) mean an already-UV-aged PP film (Ch.3) degrades orders of magnitude faster in polluted air than a fresh one — a combined-stress synergy needing testing?
## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Relevance rating: ★★★★ (explains the NOₓ/SOₓ colour-centre intensification of phenolic stabilisers noted in Ch.3 p.89; flags nylon/PU elastic elements and pollution-exposed storage; ties UV-hydroperoxide chemistry to atmospheric ageing).

**ACTION CARD 1 — Expect extra colour/yellowing in polluted-air storage
- Situation: White hygiene film/nonwoven warehouse near traffic or industry.
- How to apply: Ch.3 (p.89) notes phenolic stabiliser yellowing intensifies with NOₓ/SOₓ; Ch.7 shows NO₂ attacks C=C and decomposes hydroperoxides (p.189, p.207), generating N-containing colour centres. Specify lower phenolic load or HALS (Ch.4) and verify whiteness after polluted-air ageing, not just UV ageing.
- Expected effect: Avoids surprise yellowing/claim failure in real distribution.

**ACTION CARD 2 — Protect nylon/PU elastic elements from NO₂
- Situation: Elastic leg cuff (PU film) or nylon component in diaper.
- How to apply: p.197 (PA N–H nitrosation collapses H-bonds) and p.200 (PU tensile loss under NO₂+air). Minimise residual unsaturation, store sealed, and add antioxidant to suppress hydroperoxide build-up that NO₂ later detonates (p.207).
- Expected effect: Preserves elastic recovery/strength through shelf life.

**ACTION CARD 3 — Do not trust accelerated NOₓ ageing claims linearly
- Situation: Supplier cites "passes NOₓ ageing test."
- How to apply: p.187 — accelerated tests use 10³–10⁴× atmospheric NOₓ and linearly extrapolate, "a priori ambiguous." Require the test concentration and a non-linear (kinetics-based) justification, or run a real-concentration long-term check.
- Expected effect: Prevents over-confident shelf-life claims that fail in field.
