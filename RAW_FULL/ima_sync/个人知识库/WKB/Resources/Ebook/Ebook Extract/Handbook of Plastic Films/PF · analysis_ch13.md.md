# Chapter 13 — Recycling of Plastic Waste

## 1. Chapter Overview

Chapter 13 (E. M. Abdel-Bary) surveys the recovery of plastic waste, framed by the environmental and economic pressure of ever-growing polymer landfill. It opens with the dominant waste-stream polymer mix (LDPE > HDPE > PP > PS > PET, with plastic films ~68% LDPE/LLDPE) and then lays out the four canonical routes — primary (clean in-plant), secondary (mechanical, with separation/washing), tertiary (chemical/feedstock) and quaternary (energy recovery) — each with its economics and caveats. The bulk of the chapter is devoted to collection & sorting (SPI codes, density float-sink, colour/electrification/NIR/XRF spectroscopy, selective dissolution) and to material-specific recycling of PET, PVC, PE and HDPE, including the contamination chemistry of reprocessing (gel/crosslink blow-outs, black specks, pigment pro-oxidants). It closes with radiation-technology upgrading and biodegradable polymers. For the hygiene expert the chapter is the sustainability playbook: how to design backsheet/film packaging for recycle, what degradation recycled polyolefins suffer, and which bio-based polymers can serve eco-positioned products.

## 2. Key Viewpoints

### Viewpoint 1 — Films are overwhelmingly LDPE/LLDPE

- Page: p.357
- Quote: > "The majority of plastic films are made from LDPE or linear low-density polyethylene (LLDPE), comprising approximately 68% of the total film production."
- Interpretation: Sets the feedstock reality for any film-recycling programme — polyolefin streams dominate, so hygiene-film recovery should target LDPE/LLDPE/HDPE, not niche resins.
### Viewpoint 2 — Four recycling routes

- Page: p.358
- Quote: > "There are four main approaches to recycling plastics … These are primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary recycling."
- Interpretation: The taxonomy that organises the whole chapter; the hierarchy (reuse > mechanical > chemical > energy) is the decision frame for sustainable film design.
### Viewpoint 3 — Density float-sink is the workhorse separation

- Page: p.364
- Quote: > "Water may be used to separate PP, LDPE and HDPE from PS, PVC and PET. A liquid having a density of about 930 kg/m3 may be used to separate PP and LDPE from HDPE."
- Interpretation: A concrete, implementable method for building a polyolefin film recycle stream — directly usable for backsheet/offcut recovery.
### Viewpoint 4 — Reprocessing creates gels that burst films

- Page: p.372
- Quote: > "Loosely crosslinked regions (known as 'gels') can act as stress concentrations in film and cause 'blow-outs' in bottles made from recycled HDPE."
- Interpretation: The central failure mode of recycled PE: antioxidant depletion during remelting permits crosslinking → gels → weak spots. This is the root cause of "fish-eyes" / web breaks in recycled backsheet.
### Viewpoint 5 — Pigments can catalyse degradation

- Page: p.372
- Quote: > "green pigments are usually based on chromium(III) oxide, which can readily catalyse the thermooxidative degradation of HDPE when present even in trace quantities."
- Interpretation: Colourant choice matters for recycle stability; a "natural" recycled stream free of chromic pigments is explicitly pursued (p.372) — relevant when specifying tinted hygiene films.
### Viewpoint 6 — Biodegradable polymers as an end-of-life option

- Page: p.375
- Quote: > "Polymer types being studied include cellulose derivatives (such as cellulose acetate) [70], polysaccharides such as chitin [71], starch and poly(3-hydroxybutrate) [72]."
- Interpretation: Names the bio-based candidates (cellulose acetate, starch, PHB, chitin) for flushable/degradable hygiene films; the chapter treats degradability as a functional design route, not just disposal.

## 4. Argumentation Process

The chapter argues by levels of value: (1) prevention/collection is cheapest (primary in-plant recycling ideal); (2) once mixed, separation cost dominates, so source-segregation is the lever (p.362); (3) mechanical recycling is downgraded by incompatibility, so compatibilisation helps only in specific cases; (4) chemical recycling recovers feedstock but destroys polymerisation value-added; (5) energy recovery is the last resort with emission liabilities. The contaminant chemistry (§13.6) then explains why recycled PE underperforms: antioxidant consumption → crosslinking/pigment-catalysed degradation → gels/black specks. The biodegradable-polymer section (§13.9) offers an alternative end-of-life logic for short-life films.

## 5. Role in the Book

Ch.13 is the sustainability capstone. It consumes the material science of Ch.8–12: a recycled film's properties are governed by the same orientation, crosslinking, stabilisation and barrier principles already developed, but now complicated by contamination and degradation history. For hygiene R&D it links to Ch.9 (barrier/packaging), Ch.10 (stabilisation/UV), Ch.12 (testing of recycled film) and Ch.8 (surface treatment of recycled webs).

## 6. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths:
- Quantitative, actionable sorting data: "Water may be used to separate PP, LDPE and HDPE from PS, PVC and PET" (p.364); NIR "identification can be achieved within 0.2 s" (p.366).
- Honest contaminant mechanics: "black specks … are small areas of highly degraded polymer that have been carbonised owing to excessive residence time in an extruder" (p.372).

⚠️ Limitations:
- Film-specific recycling is thin; most data are bottles/containers. Films' large surface area = high contamination (p.359) yet receive less method detail.
- Chemical recycling still "developmental and have not yet proven themselves sustainable in a competitive market" (p.359).
- Energy recovery accused of "undesirable emissions" (dioxins/furans, p.361) — handled by modern high-T operation but not eliminated.

## 7. Key Terms / Concepts

- Primary recycling: (p.358) "recycling of clean, uncontaminated, single-type waste … most popular as it ensures simplicity and low cost."
- Secondary recycling: (p.358) separation + remelt "as a mixture without segregation" → downgraded products.
- Quaternary recycling: (p.360) "recovery of the energy content of plastic wastes" (incineration/EFW).
- Float-sink separation: (p.364) density-based resin separation via liquids of tuned density.
- Gels (in PCR): (p.372) "Loosely crosslinked regions … can act as stress concentrations in film and cause 'blow-outs'."
- Black specks: (p.372) carbonised degraded polymer from extruder residence-time hotspots.
- Biodegradable polymers: (p.375) cellulose derivatives, chitin, starch, PHB.
## 8. Micro close-reading

Paragraph (p.372, §13.6.2, reprocessing crosslinking):
> "Recycling of HDPE PCR (usually milk bottles) by melt extrusion can lead to crosslinking during the thermal reprocessing stage since the antioxidant added initially (during manufacture of the polymer) is consumed [59]. Loosely crosslinked regions (known as 'gels') can act as stress concentrations in film and cause 'blow-outs' in bottles made from recycled HDPE. Another common source of contamination in recycled HDPE (as well as virgin HDPE) is 'black specks'."

- Sentence 1 — Cause: remelting consumes antioxidant → crosslinking initiates. Technique: melt-extrusion mechanism.
- Sentence 2 — Effect: gels = stress concentrators → blow-outs (film/bottle failure). Keyword: stress concentration.
- Sentence 3 — Secondary defect: black specks (carbonised hotspots).
- Logic: stabiliser depletion → network formation → mechanical flaw. This is the precise reason recycled polyolefin film needs fresh stabiliser and careful residence-time control.
💡 Standout: "Loosely crosslinked regions (known as 'gels') can act as stress concentrations in film and cause 'blow-outs'" — the sentence that turns an abstract "recycled = lower quality" complaint into a specific, preventable failure mechanism.
## 9. Concept deep-dive

### 9.1 The four-tier recycling hierarchy

- Definition (p.358): primary (clean single-stream) → secondary (mechanical, separated or remelted mixed) → tertiary (chemical/feedstock) → quaternary (energy).
- Operational? Conceptual; secondary operationalised by the separation methods in §13.3.
- Usage: frames every subsequent section; the conclusion (p.362) stresses collection/sorting as the gating step.
- Relation: mirrors design-for-environment logic the hygiene formulator must adopt for film packaging.
### 9.2 Reprocessing degradation of polyolefins (gels, black specks, pigment catalysis)

- Definition (p.372): antioxidant depletion → crosslinking (gels) and carbonisation (black specks); chromic-green pigments catalyse thermooxidation.
- Operational? Yes — observed in melt-extrusion PCR trials; microscopy confirms.
- Usage: explains downgrading of recycled LDPE/HDPE film; dictates re-stabilisation and colour-stream control.
- Relation: connects to Ch.10 stabilisation (HALS/antioxidants) — recycled film is the "aged polymer" case.
### 9.3 Chemical recycling of condensation polymers (PET)

- Definition (p.367–368): PET glycolysis/methanolysis → oligomers or DMT; supercritical-water hydrolysis gives ~99% pure TPA.
- Operational? Yes — lab/pilot demonstrated; glycolysis feeds polycondensation or unsaturated-polyester synthesis.
- Usage: keeps PET value-added vs landfilling; limited to well-defined single streams.
- Relation: step-growth polycondensation in reverse — the polyester chemistry of Ch.9 packaging.
### 9.4 Biodegradable polymers

- Definition (p.375): cellulose acetate, chitin, starch, PHB; UV-degradable ethylene–CO copolymer and modified PET as "litter" solutions.
- Operational? Research/commercial niches; agriculture mulching films cited (p.375).
- Usage: alternative end-of-life for short-life films where collection is impractical.
- Relation: natural/step-growth polysaccharides & bacterial polyesters — relevant to flushable wipe substrates.
## 10. Academic critique & open questions

- Film gap: separation methods are bottle-centric; the high surface-area, highly-contaminated nature of films (p.359) is acknowledged but under-solved.
- Economic realism: chemical recycling "remains developmental" (p.359); the chapter is more descriptive than cost-modelled.
- Emission trade-off: energy recovery reduces landfill/ GHG vs landfill (p.361) yet dioxin/furan formation during flue-gas cooling is admitted unresolved (p.361).
- Open Q1: Can re-stabilisation + controlled residence time push recycled LDPE/HDPE backsheet to virgin-equivalent blown-film performance?
- Open Q2: For flushable hygiene films, do cellulose-acetate/starch/PHB blends meet both disintegration and skin-safety/performance specs better than oxo-degradable PE?
## 11. Hygiene-material relevance & action cards

Relevance to diaper / wipe / absorbent-core R&D: ★★★★★ (film waste, recycled backsheet, eco-positioning and design-for-recycling are core sustainability levers).

ACTION CARD 1 — Build a polyolefin float-sink recovery loop for film offcuts
- Situation: Backsheet/laminate converting generates LDPE/LLDPE/HDPE trim and rejects.
- Apply: Use density float-sink (p.364): water separates PE/PP from PET/PVC/PS; ~930 kg/m³ liquid splits PP/LDPE from HDPE. Route PE stream back as PCR content.
- Expected effect: Closed-loop scrap recovery, lower tipping fees, verifiable recycled content.

ACTION CARD 2 — Re-stabilise and de-gel recycled PE for backsheet
- Situation: Specifying PCR polyolefin in backsheet or film packaging.
- Apply: Counter the p.372 mechanism — add fresh antioxidant/HALS before remelt, control extruder residence time, screen for gels/black specks; target "natural" (unpigmented/chromic-free) stream.
- Expected effect: Avoid blow-outs/web breaks and fish-eyes; meet Ch.12 mechanical/visual specs on recycled web.

ACTION CARD 3 — Evaluate biodegradable polymers for eco hygiene films
- Situation: Developing flushable wipes or compostable outer wraps.
- Apply: Screen cellulose acetate, starch, PHB/chitin (p.375) and UV-degradable PE analogues; pair with Ch.12 biodegradation/weathering tests.
- Expected effect: Credible end-of-life claim; avoid greenwashing by choosing polymer matched to disposal pathway.

ACTION CARD 4 — Design film packaging for sortability
- Situation: Choosing materials/labels for hygiene product outer film.
- Apply: Apply SPI resin code logic (p.362) and NIR-readable composition (p.366); avoid PVC/PET contamination of the PE stream; minimise paper labels/adhesives that blind sorters.
- Expected effect: Higher resale value of separated scrap; cleaner PCR feed.
