PREFACE

The science of polymer chemistry has shown excellent at creating a broad variety of polymeric materials suitable for a wide range of uses. The molecular architecture of such substances is stunning, but the human eye cannot see atoms, thus it frequently goes unrecognized. To address this issue and provide a window into the physical and mechanical features of these complex systems, molecular modelling is a useful tool.

Contrasting the modelling often used in the pharmaceutical sector is the field of polymer modelling. Protein homology modelling, protein-ligand docking, and computer design of combinatorial libraries are all typical in the pharmaceutical modelling industry nowadays due to the enormous resources required to bring a novel medication to market. This is intriguing because whereas synthetic polymers typically consist of a few monomers, the average protein is a polymer of up to 20 monomers (amino acids).

Therefore, synthetic polymer modelling should be theoretically easier than protein modelling, yet its applications have received less attention. The computational screening of new prospective polymers may be carried out before synthesis to check for desired characteristics, due to the fast development of polymer modelling to the point where the modelling is quicker than

the synthesis. So this is the beginning of the era of computer-aided polymer design.

# CHAPTER 1

# Basic principles of polymer chemistry

Polymers are a type of organic or inorganic compounds that include both macromolecules and their smaller chemical building blocks, monomers. Cellulose, proteins, and nucleic acids are all examples of polymers found in living creatures. Concrete, plastics, paper, glass, and rubbers are all derived from these elements, as are naturally occurring minerals like quartz, diamond, and feldspar.

The number of the monomer units is not defined by the term polymer. The term "high polymer" is occasionally used to describe a compound with a very high number of monomers. Polymers are not limited to using monomers with the same molecular weight & structure as themselves. The monomers that make up certain naturally occurring polymers are all the same. However, most polymers, both natural and manmade, are copolymers, meaning that they are composed of two or more distinct kinds of monomers.

## 1.1. Historical development of polymer chemistry

Possibly the oldest significant study in polymer science dates back to 1811 when Henri Braconnot began his

groundbreaking research on the derivative cellulose compounds. Rubber, the first widely used semi-synthetic polymer, was made more durable with the advent of vulcanization in the latter half of the nineteenth century. Bakelite, the first synthetic polymer, was developed in 1907 by Leo Baekeland via the controlled reaction of phenol & formaldehyde. In 1909, bakelite was released to the general public.

The precise knowledge of the polymer molecular structure wasn't developed until the 1920s, despite substantial progress in polymer synthesis and characterisation. Before that time, scientists relied on a notion called the association theory, which postulated that polymers were clumps of microscopic molecules (called colloids) held jointly by an unknown force.

Polymers, first hypothesized in 1922 by Hermann Staudinger, are lengthy chains of atoms linked together by covalent bonds, a theory that did not become widely accepted for nearly a decade. Polymers might also be rationally manufactured from their component monomers, as shown by "Wallace Carothers's work" in the 1920s.

The "Ziegler-Natta catalyst", developed by German scientist Karl Ziegler and Italian chemist Giulio Natta, was awarded the 1963 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for its significant contribution to synthetic polymer research. The polymer pioneer Paul Flory was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1974 for his considerable contributions to the field.

The "kinetics of addition polymerization" including "step-growth polymerization", excluded volume, chain transfer, Flory convention and Flory-Huggins solution theory, were all explored in the context of polymers.

There is now a thriving polymer sector thanks to synthetic polymer products including nylon, Teflon, polyethylene, and silicone. Rational polymer synthesis has also made great strides in recent years. Most useful polymers on the market today are totally man-made using organic synthesis processes that can be efficiently scaled to industrial production levels.

Today, synthetic polymers are used in almost every sector of modern society. From playground equipment to airplanes, polymers may be found in a variety of different applications. They have found use in several medical contexts, most notably in implanted devices and the administration of drugs under tight control.

Polymers like poly(methyl methacrylate) are utilized in the semiconductor industry as photoresist materials and in the fabrication of high-performance microprocessors as low-k dielectrics. "Organic light-emitting diodes" (OLEDs) for electronic display have recently been developed using polymers as flexible substrates.

# 1.2. Monomers, polymers, repeating units, functionality.

## 1.2.1. Monomer

A monomer is a small molecule of a substance that may

mix with others of its kind to produce much larger molecules. These compounds are mostly organic. A monomer's ability to create chemical connections with at least two other monomers is its defining characteristic. Higher functionality monomers result in cross-linked, network polymers, whereas bifunctional monomers could only form linear, chainlike polymers.

Styrene, caprolactam (which copolymerizes to make nylon-6), butadiene, & acrylonitrile (which copolymerize to generate Buna N or nitrile rubber), and other monomers with double bonds between the two atoms or rings with three to seven atoms are instances of the monomers that undergo addition processes.

Condensation polymerizations are common among monomers with multiple reactive atomic groupings; for instance, a compound which is both the alcohol and the acid can undergo iterative ester formation including t alcohol group of one molecule with the acid group of the next molecule, resulting in the long-chain polyester. Nylon-6 is also synthesized when hexamethylenediamine, which has two amine groups, reacts with adipic acid, which has two acid groups.

· When monomers link together, they may create larger structures like polymers.

· Monomers may be broken down into four categories: sugars, fatty acids, amino acids, and nucleotides.

· All of these monomers are essential to the maintenance and expansion of life, and they can all be produced in an inorganic environment.

· Monomers are abundant in the void between stars, in the nebulae, and in the chondritic meteorites.

# Monomers in Nature

Carbon-containing monomers are used to make natural polymers because this element forms strong chemical connections. Dehydration synthesis is one-way nature that makes polymers; it involves joining molecules but also involves removing a water molecule. On the other side, hydrolysis is a technique for decomposing polymers into their component monomers.

This happens when enzymes and water work together to break links between individual monomers. Enzymes are big molecules that function as catalysts, hastening chemical processes. Amylase, which breaks down starch into sugar, is an enzyme utilized to catalyze this monomerization process. This is a step in the digestive process. Natural polymers are also used to stabilize and thicken substances like food and medication. The following are a few further instances of natural polymers:

· Collagen.

· DNA.

· Keratin.

· Wool.

· Rubber.

# · Simple Sugar Monomers

Monosaccharides, or simple sugars, are a kind of monomer. To put it simply, monosaccharides are made up of "carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen". Carbohydrates are chemicals present in food that can store energy for later use, and their building blocks are monomers that can link together to create polymers. Glucose is a monomer that consists of twelve hydrogens, six carbons, and six oxygens, with the chemical formula $C_6H_{12}O_6$. Glucose is the primary fuel for animals and is produced by plants mostly via photosynthesis.

Cellular respiration relies on glucose. Many carbohydrates have glucose as their building block. Galactose and fructose, two other common simple sugars, have the same chemical formula but are structural isomers of glucose. Simple sugars like ribose, arabinose, and xylose make up the pentoses.

Polysaccharides and disaccharides are formed when sugar monomers are joined together. Sucrose, or table sugar, is a kind of disaccharide that forms when glucose and fructose are combined. Lactose (milk sugar) & maltose (a cellulose byproduct) are two more examples of disaccharides.

Starch, a massive polysaccharide composed of numerous monomers, is the plant kingdom's primary energy storage compound and is insoluble in water. The glucose molecule serves as the building block for an enormous amount of starch. Seeds, cereals, and many other plant and animal

foods include starch. The protein amylase breaks down starch so that it may be broken down into glucose.

Animals store energy in the form of the carbohydrate glycogen. Glycogen also uses glucose as its basis monomer, like starch. The more branched structure of glycogen sets it apart from starch. Glycogen may be hydrolyzed back into glucose whenever cells want an energy boost.

Plants all around the globe share the structural component cellulose, the linear, flexible polysaccharide made up of long chains of glucose monomers. At least half of the carbon on Earth is stored in cellulose. Except for ruminants and termites, few creatures can completely digest cellulose.

The shells of many species, including insects and crustaceans, are fashioned from chitin, a polysaccharide that is more brittle than starch. Thus, simple sugar monomers like glucose are essential to the functioning of living organisms and provide the necessary energy for their maintenance.

# · Monomers of Fats

Lipids, which include fats, are a class of hydrophobic (water-repellent) polymers. Glycerol, a 3-carbon alcohol with two hydroxyl groups, is the primary monomer of fatty acids. Fats are a more efficient source of energy than glucose, the body's natural sugar. This is why animals need lipids as a kind of energy storage. Diacylglycerols,

often known as phospholipids, are a kind of fat that contains two fatty acids and one glycerol. Triacylglycerols, often known as fats and oils, are a kind of lipid that has three fatty acid tails & one glycerol. Insulation of the nervous system and the cell membranes is another function of fats.

# ● Amino Acids: Monomers of Proteins

Amino acids are the building blocks of protein, a common polymer in the natural world. Therefore, a protein's building block is an amino acid. Glucose is converted into an amino acid by adding an amine ($NH_3$), a carboxyl (COOH), and the side chain (R). 20 amino acids may be combined in different ways to form proteins.

Proteins serve several purposes in the bodies of living things. A protein is made up of multiple monomers of amino acids joined together by peptide bonds. Dipeptides consist of two chemically-bonded amino acids. A tripeptide is a chain of three amino acids, while a tetrapeptide is a chain of four amino acids. Polypeptides is the standard term for proteins consisting of more than four amino acids. Among these 20 amino acids, glucose with both carboxyl & amine groups is found among the base monomers. That's why you may think of glucose as a "protein monomer," too.

The fundamental structure is a chain of amino acids, while the secondary structures, alpha helices & beta-pleated sheets, are formed by hydrogen bonding. Tertiary

structural folding of amino acids into functional proteins. Complex quaternary structures, like collagen, are created by further folding & bending. The structural basis of animals is collagen. Keratin is the protein responsible for the skin, hair, and feathers of animals.

Enzymes are a kind of protein that catalyzes chemical processes in living organisms. Proteins act as messengers and transporters of substances between cells. For most species, the protein actin serves as a transporter. Proteins serve distinct purposes due to their unique three-dimensional configurations.

When the protein structure is altered, the protein's function also changes. Genes direct the production of proteins inside a cell. Amino acids, the protein's building blocks, are fueled by glucose and are responsible for the protein's interactions and diversity.

# · Nucleotides as Monomers

Proteins are built from amino acids, which in turn are assembled from nucleotides. Information and energy are both transferred and stored in nucleotides. Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) & ribonucleic acid (RNA) are natural linear polymers made up of nucleotides. The genetic code is stored in DNA and RNA. A phosphate, five-carbon sugar, and nitrogenous base are the building blocks of the nucleotide monomers. The purine bases adenine in addition to guanine, plus the pyrimidine bases cytosine and thymine (in DNA) or uracil (in RNA), make up the remainder of the bases.

The sugar and nitrogenous base combination produces distinct results. Many essential compounds in living systems are based on nucleotides. Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is an example of the main energy transport mechanism in living things. ATP molecules are composed of adenine, ribose, and 3 phosphate groups. The sugars in the nucleic acids are linked together by phosphodiester bonds. With their negative charges, the macromolecule produced by these bonds is ideal for storing genetic information. The sugar ribose, together with the nucleotides guanine, cytosine, adenine, and uracil, make up RNA. RNA acts as an enzyme and contributes to the synthesis of proteins and the replication of DNA. RNA is found in the single-helix structure.

Due to its greater stability in the double helix structure, DNA is by far the most common polynucleotide in cells. The nucleotide basis of DNA is comprised of sugar deoxyribose and 4 nitrogenous bases adenine, cytosine, guanine, and thymine. Due to its durability and length, DNA can store an enormous quantity of data. The nucleotide monomers that make up DNA and RNA, in addition to the energy molecule ATP, are largely responsible for the persistence of life on the Earth.

# · Monomers for Plastic

Poly(vinyl alcohol) is made up of several individual units that are vinyl alcohol. Children's putty typically contains this component. Carbon atoms divide aromatic rings in the polycarbonate monomers. Glasses and compact discs

often employ polycarbonate as their material. The polyethylene monomers used to make polystyrene (the stuff used to make Styrofoam and other foam insulation materials) have an aromatic ring replaced by a hydrogen atom. Several monomers of chloroethene combine to make poly(chloroethene), often known as poly(vinyl chloride) or PVC. PVC is used in the construction of vital components including pipelines and siding. The uses for plastics in common household goods are almost unlimited:

· Car headlights.

· Paint.

· Food containers.

· Fabric.

· Pipes.

· Medical equipment.

# Types of Monomers

There are six main building blocks of macromolecules in biology, each present in different proportions and configurations. Sulfur, oxygen, phosphorus, carbon, nitrogen, and hydrogen are all examples of these substances.

When monomers join together to create a polymer, one of the by-products is the water molecule. The term "dehydration synthesis" describes this process.

However, the addition of a single water molecule may cause polymers to dissolve. This is referred to as hydrolysis when it occurs.

The four most common macromolecules may be broken down into their constituent monomers as follows:

# 1. Carbohydrates Consist Of Monosaccharides

Depending on the number of monomers present in the macromolecule, carbohydrates are further classified as monosaccharides, disaccharides, or polysaccharides.

● Carbohydrates are polymers made up of smaller building blocks called monosaccharides. Galactose, Glucose, and fructose are all types of monosaccharides.

● Two monosaccharides combine to form a disaccharide. The sugars lactose and sucrose are two instances of disaccharides. The monosaccharides glucose $u$ galactose react to form lactose. Milk is a common source of this substance. When glucose and fructose are mixed, sucrose results. Table sugar may also be referred to as sucrose.

● Three or more monosaccharides may combine to form a polysaccharide. Several distinct kinds of monosaccharides may combine to form a polysaccharide chain.

# 2. Proteins Consist Of Amino Acids

Proteins are the other macromolecule class.

● Amino acids are the monomers from which proteins are constructed. Carbon is connected with

the amino group ($NH_2$), the group of carbohydrates (-COOH), hydrogen, and the other atom or group known as the R group to form an amino acid molecule.

· There are 20 distinct amino acids found in proteins, and each one has a unique R group. The chemical properties and three-dimensional structures of amino acids range from acidic to polar to helical to zigzag. Changes in the protein function and structure are the consequence of subtle differences in the amino acid sequences.

· Peptide bonds connect successive amino acids in the polypeptide chain.

# 3. Nucleic Acids Consist Of Nucleotides

Nucleic acids are polymers made up of smaller molecules called nucleotides.:

Nucleic acids are subsets of biological macromolecules formed when nucleotides form polynucleotide chains. One pentose sugar, One nitrogenous base, & one phosphate group make up each nucleotide.

Organic compounds that include nitrogen atoms in one or two rings are known as nitrogenous bases. There are four nitrogenous bases in DNA and RNA. All three of these bases (cytosine, adenine, and guanine) are present in DNA and RNA. DNA is the only place to find thymine, whereas RNA is the only place to find uracil.

The sugar pentose has five carbons in its molecule.

Nucleotides include pentose sugar ribose in the RNA & deoxyribose in DNA. The name "deoxyribose" refers to the fact that, unlike ribose, deoxyribose does not have the hydroxyl group (-OH) on its 2' carbon.

# 4. Lipids

Last but not least, lipids. However, lipids aren't "true polymers" and must be treated as such.

Fats and glycerol are the building blocks of certain lipids. Long hydrocarbon chains that finish with the carboxyl group define fatty acids. Glycerides are produced when glycerol is combined with fatty acids.

· Monoglycerides are composed of one molecule of fatty acid and one molecule of glycerol.

· A diglyceride is a triglyceride, which is made up of two fatty acid molecules and one glycerol molecule.

· Triglycerides, which consist of the three fatty acid molecules connected to the glycerol molecule, are the primary structural components of the human body fat.

# Examples of Monomers

Many different types of monomers may be utilized to illustrate the transformation from monomers to polymers. Some common monomers are included below to illustrate the procedure:

· Amino acids, like glutamate, tryptophan or alanine. Proteins are polymers, and their building

blocks are amino acids. There are 20 amino acids, and they all have somewhat distinct chemical structures and side chains. Peptide bonds connect amino acids to the polypeptide chains, which may subsequently be folded into proteins.

· Nucleotides (adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), cytosine (C), and uracil (U)): Nucleic acids like DNA and RNA are generated from monomers called nucleotides. Sugar, phosphate, and the nitrogenous base are the three components that make up the nucleotide. Phosphodiester bonds are necessary for the nucleotides to link together to create a DNA or RNA strand.

· Monosaccharides: Starches, sugars, and cellulose are all constructed from monosaccharides. Sugars that only have one carbon ring, with hydrogen and oxygen connected, are called monosaccharides. Instances of monosaccharides include fructose, glucose, and galactose. Glycosidic linkages allow monosaccharides to combine into polysaccharides.

# The functionality of a monomer

A number of the bonds that the repeating unit of a monomer generates in the polymer with other monomers is the IUPAC definition of the monomer's functionality.

For example, propene, styrene, ethene, and acrylonitrile all have one functionality, but adipic acid, ethylene glycol, and hexamethylene diamine all have two.

# 1.2.2. Polymers

A polymer consists of long, repeating chains of molecules that are chemically linked together. Polymers are versatile building blocks because their structure makes them adaptable to a wide range of purposes.

Polymers may be synthesized in a lab or found in nature. For instance, rubber has indeed been used for thousands of years and is a natural polymeric material. Due to a naturally occurring molecular polymer chain, it has remarkable elasticity. Shellac, a resin extracted from the lac insect native to India and Thailand, is the other natural polymer that has several applications in the paint industry.

Cellulose, an organic molecule found in plant cell walls, is the most abundant natural polymer on Earth. It is a key ingredient in the manufacturing of textiles, paper, and even cellophane.

Polyethylene (the most common plastic in the world, used for everything from shopping bags to storage containers) and polystyrene (the substance used to produce packing peanuts and throwaway cups) are two examples of man-made or synthetic polymers. Thermoplastic synthetic polymers can be shaped and molded easily, whereas thermoset polymers maintain their shape even when heated. Still, others are similar in appearance to natural or artificial fibres (elastomers or synthetic fibres). You may find these materials in everything from swimwear to cookware.

# Properties

Polymers may be tailored to make use of certain beneficial qualities depending on their intended application. Among them are:

· Reflectivity: Certain polymers are used to create reflective film, which has several applications in the field of lighting technology.

· Impact Resistance: Strong polymers that don't break easily are ideal for things like suitcases, computer cases, automobile bumpers, and more.

· Brittleness: Some varieties of polystyrene may be easily shaped by applying heat despite being rigid and brittle.

· Translucence: Polymer clay and other transparent polymers are popular crafting materials.

· Ductility: Ductile polymers may be bent without breaking, unlike their brittle counterparts. Ductility is a hallmark of metals like aluminium, gold, and steel. Although not as robust as the other polymers, ductile polymers have various applications.

· Elasticity: The elastic qualities of both natural and synthetic rubbers make them perfect for use in automobile tires and other comparable applications.

# Polymerization

Synthetic polymers may be made by a process called polymerization, which involves the chaining together of the monomer molecules through covalent bonds. Polymerization may occur in two primary ways: step growth & chain growth. Chain growth polymerization differs from graft polymerization in that it involves the addition of monomer molecules to the chain rather than graft polymerization. Step-growth polymerization involves the direct bonding of several monomer molecules.

Close examination of the polymer chain reveals that its molecular structure and characteristics are very similar to those of the polymer itself. For instance, if the links between monomers in the polymer chain are tightly twisted & difficult to break, the resulting polymer is likely to be robust and durable. However, the polymer is likely to exhibit pliability if the chain of molecules comprising it has stretchy qualities.

# · Cross-Linked Polymers

Most thermoplastics and other polymers have molecular chains which can be disassembled and reassembled. The use of heat allows most common polymers to be reshaped. They are also recyclable. For instance, plastic drink bottles may be recycled into everything from new bottles to carpets to fleece coats.

However, once the connection between the molecules of the cross-linked polymer is broken, a polymer cannot

reform. Cross-linked polymers are often stronger, more rigid, thermally stable, and harder than uncross-linked polymers.

The term "resin" or "thermoset resin" refers to cross-linked polymers used most often in FRP (Fiber Reinforced Polymer) composite goods. Vinyl ester, Polyester, and epoxy are the most widely used polymers for composites.

# 1.3. Nomenclature of polymers.

There has never been a time when standardized nomenclature for describing chemical structures in print and online was more crucial. Both IUPAC1a,b and CAS2 (Chemical Abstracts Service) advocate for the same things in their respective fields of chemistry. The essentials are laid forth with references to the source materials. The "IUPAC purple book" has further information.

It is important to note that polymer & macromolecule are not interchangeable concepts. Macromolecules form the building blocks of a polymer. The latter often have a dispersed distribution of molar masses (in g mol-1), which is denoted by dispersion (Đ). Specifically, Đ = Mm/Mn is the formula for the ratio of a "mass-average molar mass" to the "number-average molar mass". Italic font is used for physical quantity or variable symbols, whereas Roman font is used for unit or label symbols.

Nomenclature for polymers often refers to idealized depictions; little variations in the structure are disregarded. You may give a polymer one of two names.

When a monomer's source has to be determined, source-based nomenclature may be utilized. When the polymer structure is established, however, one may switch to a more precise structure-based nomenclature. Some more conventional names may also be used if no misunderstanding is likely to arise.

No matter the technique, all names for polymers begin with the prefix poly and are then enclosed in parentheses. In this case, the brackets go like this: [()]. Positions of structural elements such as poly(4- chlorostyrene) are denoted by locants. Poly(chlorostyrene) is the polymer, but polychlorostyrene might be a tiny, multi-substituted molecule, therefore the enclosing marks aren't needed if a source-based name is a single word with no locants. The prefixes - and - are often used to denote end-groups; for instance, $\alpha$-chloro-$\omega$-hydroxy-polystyrene.

Some polymers are given names that refer to their country of origin, such as "rubber from Brazil" for the natural rubber, or the Hevea brasiliensis. Bakelite, the condensation polymer of phenol and formaldehyde was introduced to the market in 1905 and is named after its inventor, Leo Baekeland. # 2.2. Inhibitors and retarders – functions and examples

## 2.2.1. Inhibitors

The extremely exothermic and self-accelerating nature of polymerization processes has resulted in several disastrous incidents in the past. The cooling system's inability to keep up with the heat produced by these unchecked runaway reactions causes them to accelerate on their own.

Reckless boiling of the fluids in a reaction system or the formation of vapour from the secondary reactions are only two examples of the potentially disastrous outcomes that might occur if appropriate precautions are not taken to dampen this auto-accelerative character. Over-pressurization and a subsequent thermal explosion might be the end result of both of these issues. To avoid this danger, inhibitors are introduced into a reaction system before the start of the polymerization process in industrial settings.

Endothermic processes, those in which heat is removed from the system, are often easier to regulate than exothermic reactions, those in which heat is produced. In process industries, the polymerization processes are often exothermic. Internal jackets and cooling coils are used to regulate the amount of heat emitted in the reactor under normal operating circumstances. A self-sustaining, uncontrolled runaway reaction might develop if the rate of heat creation exceeds the rate of heat removal and also a control of a reaction temperature is lost.

This is because when the temperature rises as a result of the exothermic reaction, so does the rate of the reaction. Polymerization processes also enhance viscosity, which dampens the transfer of heat and the diffusion of inhibitors. Inhibition approaches alter the chemistry of the process itself, slowing down the heat-producing reaction mechanism, while mitigation & control methods assist eliminate the heat created during the reaction.

Venting, confinement, combined venting and containment, and reaction inhibition are only some of the methods used to rein in uncontrolled reactions. Due to the perceived risk of uncontrolled laboratory research, response inhibition is one of the least studied of these methods. To prevent a reactor from continuing on a runaway trajectory, inhibitors may be injected into the reactor in minute doses. To buy time for a quick response, the chemical used may either completely halt the chemical process (by acting as the inhibitor) or slow down the reaction rate.

Inhibitors may operate in several distinct ways:

● A modest quantity of sulfides, for example, may deactivate platinum on the carbon catalysts, halting the process.

● By neutralizing the spreading chains' active terminals, they may stop a chain reaction in its tracks.

● They may halt the reaction by introducing an alternative, less energetic reaction route involving any of the reactants.

The following substances are effective inhibitors:

· Radicals that have been stabilized such that they may react with the radicals present in a polymerization system but not with monomers themselves. The chemical compound diphenyl picrylhydrazyl $(C_6H_5)2NNC_6H_2(NO_2)_3$ is an example of a substance in this category.

· Chemicals that react with the primary or the polymer radicals to form less reactive radical metabolites.

· Substances capable of acting as transfer agents notwithstanding radical displacement reactions' inability to be restarted by their products.

Substances that are often recognized as having inhibitory characteristics include:

· Nitro-compounds.

· Sulfur-based chemicals, with thiazine as the primary example.

· Nitrogen-based chemicals (nitroso, azo, diazo, etc.).

· Quinone-based aromatic molecules need oxygen to be activated.

· Initiators can be found in just a select group of metal salts and complexes.

# 2.3. Auto acceleration, thermal & electrochemical polymerization

## 2.3.1. Auto acceleration

Whenever the monomer concentration is excessive and the conversion approaches a critical threshold, an autoacceleration of the polymerization rate is often seen in the free-radical polymerizations. The kinetics of the free-radical polymerization should be first-order in relation to the concentration of the monomer.

This is seen over a large range of polymerization in most vinyl polymers. However, at a certain conversion, the polymerization of certain monomers deviates significantly from the first-order kinetics. The "Trommsdorff or Norish-Smith effect", named after the researchers who made significant contributions to this subject, is characterized by a dramatic rise in polymerization rate as well as molecular weight. Methyl acrylate, Methyl methacrylate, and Acrylic acid at varying concentrations all have this effect to varying degrees. This phenomenon is not exclusive to styrenes; it also happens with vinyl acetate and other monomers, although to a lesser extent.

Autoacceleration may be seen even under isothermal settings and does not need an external initiator. In reality, autoacceleration causes a significant rise in temperature if the process is exothermic.

In purely thermal polymerization, heat is the only energy

# 3.3. Counter ions

Mixtures of elements that have been chemically combined are called compounds. Two or more components may combine to form a compound. These constituents must be present in a predetermined proportion. Different compounds may be made from the same two elements when the bonding ratios are altered.

Despite sharing the same atomic weight and bonding pattern, two materials containing different pairings of the elements would be considered distinct compounds.

Compounds are far more common in nature than free components. Salts are a typical method of chemically binding together different elements. In the rocky interior of our planet, salts are abundant. It takes both the anion and the cation to make up a salt. It is unusual to find a cation by itself. In nature, anions almost seldom occur alone. To cancel out their negative effects, they need countermeasures.

Try to picture the salt formed when two atoms combine. Sodium and fluorine atoms have joined forces. A free electron is taken by the fluorine atom from the sodium atom. There is now the fluoride anion and the sodium cation. Attractive forces between the ions' opposing charges keep them bound. Salt has been created.

· Both anions and cations exist in pairs.

· The cation charges must equal those of the anion.

Sodium chloride, for instance, is an often-used example of an ionic compound. It's what makes up most of the salt on your kitchen table. Sodium chloride is made up of positively charged sodium ions and negatively charged chloride ions. The negative charge of the chlorides is neutralized by the positive charge of the sodium, and vice versa.

Na++Cl- = NaCl (no overall charge)

Other ionic compounds also develop in this fashion, with the result being an electrically neutral compound. The positive charges of the magnesium and the negative

charges of the oxygen cancel each other out when magnesium oxide develops.

$Mg^{2+}+O_2-=MgO$ (no overall charge)

To maintain a stable charge equilibrium, however, a different ratio of sodium oxide will need to develop. For an ideal formation in which the charges perfectly cancel one another, twice as many sodium ions as oxygen ions might be required.

2Na++O₂- = Na₂O (no overall charge)

The two sodium cations and the two oxide anions each have two positive charges, which cancel out two negative charges.

3.4.2. Living Anionic Polymerization

Early chain death and a widening of a "molecular weight  distribution" are the results of the unwanted side reactions  during anionic polymerization, like back-biting or Claisen  reactions involving acrylate chains. 4.1. Step growth polymerization

Condensation polymerization, also known as "step-growth  polymerization", is a method for creating long-chain  polymers from bi- or multifunctional monomers. Since all  monomers are reactive, the majority of them are used up at  the beginning of the process to make the reactive oligomer  chains, which later join to make the lengthy polymer  chains. 5.9. Molecular mass & mechanical properties.

5.9.1. Molecular Mass

The molecular weight of the polymer is a crucial  characteristic. Naturally, each molecule has its unique  molecular weight. It's probably not rocket science to say  that a molecule's molecular weight is one of its most  crucial characteristics.

The importance of molecular weight is magnified when  discussing polymers. The reason is, a polymer is a very big  molecule composed of repeating units, but how many? All  of these scenarios may use the same material, but their  molecular weights and attributes might vary greatly.

The molecular weight of polymers is affected in novel  ways by this variance. Since polymers are constructed  from monomers, the molecular weight of the polymer  chain is directly proportional to the number of monomers  used in its construction.

The ":degree of polymerization" (DP) of the material is  defined as the mean number of monomers in each polymer  chain.

177

Some chains in a particular substance will have more

monomers attached to them, whereas others will have less. Whence this distinction? To begin, the expansion of  polymers is a dynamic process. The reaction of monomers  is essential. What happens if one monomer begins reacting  first, generating a developing chain, and the others don't  follow suit? This chain has a head start, thus it will  eventually be the longest. What if one of the expanding  chains encounters problems and stops adding additional  monomers? Unfortunately, the chain met an untimely end  and would never mature to the same length as the others.

Therefore, the molecular weight used to describe a  polymer is always an average across its constituent  molecules. There will be both longer (and heavier) and  shorter (and lighter) chains present in the material. If you  have a set of numbers, it's always good to know how  dispersed they are.

The dispersity Ð, also known as polydispersity or  polydispersity index, PDI) in polymer chemistry  characterizes the breadth of the range of the molecular  weights. A "polymer sample's dispersity" is typically  between 1 and 2 (but it might be greater than 2).

178

Distributions are more concentrated the closer they are

near 1. A dispersion of 1.0 indicates that all of the chains in  a sample have the same length and have the "same  molecular weight".

Different techniques for determining the molecular weight (or chain length) of the polymer sample inspired the  concept of dispersity. The "average molecular  weight" (denoted by the symbol Mn) was calculated using  one set of techniques. By measuring the total mass of  the sample and tallying up the number of individual  molecules, these techniques might calculate the typical  molecular mass. Colligative characteristics experiments, like a freezing point dip, are archetypes of this method.

You are aware that contaminants in a liquid reduce its  freezing point by disrupting intermolecular connections. You may also be aware that the quantity of the dissolved  molecules or ions determines the degree to which freezing  is reduced. You may calculate the number of molecules in  a polymer by dissolving a known mass of it in solvent and  then measuring the freezing point.

179

In reality, that's not that simple since decreases in freezing

points are negligible. Nowadays, this is a rare occurrence. End group analysis is a typical kind of test used to assess  Mn nowadays. The ratio of one proton in the repetition  units to one proton in an end group is measured using 1H  NMR for end-group analysis.

Keep in mind that the terminal group might be analogous  to the initiator, which joined the initial monomer to begin  polymerization. It is an end group because it persists at the  polymer chain's terminus even after polymerization is  complete. Since there is only one end group per chain and  many monomers enchained inside the polymer, the length  of the chain may be determined by calculating the ratio of  enchained monomers to end group.

The "average molecular weight" (denoted by the symbol  Mw) was calculated using the other set of procedures used  to characterize the dispersity.

The light-scattering experiment is often cited as the  prototypical example. In this experiment, polymer chains  in the solution were subjected to a beam of light and the  resultant scattered light was examined to establish the size  of the polymer chains. Larger molecules in the solution  had a greater impact on the outcomes. Therefore, this

5.9.2. Mechanical properties

Material, and by extension polymer, mechanical response  qualities comprise a broad range of critically essential  physical behaviours shown in response to external stress

184

loads (such as the extent to which the material may be

stretched or bent). The regime of the linear elasticity  describes the initial response of a sample to the relatively  small stresses and the resulting strains, and the sample can  fully recover its original (pre-deformation) size and shape  upon a release of an external loading force.

Similar to metals and other standard crystalline  materials, polymers may be characterized in terms of their  mechanical properties by measuring their moduli of  elasticity and the other classes of strength measurements, like their yield along with tensile strengths. The most  salient mechanical and elastic characteristics of polymeric  materials are briefly discussed here:

Strength: When a force is given that is too great to be  absorbed by simple linear elastic deformation, the strength  of the material is the stress force needed to fracture the  sample under study. Quantities of strength such as tensile  strength (the ability to stretch the polymer), compressional  strength (the ability to compress the polymer), flexural  strength (the ability to bend the polymer), torsional  strength (the ability to twist the polymer), and the impact  strength (the ability to withstand the effects of the direct  hammering) are commonly measured during the  characterization of the materials.

Percent Elongation to Break (Ultimate Elongation): This  value, expressed as a % change in length, is the greatest  strain that a polymer sample can sustain before breaking (at the aforementioned strength point).

185

Young’s Modulus: The overall stiffness of the material

may be estimated by calculating its Young's Modulus, which is defined as the proportion of the stress to  the strain in the linearly elastic response zone of the  material.

The following are examples of physical parameters that  might impact semicrystalline polymers' strength and entire  mechanical properties:

• As a polymer's molecular weight increases, so does  its tensile strength. Polymers with high molecular  weights have high strength values. Their chains are  bulky & entangled with one another, whereas  the polymers with the low molecular weights  have low strength values because their chains are  loosely tied together by the "weak intermolecular  van der Waals interactions".

• Cross-linking the polymer's molecular chains  reduces the freedom of motion between them, making the polymer more robust.

• Since crystalline phases are often characterized by "stronger intermolecular interactions", increasing  the "polymer's degree of crystallinity" may also  improve its strength.

5.10. Size of polymer molecules

Like molecular weight (MW), molecular size is an essential  characteristic of most polymers. The rheological behaviour  of a polymer in the solution is closely related to its  performance as a formulation.
