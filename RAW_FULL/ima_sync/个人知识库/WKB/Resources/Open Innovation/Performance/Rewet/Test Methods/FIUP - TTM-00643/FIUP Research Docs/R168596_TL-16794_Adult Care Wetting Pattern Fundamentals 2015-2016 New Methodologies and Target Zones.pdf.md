ABSTRACT

Target zones are one of the key components for design of DEPEND® and POISE®

absorbent system designs, indicating the most critical location within the product for

absorbent performance.   DEPEND and POISE had invested little in the research for 13-

15 years, resulting in target zones that were outdated or heavily assumed without

validation.   DEPEND and POISE placed eight new studies in 2015 and 2016 to determine  new target zones for many of the current products.  Investment in these wetting pattern  studies resulted in new study/test methodologies and analysis techniques, new target

zone design criteria, and opportunities to improve product performance, optimize cost, and improve protection cue design.   The Wetting Pattern results showed some product  designs (DPU2 male and female) to have adequate absorbent designs, while revealing

deficiencies in surge coverage or overdesigned absorbents in POISE product designs.   It  is strongly recommended to establish a 5-year cadence for update of fundamentals’

research for each priority product form, as well as to establish a trigger for an update

when there are significant changes impacting a product, whether it be the consumer

base or product design changes that could impact fluid handling.

INTRODUCTION

Effective absorbent design requires relevant absorbent design criteria.

Two critical inputs for absorbent design criteria are single insult loadings

and single insult wetting patterns.  Changes in user habits, overall user

populations, significant product changes (fit, absorbent placement,

materials, configuration), and other factors can change these inputs over

time.  Reliance on old inputs or assumptions hinders absorbent designers’

ability to effectively design absorbent systems, creating risks to brand

equity and margins.  Lack of funding and prioritization in POISE and

DEPEND during 2005-2012 led to outdated or missing inputs on almost all

POISE and DEPEND codes as of early 2013.

Prioritization of single insult research in 2013 and lower cost

methodologies resulted in new single insult loadings for 70% of the

DEPEND and POISE line-ups.  TL-15582 describes the efforts to revive

single insult loading research.

Funding for single insult wetting pattern research resumed in 2015.  By this  point, 100% of DEPEND and POISE single insult wetting patterns were

based on 13-15 year old data or assumed for codes that were never tested.   For example, POISE had only tested 2 of 9 pads codes prior to 2015

(Extra & Ultra Plus, TL-6132), extrapolating or interpolating to the 7

untested pad codes.  The absence of new data, scattered documentation, and missing documentation made it increasingly difficult for absorbent

developers to translate the old study data to new product forms and insult  loadings.  The AFC Materials Team eliminated the use of this old data and  assumptions by leading eight new studies from April 2015 to May 2016,

primarily through projects SAFARI and SHOPAD.

This report documents new methodologies, tools, analysis, and target

zones from 2015-2016 single insult wetting pattern research in POISE and  DEPEND.

Absorbent Fundamentals Definitions:

A “wetting pattern” (WP) refers to the location of fluid in product, with a

“Single Insult Wetting Pattern” being that from a single insult.  For

absorbent design purposes, the “Single Insult Wetting Pattern” defines the “Target Zone” (TZ) for the absorbent system, and is a critical input for

absorbent system design, helping define the location for absorbent

materials, and important for evaluating product improvements and/or cost  savings.  This information also helps to define the insult location for test

methods and modeling.

STUDY METHODOLOGY DEVELOPMENT

Consumer vs. Clinical Approaches

Prior single insult wetting pattern studies leveraged local clinical study

resources.  Local recruiting agencies no longer maintain a sufficient pool of  recruits to represent the consumer population.  To overcome this gap,

wetting pattern research is now conducted through consumer study

agencies.

Use of non-local consumer study agencies raised several protocol

questions.  Pilot studies permitted DEPEND and POISE to explore some of  these questions prior to the placement of full-scale studies.  The following  list includes questions leading into the pilot studies (in bold) as well as

additional questions generated throughout this research.

 Can consumers detect occurrence of a single insult?

 Will they abide by a protocol that requires them to remove product  after one insult?

 Are consumer subjects accurate in their reported insult detection?  (Not yet answered – further study required.)

 Do those who can detect the occurrence of an insult belong to a

subset demographic of the incontinent population?  (Not yet

answered – further study required.)

o Does the population of those who can detect an insult have a  different single insult volume than those who cannot detect?  e.g., hypothesis:  those who can detect an insult occurrence have less  severe incontinence than those who cannot detect.

 How much do wetting patterns change during shipment of used  product?

 What packaging protocols ensure no significant absorbent

deformation during shipment?  (Further work still needed for

garments.)

 Can consumers consistently package the product for return?  (Further work still needed for garments.)

 Do those who are willing and able to do special handling (cut

product off and attach to mounting board) differ from the overall

incontinent population?  (Not yet answered – further study required.)

o Does the population of those willing and able to do special

handling/mounting of product differ from the overall incontinent

population?  e.g., hypothesis:  those willing/able to do special

handling tend to be younger and have less severe incontinence.

Can Consumer Study Subjects Detect When A Single Insult Occurs?

The number of insults became a standard question in DEPEND® HUTs

starting in 2013 and POISE® HUTs starting in 2014.   This permitted HUTs  to be used to determine single insult loadings, but also to examine what

percentage of products experience a known number of insults.   Across

eleven Human Use Tests examined across 2013-2017, subjects reported  the number of insults for 71-88% of the products tested.  The others were  unknown.  This suggests a high percentage of situations where subjects

self-reportedly are able to detect their insult occurrences.

Urine Loss Spectrum (ULS) study 34724 (2012) reported a higher

percentage of products with unknown number of insults, but the sample

size was significantly smaller than in the HUTs, and wetting pattern study  protocols are more similar to HUTs than ULS studies.  Adult studies

utilizing wetness sensors (GARBANZO, MAXWELL, etc.) are proposed for  to further explore this question and hopefully validate the methodology

based upon user reported insult occurrences.

How much do wetting patterns change during shipment?

Very little change was observed in wetting patterns before and after

shipment during simulated tests.  In one simulation, garments were

insulted with saline, x-rayed, affixed to a Plexiglas board, sealed in a zip  bag, and placed into rubber bins.  The bin was then shipped in a vehicle 4  days and x-rayed again.   Although some fluid spreading was expected to  occur, x-rays showed no noticeable change in the wetting pattern location  after the 4 days.  This confirmed the packaging protocol for pilot studies.

On another occasion, GOODNITES® products were insulted on a

mannequin, removed, laid out flat and x-rayed, then removed and placed  loosely (not affixed to a board) in a zip bag and tossed around to simulate  handling and shipping.  The products were then x-rayed a second time

after letting sit for a day.  There was no difference between the before and  after x-rays.

What packaging protocols ensure no additional absorbent  deformation during shipment?

Pilot studies revealed no substantial absorbent deformation during shipment. However, the full-size studies revealed issues depending on how the product was affixed to the boards.

● POISE pads were attached to the boards via the pads' garment adhesive. This successfully secured products to the boards when the adhesive was in direct contact with the Plexiglas boards, which required removing protective sheets from the Plexiglas boards before giving them to the agencies. When protective sheets remained on the Plexiglas boards, the products did not adhere well to the boards.

# Can consumers consistently package the product for return?

Pilot studies confirmed that the consumers were able to place the products on boards and in zip bags for return, and return products were not significantly deformed.

Full-sized studies demonstrated that subjects could follow the POISE protocol well. Modifications were required for the DEPEND protocol after the first study, as many garments were not applied consistently to the boards/Velcro (Picture 3). The mounting of the DEPEND products was often skewed and/or off-center, or not properly stretched out resulting in further difficulties analyzing the data. In addition, the product was not

always x-rayed in the same orientation, and/or the product had been worn backwards, adding further to the difficulties in the analysis. The second DEPEND study had fewer issues, but still had issues with skew, off center, or not stretched.

FINAL STUDY METHODOLOGY

Recruiting Criteria

Subjects were recruited based on their absorbency level (Maximum,

Moderate, etc.), product length (regular vs. long pads), gender, and their  awareness of when insults occur (detect insults >50% of the time).

Future Opportunity:  It is recommended to investigate whether awareness  of insult occurrence is accurate, and investigate whether packaging

protocol skews recruit population to younger, less severe incontinence

subjects.  Investigate use of sensors to place a “Single Insult Validation” study.

Recruit/Sample Size

DEPEND Maximum and POISE studies recruited 30 subjects using 4

products per code total of 120 products.  DEPEND garment sizes

(Small/Medium and Large) and POISE lengths (Regular and Long) were

treated as separate codes.

The DEPEND Moderate garments study tested a higher sample size, 8

products per code, to increase the statistical confidence with the low

loadings, and to help generate single insult loading data.  DPU1 and DSU1  loadings were not tested after significant retention capacity reduction in

2014 for DPU1, and DSU1 post-launch.  This provides some direction on  single insult loading data until DEPEND funds new HUTs.

Use Protocol

Subjects wore the products normally until they experienced a single insult, after which they removed the product and packaged it for return.  They

indicated for each product worn whether it had one, two, three or more

insults, or if insult number was unknown.

Daytime vs. night-time use varied amongst the 2015-2016 studies.  This  distinction was not explored in POISE studies in 2015, but the maximum  absorbency DEPEND study explored night-time use.  Subjects were

requested to wear 3 products per code for daytime use and one product  per code for night use.  The moderate absorbency DEPEND study did not  ask for night usage.  Both DEPEND studies included additional questions  pertaining to body position and activity at/during insult.

Return Packaging

The preferred return packaging requirements were different between  POISE and DEPEND, driven by the presence of garment adhesive on  pads to affix samples to the boards.

Pads:  The subject removed the product from their underwear after a

single insult and placed it onto a 6 inch x 16 inch Plexiglas board, adhered  by the pad’s garment adhesive (see below).  Afterwards, they placed it in a  zip bag labeled with a biohazard sticker and returned it to the agency.  The  agency then shipped the products back to IPS and WRE for weights and xrays.

Garments:   Subjects cut open the side seams of the DEPEND underwear, stretched and placed the product on a board (first study board was a

“frame” with an open center; second study was a solid board with adhesive  patch in center), then slid the board into a zip bag, and returned it to the

agency.  The agency then shipped the products back to IPS and WRE for  weights and x-rays.

Future Opportunities:  Plexiglas boards are expensive and were

discovered to be highly variable in weight, thus interfering with x-ray

densitometry analysis.  Alternative board materials should be identified to  reduce the cost and weight variability, as well as suitable for x-ray imaging.  Foam boards were evaluated prior to the final study in 2016, but were

not waterproof enough to leverage in that study.

 Alternatively for DEPEND studies:  if cutting and mounting protocol is a

deterrent for subjects, consider use of “Box Study” protocol to minimize

difficult handling for elderly users.  Used product is placed loosely into a

box lined with a plastic bag that is closed via “twist-tie” or other easy

mechanism, and then box is closed and sealed.

Product Preparation

Currently, it is standard to weigh and record product weight for these

studies.  For garment Wetting Pattern studies, it is recommended to also

measure and record the product rise and the distance of the absorbent

from front and back waist edges.

These key product measurements need to be documented and included in  the analysis (as adjustments if off-spec).  If the rise of the product, and/or

the phasing of the absorbent in a garment product are not per specification, this impacts the wetting pattern results.

 This was seen in two of the codes in the DEPEND Moderate study, and average measurements of leftover product were used to

approximate the impact.

ANALYSIS TOOLS

X-ray Images vs. Rulers

Prior wetting pattern studies determined wetting pattern position and

lengths by visual assessment and rulers.  Applying this approach to the

hundreds of products tested in the 2015 studies would have been too

much of a strain on labor resources and freezer space in the biohazard

labs.   Instead, products were x-ray imaged upon arrival at WRE, allowing  researchers to measure the product in the x-ray image at a later time and  not needing to retain the product for a long period of time.  X-ray image

techniques enable more measurements and higher accuracy than a ruler  and actual product can provide.  For example, x-ray imaging can detect the  highest mass point which may improve estimates of insult locations for

bench tests if anatomical markers are unknown (i.e. female urethra).

Wetting Pattern Analysis Program

An Excel template was developed as a first attempt to measure the wetting  pattern lengths, widths, distances from ends of the pad, and other items of  interest.   While functional, it required knowledge and time to tailor the

template to the different types of products studied and did not reliably

detect POISE wetting patterns for loadings < 14 grams.  This detection

issue was likely due to the interference of the Plexiglas mounting boards, which were later found to be non-uniform in thickness/mass when a similar  issue arose in the DEPEND moderate study.

Analytical Science & Innovation Testing provided a more reliable solution  by modifying an existing imaging analysis program to obtain the

measurements of interest, such as:

 Wetting pattern length, width, and area

 Distance of wetting pattern from the front, back, and sides of the  absorbent

 Distance of the wetting pattern from the nearest edges of the

absorbent

 Centroid of the wetted area (centroid is geometric, not mass)

# X-Ray Settings

X-ray image quality degraded in 2015 due to x-ray tube failure on the TMD unit used in these studies. The replacement tube for that unit did not provide the same power, needing more imaging time to deliver the same contrast. Another x-ray unit had different settings and image quality. More manual interaction in the program was needed to accurately detect the dry areas of the absorbent. Further work is required to determine the optimum x-ray settings and exposure time, setting a standard to ensure

future images have as good of quality and contrast as the 2015 TMD x-ray  unit provided.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis included regression models for most measurements, with the wetting pattern location and size dependent in part on the insult  size.   Single insult and total product loads change over time.   Past

DEPEND studies leveraged regression models, but POISE studies only

leveraged a subset of the available data and required full data re-analysis  as loadings changed over time.   The regression models extend the useful  life of this data by giving researchers the tools needed to predict the

wetting patterns as loads change.

# DEPEND Garments:

## Overall:

Wetting Patterns fit well within both DEPEND underwear maximum and moderate absorbent system designs. Several absorbent system designs were placed in the DEPEND Maximum study, and the DEPEND Moderate study included both PASSAT DPU1 and MIATA DSU1. Results were able to inform POVs on:

· Recommended moving surge phasing forward from 60mm to 30mm from front absorbent edge. Immented after PASSAT launch.

· Cost Transformation recommendations to not reduce surge coverage.

• Informed LCM regarding quality hold for surge placement.

• Informed KCI PASSAT and FIORANO absorbent designs.  Used

Wetting Pattern regressions from similar absorbent designs that were

analyzed in the DEPEND Maximum absorbency study overlaid with

regional single insult estimations.

• Informed Overnight DPU3 Cost Transformation recommendations.

Reviewed overnight data from the DEPEND Maximum absorbency

study to show huge variability of Wetting Patterns that covered almost

all of the absorbent system.  Recommended further night-time data

would be needed to evaluate potential changes.

NOTE:  For garments, it is critical to document Wetting Pattern locations in  relation to the Product Center (rather than the front edge).

Documentation of the actual product rise and absorbent system

placement within the chassis are also key.

 In the past, insult targets were way off because the data was

reported in relation to the absorbent front edge:

 When product designs change – particularly product rise or  absorbent placement or length – the measurements are no

longer valid and significant errors result.

 Measurement from the absorbent edge is an easy and

acceptable way to gather and record data, but it should then  be translated to reference the product center.

 Since body anatomy does not change (though variable), the

product center gives us a good landmark by which to orient the

results.  The measurements can then be compared across sizes,

brands, and designs.

- Higher % SAM → further fluid spreading; longer wetting patterns

CONCLUSIONS

Investment in eight new wetting pattern studies resulted in new study/test  methodologies and analysis techniques, new target zone design criteria, and opportunities to improve product performance, optimize cost, and

improve protection cue design.    Over $3.6MM of cost savings was

proposed that was initiated or informed by this research.

Confirming the hypothesis, wetting patterns changed since historical

studies.    POISE observed longer wetting patterns by 8-48mm.   This

research also confirmed or disproved several assumptions that had been  used for many years in product absorbent design.

Despite being out of date, the data from the DEPEND Wetting Pattern

studies showed that the absorbent systems for the most part covered the  Wetting Patterns.  Surge location for most female products was moved

forward based upon the DEPEND Maximum Absorbency study, and

results were also valuable for informing Cost Transformation projects.  In  addition, the regression data was used in conjunction with regional loading  data to aid in recommendations for international product designs.

Significant learning was gained in regards to methodology, including

handling, shipping, and analysis methods.

RECOMMENDATIONS / NEXT STEPS

Changes in wetting patterns reinforce the need for a regular cadence of

fundamental research, which was also observed in related single insult loading  research in AFC.

1) Establish a plan for updating fundamental data:

It is strongly recommended to establish a 5-year cadence for update of

fundamentals’ research for each priority product form, as well as to establish a  trigger for an update when there are significant changes in the targeted

consumer or product design that could impact fluid handling.

2) Optimize protocol:

Protocol optimization is needed in the following areas:

 Adjust garments’ return packaging to minimize shipping impacts on  pad integrity..

 Evaluate lower cost, lower weight variability board materials.

o Foam boards:  non-wettable with no significant x-ray interference.

 Potentially utilize “Box Study” protocol to minimize difficult handling for

elderly users, especially if the current protocol is skewing recruit.

(Used product is placed loosely into a box lined with a plastic bag that

is closed via “twist-tie” or other easy mechanism, and then box is

closed and sealed.)

 Optimize and standardize (STM/TTM) the x-ray settings and

procedure to ensure good image quality.

 For garment products, it is important to include measurement of the

rise and the absorbent placement in addition to the dry weight.

Garment rise and distance from front product edge to absorbent front  edge are critical – if off-specification, they will result in WP errors

unless accounted for in the analysis.

3) Compare to and update bench and/or mannequin test protocols:

Use the wetting pattern data from these studies to compare to wetting

patterns generated from bench and mannequin tests, and adjust bench

and/or mannequin test protocols as needed to reflect the user data.  (This  research was started but is on hold at this time awaiting resource

availability.)

4) Further investigate in-use insult points, flow rates, distribution:

Further research is planned to study insult points, flow rates, and fluid

distribution in use studies using fluid distribution sensors.   Historical

research assumed the insult points were at the front of the wetting pattern, which is hypothesized to be too far forward.

5) Understand overnight usage (and other special use conditions):

Targeted studies are needed to better understand overnight use situations, such as differences due to body positions.

6) Investigate synergies with HUTs:

Explore the option to use a subset of HUT products to measure Wetting  Patterns, eliminating the need to place separate studies.
