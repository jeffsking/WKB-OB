ABSTRACT

In August, 2014, Proctor and Gamble introduced pads, liners and garments in the  incontinence category, with an aggressive claim that Always Discreet Pads were  drier than Poise pads** (** based on average US consumer usage). Dryness is a  highly important benefit in the incontinence category, one that Poise needs to

own as the leader in Light Bladder Leakage (LBL).

POISE developers have conducted extensive consumer research in the design of  the product, to meet the needs of women for all frequency and severity levels. In  managing their LBL, women place a very high importance on being dry.  This

need is not met when a product feels wet. POISE products are designed to stay  dry throughout the normal use period, at a loading level that is experienced by

most women.

To assess the level of product dryness, POISE researchers use a measure of

flowback – the amount of liquid that escapes the pad under normal pressure.

POISE used this same bench test to substantiate a claim in May of 2014 of being  3X drier than the leading regular period liners and pads.

In March 2015, POISE introduced a new line of thinner pads – POISE Thin

Shape.  These were targeted at the same user group as Always Discreet pads.  Rather than challenge the Always Discreet dryness claim, the team initiated work  to develop a claim for POISE Thin Shape. Although testing demonstrated that

Poise was up to 50X drier than Always Discreet, the business chose the claim,

“POISE Thin Shape Pads stay 10X drier than Always Discreet” (vs. regular

length pads).

This TL outlines the work completed to substantiate the 10 x claim and explains  the consumer relevancy of each of the elements in the bench test used for that

substantiation.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This document is a summary of the work completed to substantiate a

dryness claim for Poise® Thin Shape pads vs. Always® Discreet pads. It  also summarizes the method used for substantiation and its parameters.

In August, 2014, Proctor and Gamble introduced pads, liners and

garments in the incontinence category. Within a few months, P&G made  an aggressive claim that Always Discreet Pads were drier than Poise

pads** (** based on average US consumer usage). The Poise team had

data to refute the claim, but decided to make a dryness claim of their own  vs. Always Discreet, rather than challenge the P&G claim.  Ultimately,

Poise used the following claim, “POISE Thin Shape Pads stay 10X drier

than Always Discreet” (vs. regular length pads), and P&G did not

challenge.

During 2015, the K-C legal team and the P&G legal team had several

discussions regarding the test methodology and parameters used in each  company’s “dryness” test.  The methods have many of the same

parameters, but the companies did not agree completely on some of the

elements.

The K-C test method used to substantiate the dryness claim is referred to  as the Bladder Box method for intake and flowback (draft STM-00497).  It is  the flowback measurement that is used for substantiating dryness.  Note: the  claim is targeted toward product dryness, not skin dryness, and also not how  dry the user feels.  The test method has many parameters, and each

parameter is based on consumer relevant data.  This document will explain  the origin of each parameter and direct the reader to other sources of

information.

INTRODUCTION

In August, 2014, Proctor and Gamble introduced pads, liners and

garments in the incontinence category, with an aggressive claim that

Always Discreet Pads were drier than Poise pads** (** based on average  US consumer usage). Dryness is a highly important benefit in the

incontinence category, one that Poise needs to own as the leader in Light  Bladder Leakage (LBL).

POISE developers have conducted extensive consumer research in the

severity levels. In managing their LBL, women place a very high

importance on being dry.  This need is not met when a product feels wet. POISE product are designed to stay dry throughout the normal use period, at a loading level that is experienced by most women.

After much information exchange and discussion about test methods and  parameters among the legal representatives of both companies, P&G did  not challenge the 10X claim.

The K-C test method used to substantiate the dryness claim is referred to  as the Bladder Box method for intake and flowback.  It is the flowback

measurement that is used for substantiating dryness. In general, the

procedure includes loading a pad with multiple insults (insult amounts based  on consumer use information) using urine-like fluid while pressure is being  applied (to simulate wear conditions). DISCUSSION

This section contains data for each parameter of the test method.

Product procurement

To conduct substantiation testing on a representative sample of products, similar to what would be available to consumers, products from both

brands being evaluated were purchased via an external vendor during a  similar timeframe (April-May 2015) across four different geographical

regions in the United States (Northeast, Southeast, Southwest, Midwest).  This is consistent with ASTM E1958 Standard Guide for Sensory Claim

Substantiation (there is not a guideline available for bench test supported  claims).

Sample Size

Previous testing of these product codes indicated that n = 5 products per  code would provide sufficient power for detecting differences expected if  they exist at a 95% confidence level.  A sample size of n = 20

products/code was chosen for this testing to allow representation from

multiple production lots and a sample size in line with historical amounts  used with NAD on claims supported by bench testing.

The 20 total specimens for claim substantiation bench testing from each  code listed below were pulled from one manufacturing lot in each

geographic region (n=5 per manufacturing lot/region for a total n=20).

 Currently available in-market Poise® Thin-Shape pads:

o Moderate Regular Length pad

o Maximum Regular Length pad

o Ultimate Regular Length pad

 Corresponding current in-market Always Discreet pads:

o Moderate Regular Length pad

o Maximum Regular Length pad

o Ultimate Regular Length pad

Note: after this study was completed, a recommendation was made to

increase future claim testing sample size to 30, to align with other AFC

brands and to strengthen KC’s position for any potential challenges.

Sample randomization

Prior to testing, all samples were randomized based on a randomization  plan from the statistician. Code testing order was randomized in blocks

within absorbency level to ensure testing across time with all codes, again  consistent with recommendations in ASTM E1958 to address potential

testing order bias.  Five products/code were selected from each

manufacturing lot source, and at least one product/lot was tested for each  of the two pad locations evaluated. Testing was randomized across two

trained testers. Specimens were tested 2 at a time, always paired with the  same brand/absorbency level product.

Test Method (draft STM-00497)

To substantiate the aforementioned dryness claim for POISE Thin-Shape  pads over Always Discreet pads, a bench test measuring flowback that is  representative of consumer use was performed using calibrated

equipment.

Flowback is a measure of how many grams of fluid return to the surface of  a pad, which has been loaded with a consumer relevant amount of fluid,

when that pad is placed under a consumer relevant pressure.

Specific steps in the flowback bench test can be found in the Appendix. In  general, the procedure includes loading a pad with multiple insults (insult  amounts based on consumer use information) using urine-like fluid while  pressure is being applied (to simulate wear conditions). Once the final

loading has entered the pad, flowback is measured. The flowback is

determined by collecting and weighing the grams of fluid that return to the  surface of the pad when a pressure is applied to the pad, simulating in-use  experience. Having low flowback is the ability to retain fluid under pressure  in the absorbent core away from the surface of the pad.

Liquid used for insults

The fluid used in bench testing is a 0.9% saline solution that is heated to  body temperature (37º C). This solution provides a stable and suitable fluid  to simulate urine as it leaves the body for absorbent bench testing.

Fluid is insulted at either a ‘middle-front’ or ‘middle-back’ location during

the bench test. One half of the samples are tested at the ‘middle-front’

location while the other half are tested at ‘middle-back’ location. The

analysis is done combining results from the two locations to support the

advertising claim, as well as at each individual location to understand any

differences.

The two locations are determined based on the absorbent pad length of

the product being tested. The locations are measured based on a set ratio

of the overall pad length, with the middle-front location being 5/12 x pad

length from the front end of the absorbent and the middle-back location

being 7/12 x pad length from the front end of the absorbent (or 5/12 from

the back end of the absorbent). Both insult points are centered on the CD width of the absorbent pad. See the image below.

These locations are consistent with general female anatomy of the urethra being skewed forward when measuring from the pelvic bone to the coccyx bone area (Fem Care Anthropometric Measurement Study, AF32161-00, June 2008). The use of middle front and middle back locations account for consumer front-to-back flipping of the pad, which reverses the orientation. In addition, both locations are within wetting stain patterns.

NOTE: Prior bladder box bench testing had included four different insult positions. See Figure 1 below depicting these locations. The four different locations (Front, Middle Front, Middle Back, and Back) account for the variability in pad placement by consumers, including front-to-back flipping and forward or backward skew. These test locations were also consistent with internal data on the overall size of the wetting pattern in worn pads. However, some Always Discreet products failed the testing in the front and back locations, so only 2 locations were used for this claim, in order to

have equal test comparisons.

# Insult amount and number of insults

The total volume of fluid loaded into a pad during the flowback test was determined based on analysis of weighed post-use pads from Urine Loss Spectrum study (IT-34734). In this study, women were given their normal products to use and then returned them post-use. The used products were weighed to create data on product loading distributions by product form. The 75th percentile total pad loading was used from the ULS study to define the bench test total loading value for each product form. The 75th percentile total pad loading was chosen over a mean load to ensure delivery of dryness on both light and heavy leakage days for consumers. Those total loading amounts are as follows:

· Microliner – 3mL

- Liner – 6mL (NOTE: Some historical testing on liners was done at 9mL total loading)

- Ultrathin - 12mL

· Moderate – 30 mL

· Maximum - 78mL

$\blacksquare$ Ultimate - 114mL total loading

The total loading amount in the flowback test is delivered via three equal insults. Delivering the loading in three insults is consistent with consumer feedback on the number of insults experienced when wearing pads for incontinence (Study A-36049, Study A-35609), and provides for a bench test that is not a stress test in terms of pad capacity.

Detailed explanation of loading amount in Appendix B: Pad Dryness Ad Claim Substantiation – Consumer Relevant Fluid Amounts to Assess Flow Back Performance

Product is placed inside a Bladder Box is during the insult portion of the bench test. This box is a flat platform with a top opening lid (see image below). The product is attached to the underside of lid. An inflatable bladder is located inside box which applies a specified pressure (psi), 0.25psi in this method, via compressed air when the product is being insulted. . Ports are located in the top opening lid of the apparatus to allow the pad to be insulted with fluid. The equipment is calibrated quarterly

Applying pressure when a pad is loaded helps mimic the in-use wear environment, which is important as functionality of an absorbent can be impacted by compression (i.e., void area available and material performance under load).

# Flow rate of liquid

A programmable fluid pump is used to insult the products during the bench test. The pump is capable of delivering batch volumes between 15 and 200.

mLs at delivery rates between 4 and 20 mLs/sec. (Example: ISMATEC

NCP-Z model C.P. 78004-02 with a Micropump pump head from ColePalmer L21833). Prior to using the pump to conduct bench testing the

tester verifies it is dispensing the correct amount of fluid.

For heavier absorbency pads - moderate, maximum, and ultimate pads – the loading fluid is delivered at 8 mL/sec to represent general consumer

use. Urine flow rates for incontinent, as well as continent, women vary

widely and are dependent on a number of factors, in particular volume.

References to peak and average flow rates in literature (See Appendix C) for normal women range generally range from 12 mL/sec to 20 mL/sec.

However, flow rates described among those having urinary dysfunction are  typically lower in the literature, supporting the 8mL/sec use in bench

testing.  Decreased flow rates are also experienced as people age.

Consumers using moderate through ultimate pad absorbency levels (as

tested in this claim) can experience stress and/or urge incontinence.

For the very light absorbency products – microliners, liners and ultrathin

pads – the loading fluid is delivered at 1 mL/sec to represent general

consumer use. Urine flow among consumers using these products is

typically more of a dribble leak, so flow rate is slower than the heavier

absorbency pads. At this low a flow rate, the programmable pump is not

used as the rate and fluid insult amounts are below the pump’s

capabilities. A syringe loaded with the appropriate fluid is used and

dispensing timing is consistent with the desired 1 mL/sec flow rate.

Wait time between insults

While the average wear time for pads is 8 hours, and consumer use tests  suggest total fluid loading occurs over more than one insult/episode for a  good portion of products, it is possible to simulate pad conditions without

conducting an 8-hour long bench test that would have multiple hours

between insults.

Waiting a period of 15-minutes after an insult will provide enough time for  typical absorbent cores manufactured today to distribute the fluid and

come to equilibrium. In other words, after 15-minutes, the absorbent core  has done its work, and waiting any longer in a bench-top test is not

needed.

Therefore, after each of the first and second fluid insults complete in the

bladder box bench, there is a 15 minute wait time. This results in a test that  can be completed in about 40 minutes, a short enough time to allow for

evaluating multiple specimen for a higher level of confidence in any

differences that may be seen.

Wait time after 3rd insult / before flowback measurement

As the bladder box bench test is meant to provide information on how wet  the surface of the pad is in use, the wait time after the 3rd and final insult is  not the 15-minutes wait described earlier. In fact, the wait time after the 3rd  insult was designed to be minimal while still allowing for a safe and

consistent method.

This shortened wait time is designed to be representative of an in-use

situation, where a consumer would not wait 15-minutes (or longer, as the  15-minutes has been noted to adequately represent an absorbent that has  come to equilibrium) before assessing whether the pad was wet. A

consumer would make that assessment more quickly, and likely typically

right experiencing unintentional urine loss. In fact, during the

aforementioned Urine Loss Spectrum consumer use test, about one-third  of pads were changed because the user noted it felt wet. Changing due to  a wet feeling product was the second most selected reason, with changing  due to convenience (e.g., in the bathroom, going to bed, usual time) being  the top selection.

During the bladder box bench test, the wait time after the 3rd insult

completed was approximately 2 minutes prior to the flowback

measurement starting. More recently, the wait time has been specified to  be a timed 2-minute wait.

Medium used to measure flowback

A material is needed to collect the fluid that flows back out of the product  during the flow back portion of the bench testing. It’s important the medium  used meets the following criteria to be an appropriate choice:

1. repeatable  and reproducible results

2. sufficient capacity to collect the range of expected flowback

3. hold fluid until it can be measured

Investigations have been done in BCC using filter paper, blotter paper, and  collagen paper, with final recommendation to use blotter paper due to it

being readily available and easily handled, resulting in more consistent

bench testing.

Included in the Appendix C is a Point of View on the use of blotter paper

vs. collagen paper as the flow back medium authored by Test

Development expert, Sam Henry, supporting a preference for blotter paper  over collagen in the bladder box bench testing method based on the test’s  purpose.

For the bladder box test, two pieces of blotter paper measuring 3.5” x 12” are stacked on top of each other and then placed on the body side surface  of the pad to collect the flowback fluid. The blotter paper was chosen as it  provides a medium that is able to collect and hold any fluid that returns to  the surface of the pad, and is consistent, easily handled, and readily

available, minimizing variability.

Type and amount of pressure during measurement of flowback

Based on research, the pressures experienced on an absorbent pad

during wear are more closely simulated with a point pressure versus

uniform pressure applied across the surface of the product. As such, a

point pressure of 0.7 psi is applied at the insult location during the bladder  box bench measurement of flow back. The 0.7psi is achieved using a 1”

diameter weight, a size that is consistent with the general width of contact  with the pad in use. This 1” diameter weight is placed on top of the blotter  paper at the location of the insult point.

Amount of time to measure flowback

The blotter paper and 1” diameter weight are left on top of the pad for a

total of two minutes in the bladder box bench test. The two minutes

provides adequate time for the blotter paper to collect the flow back fluid in  a consistent and repeatable manner. After the two minutes, the amount of  flowback is calculated by determining the increase in weight of the blotter  paper.

# 3.2 Insulting Product

3.2.1 Ensure the pump is calibrated to the appropriate flow rate and dispense time. (See Tables 2-6 in Appendix 12.1.). If testing microliners, liners, or ultrathin pads the pump is not needed, and a refillable syringe should be used to better approximate the slow flow rate (1 mL/sec).

3.2.2 Place stop watch (cleared to zero [0]) in one hand.

3.2.3 Place nozzle in other hand. Aim the nozzle to hit the side of the port, and not directly onto the specimen. If testing microliners, liners, or ultrathin pads, place the syringe in the other hand and aim at the side of the port.

3.2.4 Make sure to press the "run" button on the pump and the "Start" button on the stop watch simultaneously. Observe the fluid going into the product. For microliners, liners, and ultrathin pads, start dispensing fluid from the syringe at the same time as starting the stop watch. To get the desired flow rate of 1 mL/sec, the syringe should be emtied in about the following amount of time: microliners, 1 second; liners, 2 seconds; ultrathin pads, 4 seconds. It is recommended the tester count in his/her head to approximate the time to empty the syringe (e.g., 1 one-thousand, 2 one-thousand, etc).

3.2.5 Press "stop" on the stop watch as soon as fluid is absorbed and no longer visible above the body side surface of the product (this is the 'Intake Time'.)

3.2.6 Log the Intake Time. If requester has approved testing more than one specimen at a time, move on to the next product in the bladder box to be insulted.

3.2.7 After all products have been insulted and all times logged, set a timer for 15 minutes.

3.2.8 Repeat steps 5.2.1-5.2.7 to insult the product a second time; starting with pump calibration and ending with the setting the timer for 15 minutes.

3.2.9 Repeat steps 5.2.1-5.2.6 to insult the product a third time. After the third insult on the last product is logged, set a timer for 2 minutes and then press the "abort" button on the bladder box to release the pressure in the box.

3.2.10 Before the 2 minute timer completes, the SV readout on the bladder box should read zero (0). Uunclip and open the bladder box. Carefully remove the insulted product(s) from the box and place insulted side up on a flat, solid surface.

3.2.11 Once the 2 minute timer completes, start the flow back measure

# 3.3 Flow back

3.3.1 Place two (2) pieces of pre-weighed, pre-cut, blotter paper atop each insulted product.

3.3.2 Place 25 mm (1 inch diameter), 0.7 psi weight on blotter paper at insult target location.

3.3.3 Set timer for two (2) minutes.

3.3.4 When time is up, remove weight from blotter paper (wiping weight with dry paper towel) and carefully move the 2 pieces of blotter paper to a balance to weigh them; ensure that the balance is zeroed (0) prior to weighing the blotting paper.

3.3.5 Record weight of the blotter paper.

## 3.4 Wet Weight and Wet Bulk

3.4.1 After flow back is complete, weigh the product to the nearest 0.01 gram.

3.4.2 After weighing, measure the wet bulk at the insult point to the nearest 0.1 millimeter.

## 3.5 Run-Off Failure

3.5.1 After conducting the entire procedure, make a note of any fluid run-off that occurred during the insult portion of the test in the comments section of the data collection spreadsheet. If testing more than one product at a time, include a comment for each product in the bladder box at the time run-off occurred and note whether that particular product was the one that failed. If unable to decipher which product failed, note that in the spreadsheet.

4.1 Record the Dry and Wet weights of the product to the nearest 0.01 gram.

4.2 Record the Dry and Wet Bulk of the product to the nearest 0.1 mm.

4.3 Record all 3 intake times to the nearest 0.01 second.

4.4 Record the pre-weight and final weight of the blotter paper to the nearest 0.001 gram.

APPENDIX C: Technical point of view for the use of blotter paper vs. collagen paper in a surface moisture quantification test

Sam Henry

12/9/15

This document is a technical point of view analyzing the use of blotter

paper versus collagen paper (absorptive material) in methods to quantify

the amount of fluid that is not contained in the core and thus away from the  topsheet and wearer's skin of an absorbent article after a given insult

procedure has been performed and pressure is applied on said article.

Explicitly, the use of absorptive material in a method of this type is

performed by placing it on top of the absorbent article after the insult

procedure with a preset set of conditions, e.g., pressure, area, shape,

duration, etc., after which the absorptive paper is weighed. Any weight

added beyond its dry weight is assumed to be insult fluid that was not

absorbed entirely by the absorbent article, either due to remaining at the

surface or returning to the surface when pressure is applied.

The ideal material for this purpose would be able, under test conditions, to  absorb only the entirety of the moisture at the surface of the article. This

would require an ability to absorb fluid, intake fluid at a given rate, and

store fluid within the absorptive material appropriate to meet the success

criteria. If the ability to absorb fluid is too great, it may draw fluid out of the  absorbent article, biasing surface moisture readings high, and vice versa. If  fluid intake rate is small such that the surface moisture cannot be absorbed  in the duration of application of absorptive material to the insulted

absorbent article, the surface moisture readings would be biased low.

Lastly, if there is little volume within the absorptive material, there will be

little insult fluid retained within the absorptive media, biasing surface

moisture readings low.

Blotter paper and collagen paper have different physical and chemical

properties that affect their interactions with fluid as outlined above. Table 1  depicts the difference in internal volume between the two absorptive

materials.

# Table 1: Summary of select macroscopic physical properties of the two materials of interest pertaining to internal volume.

|Material|basis weight ($g_{in.}$)$_2$)|specific saturation* capacity ($g_{fluid}$ $g_{sample-1}$)|basis saturation capacity ($g_{fluid}$ $in.^{-2}$)|
|---|---|---|---|
|Blotter paper|0.199|2.26|0.450|
|Collagen paper|0.019|2.22|0.042|

After 20 minutes of soaking in distilled water

The basis saturation capacity describes the availability of volume within the material per square inch. Assuming that the ability and rate of absorption is ideal for a given application as defined above, the collagen paper would only be able to absorb 0.42 g in-2, roughly 1 drop over 1.2 in2 assuming a representative 20 drops per gram. This is a tenth of the capacity of blotter paper. It is not unreasonable to assume that there may be one or more drops (more than 0.05 g) over 1.2 in2, therefore the collagen paper in this context would have a much greater tendency to bias any surface moisture measurements low.

A vertical wicking test was used to gauge the ability to draw fluid into itself for blotter paper and collagen paper. The test showed that the blotter paper was able to draw 0.85 g (17 drops) in 5 minutes, collagen paper drawing less than 0.05 g (1 drop). Over the course of this 5 minutes, the blotter paper was consistently absorbing more than the collagen paper at any given time. This upper limit for collagen paper is estimated from the variability in the results. This observations would indicate that the use of blotter paper may bias a surface moisture quantification high relative to the collagen paper by more readily absorbing fluid.

There are several experimental considerations as well. The relative scaling of product performance when using blotter vs collagen paper is generally similar, but the relative differences between the samples can be different. The preparation procedures for collagen are more restrictive than blotter paper. If the internal volume of the absorptive material is inadequate for the amount of rewet, additional layers may be added with the understanding that any volume that exists between the different layers may affect its performance and may not be strictly controlled.

To fully understand the effects of using blotter and collagen paper in this and other contexts, one would have to characterize the fundamental

properties of these two materials and observe their performance in

controlled experiments.

Based on this information, I would recommend the use of blotter paper for  this application because due to 1) it having a more appropriate internal

volume per area and 2) its ability to absorb fluid as shown by the vertical  wicking test. To clarify the second point, on a scale of 5 minutes, the

collagen paper was able to absorb < 1 drop (0.05 g) of fluid. In the context  of quantifying surface moisture, some absorption is necessary that

appears to be lacking in collagen paper on timescales reasonable for

rewet.

The intent of this analysis is not to comment on the use of blotter paper

and collagen paper in any context beyond what is explicitly mentioned

here, any conclusions drawn from this analysis may or may not be

endorsed by the original author of this document.

The maximum and average urine flow rates of the first voids in both sexes were compared with the respective voided volumes. Nomogram charts, in centile form, for both the maximum and average urine flow rates were constructed using statistical transformations of the data.|
