## ABSTRACT

Efficient water-absorbing and water-holding materials have shown notable promise in various applications, including hygiene products, agriculture, and drug delivery systems. Opposed to traditional absorbents prepared using synthetic polymers, bio-based, environmentally friendly efficient absorbents have attracted more attention from both academia and the industry. Herein, the aerogel absorbents from functional sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC—Na), citric acid (CA) crosslinker, and cellulose nanofibers (CNF) have been developed via freeze-dyring and cross-linking process. The effects of aerogel microstructure, doses of CMC-Na and CNF, thermal cross-linking time, salts present in solutions, and solution pH on water storage by aerogels are examined in detail. The water absorption and water retention capacities of aerogels are observed to be 153 ± 3 and 123 ± 4 g g⁻$^{1}$, respectively, showing satisfactory performance. The aerogels possess environmentally friendly raw materials, simple preparation method, remarkable water absorption and retention performance, demonstrating the potential to replace petroleum-based absorbents.

### 1. Introduction

Currently, the growing emphasis on personal care products has led to rising demand for baby diapers, female hygiene items, and adult incontinence products, driving a notable growth in the superabsorbent material market [1–3]. Superabsorbent materials (SAMs) are capable of absorbing and retaining large amounts of water [4,5]. The remarkable fluid uptake capacities of SAMs have also gained considerable research interest because of their notable promise in serving various industries, including those related to hygiene products [6,7], agriculture [8,9], the food industry [10,11], civil engineering [12,13], and water treatment [14,15]. Nowadays, most SAMs are produced based on petroleum-based polymers such as acrylate, acrylamide, and acrylic acid [13,16]. These synthetic polymers usually have highly hydrophilic groups attached to a gel network, helping them absorb and retain water or aqueous solutions. These superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) are popular for their significant swelling capacity and high absorption capability [17,18]. However, these SAPs usually suffer poor sustainability and the risk of microplasics, causing safety concerns and environmental burdens [19]. Thus, bio-based SAMs have drawn people's attention, particularly for personal hygiene products [20].

Bio-based SAMs are generally derived from cellulose, chitosan, polyglutamic acid, plant protein, and starch [21–25]. Cellulose, the most abundant bio-based polymer, attracts attention as an eco-friendly product because of its biocompatibility, biodegradability, sustainability, and cost effectiveness [26]. Cellulose comprises hundreds to thousands of glucose units ($C_6H_{10}O_5)_n$ and abundant hydroxyl groups, which make it available for surface chemical modifications to introduce new functional groups [27]. In recent years, various cellulose-based SAMs prepared through functionalization, chemical grafting, and cross-linking have been extensively reported [28]. For instance, I. Nurulla et al. [29] fabricated a poly(acrylic acid)-grafted maleylated cellulose superabsorbent via photopolymerization with maleic anhydride-modified cotton stalk cellulose and acrylic acid. Dai et al. [30] synthesized superabsorbent hydrogels by grafting acrylic acid and acrylamide onto carboxymethyl cellulose. However, despite significant advancements in eco-friendly SAMs by integrating natural polymers with synthetic polymers, achieving bio-based SAMs with high performance remains the ultimate goal.

Aerogels with favorable attributes, such as high porosity, highly interconnected pore structure, and adjustable pore morphology, have attracted extensive attention in absorption, separation, environmental

protection, and energy-storage research [31,32]. Notably, cellulose aerogels integrating these advantages of cellulose and aerogels are an ideal alternative for the development of high-efficiency water-absorbing SAMs. A few researchers attempted to design highly absorbent cellulose aerogels by using different precursor media and drying processes [33,34], while the water-retaining capacities of these cellulose aerogels remains unexplored. The high water absorption capacity enables efficient liquid storage, while the remarkable water retention property significantly reduces reverse osmosis and overflow risks [35]. Thus, cellulose aerogels should exhibit not only high water absorption but also a remarkable water retention capability, both of which are expected to make them highly suitable for applications requiring sustained water management, such as hygiene products. Therefore, it is highly desired to create a cellulose-based aerogel with favorable water-absorbing capacity, high water-retaining performance, and remarkable structural stability.

To design a cellulose-based aerogel with remarkable water absorbency and water retention capacities, we fabricated cellulose-based aerogels based on the following four requirements: (1) Aerogel should possess good hydrophilicity and well-interlinked pore structure to ensure repaid water diffusion and adequate hygroscopic swelling behavior. (2) The pore structures of aerogels should not collapse during the long-term water absorption process. (3) Aerogel should be functionalized with readily accessible absorption ligands to effectively capture and hold the absorbed water. In light of these requirements, we chose two cellulose-based polymers with good flexibility and remarkable hydrophilicity as building blocks to fabricate aerogels via a simple freeze-drying and a subsequent thermal cross-linking process. Specifically, sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na), because of its carboxylic group binding sites, served as the main body for improving the water absorbency and water retention capacities of aerogels, and cellulose nanofibers (CNF) was selected to form scaffolds of aerogels to enhance underwater structural stability. In addition, citric acid (CA) was introduced to facilitate cross-linking between CMC-Na and CNF to form a robust network structure, as well as for endowing the aerogels with additional carboxylate groups. Because of the unique porous architecture and abundant carboxylated active sites, CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels exhibited high water absorption and retention capabilities. Moreover, CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels exhibited remarkable water-holding capacities both under the influence of centrifugal forces and elevated temperatures, highlighting their excellent potential for use in practical applications. This study presents the development of high-performance absorbents derived from abundant biomass resources, providing a sustainable and economical pathway for water management in diverse domains (personal care, agriculture, industry, etc.).

# 2. Experimental section

# 2.1. Materials

CNF suspension (8 wt%) was purchased from SAPPI MAAS-TRICHT BV (Netherlands). CMC-Na, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium chloride (NaCl), calcium chloride (CaCl₂), potassium chloride (KCl), magnesium chloride (MgCl₂), ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl₃·6H₂O), aluminum chloride hexahydrate (AlCl₃·6H₂O), and hydrochloric acid (36 %–38 %) were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. CA was supplied by Shanghai Titan Scientific Co. Ltd. All chemical reagents were of analytical grade and used as received.

# 2.2. Fabrication of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogel

The CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogel was fabricated using a freeze-drying approach. First, the CNF suspension was diluted to various concentrations (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4 wt%) by adding various amounts of deionized water followed by stirring for 30 min. Certain amounts of CA were then added to diluted CNF suspensions under stirring to afford a uniform

CNF/CA suspension. Designed amounts of CMC-Na were further separately added to the resultant CNF/CA suspensions, after which all CMC-Na/CA/CNF suspensions were physically mixed for 6 h at 25 °C to achieve homogeneous CMC-Na/CA/CNF suspensions. Subsequently, CMC-Na/CA/CNF suspensions obtained were centrifuged to remove air bubbles and then frozen into an ice gel at different temperatures (–18, –80, and –196 °C). Frozen samples were lyophilized in a freeze-dryer (Labconco FD5–3, USA) for 48 h to obtain aerogel skeletons. Finally, the aerogel skeletons were transferred into an oven and heated at 160 °C to achieve chemical cross-linking between CMC–Na, CA, and CNF, hence further enhancing the structural stability of the network skeleton in CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels (Fig. 1a-b). Notably, we systematically investigated the effects of the following two factors on the water absorption and retention capacities of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels: (1) various contents of CMC–Na, CA, and CNF and (2) different parameters of the fabrication method (e.g., freezing temperature, thermal cross-linking duration). The formulations of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels prepared at different polymer dosages, freezing temperatures, and thermal cross-linking durations are presented in Tables 1–5.

## 2.3. Water absorption capacity measurement

The water absorption capacities of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels were measured using deionized water at different pH values (1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, and 13) and in various types of ionic solutions (NaCl, KCl, CaCl₂, MgCl₂, AlCl₃ and FeCl₃). Herein, the CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogel with 33 mm diameter and 2-3 mm thickness was immersed in deionized water (500 mL) or a salt solution (500 mL) for 24 h at 25 °C to attain swelling equilibrium. Subsequently, the liquid-absorbed aerogel was carefully lifted from the liquid, and then 1 g of filter paper was placed on its surface and kept for 2 min to remove excess liquid absorbed on its surface. The water absorption capacity (Qeq) of the CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogel was calculated as follows:

$$Q_{eq}(g/g) = \frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1} \qquad (1)$$

where $m_1$ denotes the initial weight of the aerogel and $m_2$ denotes the weight of the swollen sample after reaching equilibrium.

To test the water absorption speed (WAS) of the CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogel, it was immersed in deionized water (500 mL) for 1 min at 25 °C. After the desired time, the sample with water absorbed was taken, and then 1 g of filter paper was placed on its surface and kept for 2 min to remove excess water. The WAS was then calculated as follows:

$$ \text{WAS}(g/g/\text{min}) = \frac{m_4 - m_3}{t \times m_3} \quad (2) $$

where $t$ denotes an absorption time of 1 min, $m_3$ denotes the initial weight of the aerogel, and $m_4$ denotes the weight of the swollen sample.

# 2.5. Density measurement and porosity calculation

The apparent densities ($\rho_a$) of the CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels were calculated as follows:

$$ \rho_a (\text{kg/m}^3) = \frac{m_8}{V} \qquad (5) $$

where $m_8$ and $V$ denote the weight and volume of aerogels, respectively.

The porosities ($P_a$) of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogel were determined as follows:

$$ P_a(\%) = \left(1 - \frac{\rho_a}{\rho_s}\right) \times 100\% \quad (6) $$

where $\rho_a$ denotes the apparent density of the aerogel, provided by Eq. (5) and $\rho_s$ denotes the skeleton density of the aerogel. $\rho_s$ was estimated using aerogel composition as follows:

$$ \rho_s = \frac{1}{(W_{CMC-Na}/\rho_{CMC-Na}) + (W_{CA}/\rho_{CA}) + (W_{CNF}/\rho_{CNF})} \quad (7) $$

where $W_{\text{CMC-Na}}$ denotes the dry weight fraction of CMC-Na in the aerogel, $W_{\text{CA}}$ that of CA in the aerogel, $W_{\text{CNF}}$ that of CNF in the aerogel. $\rho_{\text{CMC-Na}}$ was fixed at 1590 kg/m $^{3}$, $\rho_{\text{CA}}$ at 1670 kg/m $^{3}$, and $\rho_{\text{CNF}}$ at 1500

kg/m $^{3}$.

# 2.6. Statistical analysis

The samples were tested and the results were recorded as Mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using the IBM software (SPSS 22, USA). The statistical differences were analyzed by the one-way ANOVA, and the level of significance was set at p<0.05.

# 2.7. Characterization

The morphologies of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels were observed via scanning electron microscopy (Hitachi TM-3030, Japan). The chemical structures of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels were studied by Fourier transform infrared spectrum in the 500–4000 cm $^{-1}$ (PerkinElmer Spectrum Two, America). The compressive mechanical property of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogel was measured on a universal testing machine (UH6502, China). The porous structure of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogel was determined through Brunauer-Emmett-Teller nitrogen adsorption/desorption measurement (V-Sorb 2800P, China).

# 3.2. Effect of CMC-Na and CNF on water absorbency and water retention capacities

In the CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels, CMC-Na and CNF were the functional components and were of considerable importance to the water absorption and retention performance of aerogels. The absorption process of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels is related to the following three primary factors: 1) capillary forces generated by the porous network structure, facilitating the penetration of water into aerogels; 2) interactions between hydrophilic functional groups (-COO– and –OH) and water molecules, enhancing the affinity of aerogel for water; and 3) ion concentration gradients between the inside and outside of the aerogel network, driving the osmotic influx of water molecules. Simultaneously, the electrostatic repulsion between adjacent carboxylate groups facilitates the expansion of the porous network, thereby creating more space to accommodate water molecules (Fig. 3a-b) [40–42].

The effect of CMC-Na content (5, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10 wt%) on the water absorption capacities of aerogels were intensively investigated, as shown in Fig. 3c. The water absorption capacities increased from $99 \pm 6$ to $152 \pm 4$ g $g^{-1}$ with increasing CMC-Na content within the range of 5-7 wt%, which may be attributed to the swelling space increase due to the increased electrostatic repulsion between carboxylate groups and the elevated driving force due to increased $Na^+$ osmotic pressure. The water absorption capacities of aerogels decreased from $152 \pm 4$ to $114 \pm 4$ g $g^{-1}$ with increasing CMC-Na content in the range between 7 and 10 wt%. This phenomenon could be attributed to the structural change induced by excessive incorporation of CMC-Na. On the one hand, the increased solid content resulted in reduced porosity (Fig. S2), thereby limiting the void space available for water storage. On the other hand, the large number of carboxyl and hydroxyl groups caused rapid swelling of pore walls and blocking of pore channels, thus reducing the water

absorption rate and hindering the absorption of water molecules (Fig. S2). The water retention capacity of the CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogel was observed to be closely related to the amount of hydrophilic functional groups. As shown in Fig. 3d, the water retention ratio significantly increased from $67 \pm 0.6\%$ to $85 \pm 0.5\%$ when the CMC-Na content was increased from 5 to 10 wt%. This was because the increased CMC-Na content could provide more interactions with water molecules and facilitate the formation of a more extensive porous gel network structure, thereby entrapping more water molecules within pores. However, the maximum water retention capacity (~126 g $g^{-1}$) was achieved at a CMC-Na concentration of 7 wt%, which may be attributed to the comprehensive effects of the high water absorption capacity and remarkable water retention ratio. Considering the importance of retaining more amount of absorbed water within the aerogel, the optimal concentration of CMC-Na to be incorporated in the aerogel was determined to be 7 wt%.

In addition, the content of CNF in CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels also significantly affected their water absorption capacity and retention capability. In the absence of CNFs, the efficiency of cross-linking between CA and CMC-Na significantly compromised because of the high substitution degree of carboxyl groups at C6 hydroxyl groups and the electrostatic repulsion between the polyelectrolyte chains of CMC-Na. This low cross-linking efficiency resulted in a loosely structured network, leading to the poor structural stability of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels in their swollen state. As shown in Fig. 3e and Fig. S3, the water absorption capacity, porosity and water absorption rate of aerogels decreased from $158 \pm 6$ g $g^{-1}$, $95.8 \pm 0.1$%, and $25 \pm 0.4$ g $g^{-1}/min$ to $84 \pm 8$ g $g^{-1}$, $94.2 \pm 0.2\%$, and $20 \pm 0.4$ g $g^{-1}/min$ with increasing CNF concentration (0.0-0.4 wt%); this was because the addition of CNFs facilitated the formation of a rigid and dense network structure and reduced the water permeability during the swelling process. However, the water retention ratio increased from $73 \pm 3$ % to $89 \pm 1$ % with increasing CNF concentration (0.0-0.4 wt%) (Fig. 3f), which could be ascribed to the reinforced network structure at high CNF concentration, resisting structural collapse during dehydration and maintaining capillary-driven water retention. In addition, the water retention capability could be facilely regulated by optimizing the CNF concentration (Fig. 3f), and the water retention capability was observed to be maximum at a CNF concentration of 0.1 wt%. Upon comprehensively considering the water absorption performance, water retention capacity, and structural stability, CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels with a CNF concentration of 0.1 wt% were chosen as the representative sample.

The CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels were cross-linked via thermal treatment, and the influence of thermal cross-linking time on the water absorption and retention performance of aerogels was studied. As shown in Fig. 5a, the water absorption capability of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels considerably initially increased from 78 ± 2 to 157 ± 2 g $g^{-1}$ and then decreased to 149 ± 1 g $g^{-1}$ with increase in thermal cross-linking time. Insufficient cross-linking time led to the formation of fragile pore walls with poor structural stability, which were unable to support the swelling behavior of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels. However, prolonged heating time induced excessive cross-linking, resulting in overly rigid pore walls that restricted the swelling capacity of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels. In Fig. 5b, the water retention of the aerogel showed an increased trend from 66 ± 4.0 to 122 ± 3.2 g $g^{-1}$ with increase of thermal cross-linking time. These results highlighted the critical influence of cross-linking degree on the water absorption and retention performances of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels. # 3.4. Water absorption performance of aerogels under salts and pH

Herein, the effects of ionic strength, ion types, and pH values on the absorption performance were evaluated. Liquid absorption capacities at various ionic strengths by adding various concentrations of NaCl (from 0.1 to 0.9 wt%) were experimentally studied. As shown in Fig. 6b, the absorption capacity of aerogels decreased from 60 ± 1 to 37 ± 1 g $g^{-1}$ with increasing NaCl concentration; this decrease was attributed to the screening effect of salt ions and the weakening dissociation of -COONa with the increasing ionic strength (Fig. 6a). Moreover, the liquid absorption capacities of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels in the ionic solutions of NaCl, KCl, CaCl₂, MgCl₂, AlCl₃, and FeCl₃ were measured to be 37 ± 0.8,

32 ± 1.4, 17 ± 0.1, 17 ± 0.7, 11 ± 1.1, and 11 ± 0.6 g g $g^{-1}$, respectively (Fig. 6c). At equivalent molar concentrations, the liquid absorption capacity progressively decreased with increasing charges. This was because (1) cations introduced in salt solutions reduced the osmotic pressure gradient between the aerogel network and surrounding solution because of the charge screening effect and (2) multivalent cations underwent specific coordination reactions with carboxylic groups ($-\text{COO}^-$) in the aerogel, thereby reducing the interaction between CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels and water molecules.

The pH of a solution is crucial for determining the absorption capacity, as shown in Fig. 6d; the absorption capacity initially increased from $16 \pm 0.2$ to $152 \pm 2.9 \text{ g} \text{ g}^{-1}$ and then sharply decreased to $16 \pm 0.2 \text{ g} \text{ g}^{-1}$ with increase in pH from 1 to 13. With increase in pH while conditions remaining acidic (i.e., pH<7), -COOH groups gradually changed into $-COO^{-}$ groups and the electrostatic repulsion in the network structure enhanced, which facilitated swelling behavior and improved absorption capacity [45]. With increase in pH from 7 to 13, on the one hand, the charge screening effects became stronger, shielding the carboxylate anions and suppressing the anion-anion repulsion. On the other hand, the ionic strength in the medium increased, thereby reducing absorption capacity [46,47].

# 3.5. Water retention performance of aerogels under centrifugation and heating conditions

Water retention performance of aerogels is important in practical application, which is evaluated by centrifugation and heating methods (Fig. 7a). First, the water retention performance of swollen CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels was determined through centrifugal dewatering experiments conducted at increasing centrifugal speed ranging from 3000 to 15,000 r/min. As shown in Fig. 7b, after 30 min of centrifugation at 3000, 6000, 9000, 12000, and 15,000 r/min, the water retention ratios were measured to be 100 %, 91 ± 0.1 %, 85 ± 0.2 %, 80 ± 1.1 %, and 76 ± 0.6 %, respectively.

The water retention performances of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels were also systematically evaluated at 40 °C, 60 °C, and 80 °C, as shown in Fig. 7c. Swollen aerogel exhibited temperature and time-dependent water retention behaviors. After 5 h of heat treatment at 40 °C, 60 °C, and 80 °C, the water retention ratio of swollen aerogel were 90 ± 0.8 %, 79 ± 1.0 %, and 68 ± 0.2 %, respectively. More remarkably, even upon extending the treatment time to 11 h at both 40 °C and 60 °C, the water retention ratios remained at 77 ± 0.6 % and 58 ± 1.3 %, respectively. These results indicated that absorbed water could be effectively locked in the cross-linked network of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels, contributing to their relatively stable water retention capacity.

The CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels showed satisfactory water absorption and retention capabilities in comparison with previously reported biobased absorbents and commercial diaper (Table 6) [21,33–35,48–56].

Moreover, the compression strength of the CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogel was 232 kPa under 50 % strain (Fig. S6), demonstrating good mechanical structural stability. With the strengths of great sustainability and environmental friendliness, reliable water absorption capabilities, and remarkable water retention stability, the prepared CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels have potential application in the absorbent layer of personal hygiene products (Fig. 7d). Despite these advancements, this study has several limitations. First, it is difficult for freeze-drying method to prepare the large-sized CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogel absorbent, and it needs to develop the appropriate drying techniques of aerogel absorbent for satisfying the industrialization large-sized demands. Second, the liquid absorption capability of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels absorbent in ionic solutions decreased due to the charge screening effect, so it needs to enhance the ion tolerance of CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels absorbent in practical applications.

# 4. Conclusion

In summary, a new bio-based aerogel absorbent was prepared from CMC-Na and CNF cross-linked with CA via freeze-drying method and subsequent thermal cross-linking technique. The effect of preparation conditions (i.e., freezing temperatures and thermal cross-linking durations) and component doses (i.e. CMC-Na, CA and CNF) on the water absorption and retention performance of aerogels has been systematically studied. The maximum water absorption (153 ± 3 g $g^{-1}$) and water retention (123 ± 4 g $g^{-1}$) capacities were achieved under the optimum conditions, which were found to be freezing temperature – 80 °C, thermal cross-linking time 35 min, CMC-Na 7 wt%, CA 0.005 wt%, and CNF 0.1 wt%. The CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels showed satisfactory water absorption and retention capabilities. These remarkable properties suggested that CMC-Na/CA/CNF aerogels could be tailored to meet the requirements of diverse water management applications.
