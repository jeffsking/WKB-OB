## ABSTRACT

This study explores the development of citric acid (CA)-crosslinked superabsorbent hydrogels (SAHs) based on cellulose derivatives as sustainable alternatives to conventional petroleum-based superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) used in personal hygiene products. A biodegradable and biocompatible hydrogel formulation was synthesized using CA as a green crosslinking agent, with sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (Na-CMC) as the primary polymer and hydroxethyl cellulose (HBC) as the secondary polymer, in varying ratios. The effects of polymer composition, swelling medium, pH, and water/saline solution absorption under load (AUL) were systematically evaluated by employing thermal analysis, rheology, water absorption capacity, swelling/deswelling kinetics, and soil biodegradation tests. Biocompatibility was also assessed via indirect contact cytotoxicity assays using mouse fibroblasts cells. The optimized formulation, particularly the CMC–1.5 % CA hydrogel, exhibited high equilibrium water absorption ($Q_{eq}$) of 110 g/g in saline media at 38 °C, favorable swelling/deswelling behavior, rheological robustness, significant biodegradability (72 % within 7 weeks under composting conditions), and non-cytotoxicity (>80 % cell viability). These results highlight the potential of these hydrogels as biodegradable, biocompatible materials aligned with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), especially for eco-friendly hygiene applications.

## 1. Introduction

Superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) are hydrophilic homopolymers or copolymers that function as hydrogels, capable of absorbing and retaining large amounts of water relative to their weight. Hydrogels typically are highly porous, macroscopic, water-insoluble, and three-dimensional (3D) polymeric networks with a ductile texture. These materials can absorb and retain water hundreds of times their own weight, undergoing significant swelling without dissolving or losing structural integrity due to optimal crosslinking [1]. Hydrogels made from SAPs constitute superabsorbent hydrogels (SAHs) and retain water through hydrogen bonding between their hydrogen-bond-forming functional groups, such as carboxylate (—COO$^{-}$) and water molecules. The amount of fluid absorbed by SAHs depends on their structural characteristics and environmental factors such as temperature, pH, and the ionic strength of the surrounding solution [2]. Commercially available SAHs are primarily derived from non-renewable petroleum-based

sources. Synthetic SAPs, such as polyacrylic acids, polyacrylamides, polyacrylonitriles, and polyvinyl alcohols, are typically high-molecular-weight, non-biodegradable polymers with superior water absorption compared to natural biopolymers like chitosan, starch, and cellulose [2].

Due to their superior water absorption capabilities, synthetic SAPs are widely used in absorbent hygiene products (AHPs) designed to manage bodily fluids, secretions, and excrement across various stages of life. These products include baby diapers and training pants, adult urinary incontinence products (e.g., adult briefs and pads), and feminine hygiene products, such as sanitary pads and liners [3]. Since the 1940s, disposable synthetic diapers have become an essential baby care product worldwide. Among the AHPs mentioned, diapers are the largest consumers of SAPs. Each year, billions of disposable diapers are discarded into landfills across North America and other countries, creating significant waste management challenges [4,5]. Synthetic diapers consist of four primary layers. The top sheets and protective back sheets are typically made of non-biodegradable polypropylene (PP) or

polyethylene (PE) plastics. The middle acquisition–distribution layer, also known as the guiding layer, is composed of non-woven polyesters. Meanwhile, the absorbent core generally consists of fluff pulp combined with synthetic SAPs (such as polyacrylates) encapsulated by cellulose [6].

According to the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), a single disposable diaper can take up to 450 years to decompose in a landfill [7,8]. Additionally, absorbent hygiene product (AHP) waste significantly contributes to carbon emissions, particularly through incineration [8,9], posing challenges to achieving the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals (UN SDGs). Growing concerns about the environmental impact of diaper waste management, along with increasing demand for eco-friendly materials from sustainable sources, have driven global research into sustainable biopolymers [10–12]. Cellulose is the most abundant biopolymerer on Earth, primarily derived from plants. Due to their attractive physicochemical and mechanical properties, hydrogels synthesized from cellulose and its etherified or esterified derivatives present a promising and sustainable alternative to synthetic superabsorbent polymers [13,14] derived from non-renewable materials. Given their biocompatibility and inherent physicochemical properties, significant research has focused on developing biopolymeric hydrogels for diverse applications [15,16]. In particular, cellulose and other polysaccharide-based hydrogels synthesized via crosslinking with various agents have been studied [17,18].

In this study, we synthesized bio-based and biocompatible SAHs using sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) and hydroxethyl cellulose (HEC) as simultaneous base polymers and crosslinked with CA as an eco-friendly alternative to synthetic SAHs for personal hygiene products. This study uniquely identifies and validates a low-proportion citric acid crosslinking condition (1.5 % CA relative to total polymer mass) that optimizes swelling performance and minimizes crosslinker consumption—an approach not reported in previous CA-crosslinked polymeric hydrogel studies [17,18,32,35–39]. The physicochemical properties of these hydrogels were analyzed using various analytical tools to assess their structural and morphological characteristics. To evaluate the performance of the new eco-friendly SAHs, the swelling ratio (SR) in water and saline (0.9 % w/v NaCl solution) was measured and compared to commercially available SAHs used in diapers and hy-giene products. We also examined the effect of pH changes in the swelling medium (saline) on hydrogel performance, as well as their absorption efficacy under specific loads (absorption under load). Furthermore, we investigated the biodegradability of the swollen hydrogels in a soil mix and assessed the biocompatibility of the eluted liquid from the hydrogels with mouse fibroblasts cells. This study highlights the development of optimally crosslinked, sustainable hydrogels with high water absorption capacity, favorable absorption kinetics, and low deswelling rates with biodegradability and safety attributes. The proposed material offers a promising, eco-friendly, and sustainable alternative to conventional superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) for personal hygiene applications while also supporting the achievement of multiple Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

### 2.2.1. Synthesis of hydrogels

The overall synthesis and purification steps are illustrated in Scheme I. For the synthesis of the CA-crosslinked hydrogels, the polymers were combined in specific ratios (as shown in Table 1), with sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (Na-CMC) serving as the primary polymer and 2-hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC) as the secondary polymer. In a typical procedure, the polymers were weighed according to the selected ratio as dry mass using an analytical balance (Ohaus, GT 410).

The polymer blend was then mixed with the required volume of preheated (at 30—35 °C) DI water in a 2 L borosilicate beaker, achieving a 2 % (w/v) total polymer concentration. This concentration was chosen to ensure optimal viscosity for homogenization, followed by subsequent mixing with the crosslinker and to ensure the ease of transfer to silicone molds.

The polymer-water mixture was homogenized for approximately 10 min using a Fisher Brand Homogenizer 850. After the polymer had dissolved or dispersed, an appropriate volume of 0.1 M citric acid solution was added to ensure the crosslinker remained 1.5 % (weight) of the polymer used and mixed again for 30 min to ensure uniform distribution of the crosslinker. The mixture was then dried in a convection oven (30 °C for 24 h). This pre-drying stage removed absorbed water and initiated crosslinking at one end of the CA molecule, as shown in Scheme II.

Following this, the temperature was increased to 85 °C for an additional 24 h to complete the crosslinking process at both ends of the CA molecule and form a film through drying at this temperature. The resulting dried mass was then carefully peeled off and ground into coarse granules using an immersion blender (BRAUN Multi Quick 5 V) and stored in a dry, cool place until further use.

# 2.2.4. Thermal analyses

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted to evaluate the degradation properties of cellulose-based hydrogels. The analysis utilized a TGA 550 instrument (TA Instruments, DE, USA) under a nondrying protocol. Approximately 10 mg of dry samples were weighed using an Ohaus AP250D analytical balance and placed in platinum pans. The samples were heated from room temperature to 700 °C at a rate of 20 °C/min under an inert nitrogen (N₂) atmosphere. The nitrogen flow rate was set to 60 mL/min, supplemented by a balanced flow of 40 mL/min.

# 2.2.5. Rheology analyses of the hydrogels

The viscoelastic properties of the hydrogel samples were analyzed using a HAAKE Mars III parallel plate rheometer (Thermo Scientific, United States) at a temperature of 25 °C using a 3.5 cm diameter parallel-plate geometry. To prepare the samples, the dried hydrogel was

rehydrated by adding de-ionized water to achieve a final concentration of 4.2 mg hydrogel per mL of water. The hydrogel sample was placed between the top and bottom plates for testing with a gap of 0.5 mm. To identify the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) of the hydrogel samples, an amplitude sweep analysis was performed over a strain range of 0.001 % to 10 % at a frequency of 1 Hz. Subsequently, a frequency sweep test, ranging from 0.01 Hz to 10 Hz, was conducted to evaluate the visco-elastic properties of the hydrogel within the LVR. For subsequent linear rheological experiments, a strain within the LVR was used, with fresh samples for each test. Dynamic oscillatory experiments were conducted over a frequency range of 0.1–100 rad/s within the LVR, yielding the storage modulus ($G$) and loss modulus ($G$). The storage modulus ($G$) represents the elastic, non-dissipative component of the material, while the loss modulus ($G^*$) reflects its viscous, dissipative behavior. The relationship between angular frequency ($\omega$) and the dynamic moduli ($G$, $G^*$) was analyzed using the power-law model, as described by Eqs. (1) (4) provided below.

$$ G' = G'_{0}\omega^{\pi'} \quad (1) $$

$$ G'' = G''_0 \omega^{n''} \quad (2) $$

$$ \tan\delta = G'/G' \quad (3) $$

$$ |\eta^*| = \frac{\sqrt{(G)^2 + (G'')^2}}{\omega} \quad (4) $$

where $G'_0$ represents storage modulus, $G''_0$ is the loss modulus, the exponents $n'$ and $n''$ are dimensionless numbers and represent the influence of angular frequency on each modulus, and $|\eta^*|$ denotes the complex viscosity.

# 2.2.6. Absorption/swelling in swelling media and effects of pH of swelling media

The swelling kinetics and equilibrium absorption behavior of the hydrogels (HGs) were analyzed gravimetrically at room temperature (RT, 23 °C) and 38 °C using a 0.9 % (w/v) aqueous NaCl solution (representing urine) and deionized (DI) water, at their default pH 6.2 ± 0.8 and 6.4 ± 0.4 respectively. Approximately 200–250 mL of each liquid medium was measured into separate beakers and temporarily sealed with parafilm. For tests conducted at 38 °C, the beakers were placed in a water bath maintained at 38 °C, equipped with a temperature probe, and thermally equilibrated for 1 h. For the 24 h swelling tests, hydrogel samples (–0.2 g) were submerged in 250–300 mL of DI water or saline solution in pre-labeled beakers and allowed to rest at RT or 38 °C, depending on the experimental setup. Moving forward, due to the heterogeneous distribution of superabsorbent materials inside commercial products (such as diapers and training pants), these materials were excluded from all swelling/absorption, deswelling, and kinetics tests.

To evaluate the absorption/swelling efficacies of the HGs over pH variation of the swelling media (saline, in this case), the same procedure and experimental set-up as mentioned above were followed, utilizing pH-adjusted saline with pH values of $5.1 \pm 0.7$ and $8.3 \pm 0.4$ to represent acidic and alkaline swelling media, respectively. However, to evaluate the dynamic swelling of the HGs, additional kinetics tests were

conducted at 38 °C in saline at default pH using the same hydrogel formulations to simulate the average urination environment in diapers.

From the dry and swollen mass of the HG, the swelling ratio (SR), water content percentage (WC%), equilibrium water absorption ($Q_{eq}$) was calculated using Eqs. (6)–(8), where $W_D$ is the dry mass of the hydrogel and $W_s$ is the swollen mass of the hydrogel:

$$ SR = \frac{W_S}{W_D} \quad (6) $$

$$ \text{WC}\% = \left(\frac{W_s - W_D}{W_s}\right) \times 100\% \qquad (7) $$

$$Q_{eq} = \frac{W_S - W_D}{W_D} \qquad (8)$$

# 2.2.7. Absorption under load/pressure (AUL/AUP)

In all the routine tests of 'absorbency under load/pressure' (AUL/AUP) reported in literature, the measurement concept of swelling/absorption of water was first developed by Ramazani-Harandi et al., and the standard tool employed for conducting such tests is the MultiTest 2.5-xt apparatus [21,22] (manufactured by Mccmesin). ## 2.2.8. Soil disintegration, biodegradation, and biocompatibility tests

The soil biodegradation test was conducted using a modified method of ASTM D5988. While ASTMD5988 typically prescribes incubation at 20–28 °C (±2 °C) for 6–12 months, this study conducted soil degradation tests at an elevated temperature of 58 °C to accelerate degradation and to align with industrial composting conditions and maintain a thermophilic environment. Details of the test protocol are presented in

the supporting information section (S2.2.11). For the biocompatibility test, NH3T3 mouse fibroblasts cells (ATCC, US) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10 f fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5 % CO₂ atmosphere. The culture medium was changed every other day, and cells were passed upon reaching ~90 % confluency. The biocompatibility experiments were conducted using cells between passages 28 and 32. Details of the test are presented in the supporting information section (S2.2.12).

# 3.2. X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS) analyses

The XPS spectra of carbon (Fig. 2) were analyzed to confirm the successful synthesis of hydrogels from Na-CMC and HEC poly-saccharides with the citric acid (CA) crosslinker. In the unmodified N-CMC and HEC polymer chains, three peaks appeared at 284.4 eV, 286.1 eV, and 287.2–287.9 eV, corresponding to C–C/C–H, C–O/C–O–C (ether), and O–C–O/O–C=O linkages, respectively–characteristic of cellulose-based materials [27–31]. A peak between 288.2 eV and 289 eV typically indicates O–C=O groups, though it may overlap with the C–O/C–O–C (ether) region (~288 eV), as seen in hydroxethyl cellulose (HEC), where carbonyl (C=O) groups are absent [28,29].

For citric acid (CA)-crosslinked hydrogels synthesized from Na-CMC and HEC polymers, significant changes in the peak area under the curve (AUC) were observed. Citric acid forms ether linkages with polymer chains (Na-CMC or Na-CMC/HEC). Since Na-CMC contains abundant carbonyl (C=O) groups due to its carboxymethyl content, many of these groups are consumed during hydrogel formation, establishing ester linkages with citric acid's terminal carboxylic acid groups (–COOH).

Additionally, the —C=O peaks areas for the CMC-HEC (9:1)—1.5 % CA and CMC-HEC (3:1)—1.5 % CA hydrogels also follow the same trend and align with the anticipated crosslinking. A similar trend in the FTIR spectra, where the C=O peak intensity diminished, further validates this conversion of carboxymethyl groups into ester linkages via CA crosslinking. Together, the XPS and FTIR results confirm the successful crosslinking of polysaccharides through citric acid and the formation of hydrogels.

# 3.3. Hydrogel synthesis and yield

Challenges in crosslinking Na-CMC-based hydrogels have been reported in the literature, primarily due to electrostatic repulsions between carboxylate (—COO—) groups on the polyelectrolyte chains in solution [32]. However, incorporating HEC as a secondary polymer enhances inter-chain rather than intra-chain crosslinking [32,33]. Hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC) is a non-electrolyte with amphipilic properties, allowing it to function similarly to a surfactant. Since HEC lacks charged functional groups, it can form stable networks through hydrophobic interactions with itself or with other charged/uncharged polymer chains during hydrogel synthesis [33,34]. During the HEC-consisting hydrogel synthesis, the absence of charged functional groups in HEC facilitates the crosslinking reactions, as reported in literature, between CMC and HEC via citric acid (CA) through nucleophilic (Sn₂) attack, forming the ester linkages. The representative schematic chemical structure of CA-crosslinked HG is presented in Fig. 1a.

Several studies reported various amounts of citric acid as crosslinkers usage, ranging from 0.5 to 40 % of citric acid [17,18,32,35–39] to the total amount of polymers considered for hydrogel synthesis. The synthesis of citric acid (CA)-crosslinked hydrogels involved a double esterification crosslinking process between polymer chains, leaving the reaction mass residue as a film over two stages [16,26,43,44]. In all syntheses utilizing CA, a pre-drying step at 30 °C for 24 h was required to remove absorbed water. This was followed by curing at 85 °C for 24 h to allow the crosslinking reaction to complete [46,47]. A notable ~85 % yield was observed in the crosslinking of cellulose/derivatives of cellulose with CA, attributed to the ease of ester bond formation between the functional groups of Na-CMC [carboxymethyl (CH₂COO) groups) or the hydroxyl (—OH) groups of all polysaccharides and the carboxyl (—COOH) groups of the CA molecules [46,47] as shown in Fig. 1c. The crosslinking process begins with the formation of a highly reactive anhydride species on one end of the CA molecule through dehydration during heating. This anhydride rapidly reacts with hydroxyl groups of the polysaccharide chains, forming ester bonds. Additionally, CA undergoes further esterification by forming another cyclic anhydride, which facilitates the creation of diester bridges. These bridges promote crosslinking between polymer chains, thereby enhancing the structural integrity of the hydrogel [46–49].

Wen dispersed in water, CMC chains experience strong interchain repulsions due to the anionic charges of their carboxyl groups. Similarly, CA molecules, which possess three ionizable carboxyl groups, encounter repelling forces during the crosslinking reaction. The incorporation of hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC), a non-ionic polymer, as a secondary component in the hydrogel formulation helps to mitigate these interchain repulsions [50,51]. This effect is particularly notable when CMC and HEC are used in a 9:1 ratio, resulting in a slightly higher yield due to improved crosslinking efficiency via CA molecules. However, a further increase in the HEC content (e.g., to a 3:1 ratio) may mask the surface hydroxyl groups [32,52] of the primary polymer (CMC), thereby slightly reducing the overall yield. Regardless of the type or ratio of secondary polymers used, the overall yield of CA-crosslinked hydrogels remained above 85 % across multiple synthesis batches. Given the minimal CA usage (1.5 wt% of total polymers) and the reaction mechanism involved, it is assumed that the entire CA content is fully utilized for crosslinking. Consequently, the literature does not mention any purification or washing steps, which may explain the consistently high yield observed in these synthesized hydrogel samples.

Although the inclusion of HEC during CA-crosslinked HG synthesis has been reported to enhance crosslinking when combined with anionic polymer, Na-CMC, at specific ratios, it also contributes to higher water sorption capacities in water without any electrolytes present or ions and slightly looser morphologies (in the partially swollen state). It was found that beyond an optimal ratio (9:1 primary polymer to HEC), additional HEC (for example, Na-CMC;HEC = 3:1) moderately reduced water absorption/swelling capacity from non-electrolyte water. Although the CMC;HEC = 1:1 and 1:3 ratio was attempted for synthesis, for the abovementioned reason (very poor or no absorption), these two HGs were excluded from this study.

# 3.4. Thermal analyses

The thermal decomposition curves of the constituent polymers and the corresponding hydrogels (HGs) made via citric acid (CA) cross-linking are presented in Fig. 3. The thermogram in Fig. 3 shows two main stages of decomposition [53,54] for Na-CMC and HEC polymers, as well as for the synthesized HGs. For the constituent polymers (CMC and HEC), the first stage began at 30 °C and ended at 100 °C, which could be ascribed to the loss or removal of free moisture from the surface of the polymers and/or physically entrapped water within the polymers. A similar stage of water removal was seen over the progressing temperature range of 35 °C–250 °C for the three hydrogels reported in the plots, where the weight loss is mostly due to the loss of absorbed water and most probably residual traces of unbound citric acid molecules within the hydrogels.

Literature suggests that the decomposition of anhydrous citric acid usually starts at 150 °C with a significant mass loss [55]. However, the major weight loss, notably over 50 % weight loss trends, were observed during the second stage of the TGA thermogram, which were seen at around 260 °C to 380 °C for the polymers and the corresponding HGs. Such significant weight losses in the second stage could be attributed to the thermal decomposition of the polymer backbone and the hydrogel skeleton. The two stages of decomposition for the CA-crosslinked hydrogels (HGs) are more evident from the DTGA thermogram, presented in Fig. 3b.

The linear dynamic viscoelastic properties of these experimented hydrogels are presented in Fig. 4. As expected, the water absorption efficiencies from the saline solution (0.9 % w/v NaCl, 0.154 M) were moderately lower for both the synthesized hydrogels (HGs) and commercial superabsorbent polymer (SAP), sodium polyacrylate, as shown in Fig. 6c and d. When SAPs are immersed in water, the associated counterions ($Na^+$) distribute between the swollen hydrogel network and the swelling medium [68]. However, in saline as the swelling medium, this ion exchange process was disrupted, which drastically reduced the absorption and swelling performance. This may explain why SAPs perform slightly lesser in saline compared to the lab-made hydrogel formulations. The CMC—1.5 % CA hydrogels, being more hydrophilic than SAPs and containing lower sodium content than SAPs, demonstrated slightly better water absorption ($Q_{eq}^n$ 92 g/v vs 85 g/g and 110 g/g/v 93 g/g) from saline, at RT and 38 °C, respectively. This makes the CMC-based HGs particularly advantageous for hygiene products like diapers.

Normal saline as a swelling medium contains an abundance of Na + and Cl - ions, which modifies the ion-mediated osmotic pressure difference [69] between the swelling medium and the semi-swelled hydrogel network. For Na-CMC-based hydrogels, the carboxylate groups (—COO⁻) interact electrostatically with Na⁺ ions, further influencing absorption behavior, as the water dipoles compete with the bulk Na⁺ to form hydrogen bonds with the accessible —COO⁻ groups. This phenomenon leads to the formation of non-ionized sodium carboxylate groups (—COO⁻-Na⁺) within the hydrogel, thereby reducing the number of ionized carboxylate groups (—COO⁻) available for hydrogen bonding with water dipoles, as illustrated in Fig. 7.

Moreover, the water activity coefficient in the saline solution decreases due to ion hydration (solvation) between NaCl and water dipoles [70]. This reduces the driving force for water influx into the hydrogel and limits the availability of free water dipoles for hydrogen bonding, as these dipoles are already engaged in hydrating $Na^+ + Cl^-$ ions (Fig. 7). Consequently, the water absorption and swelling ratio of the synthesized hydrogels in saline solution were significantly reduced, exhibiting more than a threefold decrease compared to their swelling in deionized (DI) water.

The absorption/swelling performances of the other two hydrogels [CMC:HEC (3:1)—1.5 %CA and CMC:HEC (3:1)—1.5 %CA] were significantly lower in saline at RT and 38 °C due to the same reasons discussed above. Despite variations in the equilibrium water absorption ($Q_{eq}$ g/g) and swelling ratio (SR), all samples presented in Figs. 6a–d reached over 90 % saturation in terms of WC% after 24 h, regardless of the swelling medium used (as shown in Fig. S8).

Several studies have reported the equilibrium water absorption capacities ($Q_{eq}$, g/g) or swelling ratios (SR) of CA-crosslinked hydrogels synthesized from various polymers, with experiments conducted at varying temperatures, swelling media, and durations. # 3.8. Absorption under load/pressure (AUL/AUP)

The swelling of a hydrogel in water is primarily influenced by the polymer network's degree of crosslinking, polymer chain hydrophilicity, solution ionic strength, pH, temperature, and hydrogel pore size, all of which affect the balance between osmotic pressure driving water absorption and the elastic forces resisting expansion [77]. Other notable external factors, such as the presence of an adequate volume of the swelling media, the presence of multivalent minerals, and the existence of external force or pressure [78,79] upon the swelling-HGs directly and drastically affect the absorption/swelling efficacy of the HG of interest.

As shown in our previous work, the charged HGs (anionic, mostly due to the presence of the primary constituent polymer, Na-CMC) are

capable of absorbing more water from a swelling media when there are no additional ions (say, $Na^+$, $Cl^-$) in the media. Similar patterns were observed when the HGs were tested under a specific load in DI water. Regardless of the nature of the employed swelling media, absorption efficiencies by all the HGs were significantly impacted under the load/pressure used during the experiment. The $Q_{eq}$ (g/g) was reduced by 39 % for CMC-1.5 %CA in DI water when tested under load at 38 °C, and the CMC:HEC (3:1)-1.5 %CA hydrogels showed an 82.6 % decrease in water absorption under the noted load (corrected against buoyancy, ~170 g), as seen from the plots presented in Fig. 10. # 3.9. Soil disintegration/biodegradation tests

Biodegradability was evaluated using two main metrics: a soil disintegration test and a physical disintegration method based on weight loss. The biodegradation percentage (Fig. 11a) was determined by weekly measurements of CO₂ evolution, normalized to the known carbon content of each hydrogel, as described earlier in the methods section. Weight loss (Fig. 11b) was calculated by recording the weekly mass of each sample jar/setup.

According to the literature [80], the most common mechanisms driving hydrogel degradation include hydrolysis, solubilization/ionization, oxidation, photodegradation, and enzymatic breakdown. Factors such as crosslink chemistry, degree of crosslinking, polymer nature, and charge density significantly influence hydrogel degradation [81]. In this study, thermal energy (58 °C, used in the soil disintegration test) and microbial activity are believed to have facilitated ester bond hydrolysis followed by enzymatic degradation, particularly for the citric acid (CA)-crosslinked hydrogels.

As shown in Fig. 11a, CMC-1.5 %CA hydrogel degraded the fastest, followed by CMC:HEC (9:1)-1.5 %CA, while CMC:HEC (3:1)-1.5 %CA showed the slowest degradation. ## 3.10. Biocompatibility tests

The cytotoxicity of CA-crosslinked hydrogels was assessed in vitro using NIH3T3 mouse fibroblasts cells via a leaching method [83]. Hydrogels were incubated in serum-free DMEM at 37 °C with agitation for 24 h, and the resulting leachate was applied as the culture medium. After a 24-h exposure, cell viability was measured (Fig. 12a, b), revealing normalized viabilities of 80.6 ± 17.0% for CMC-CA and 120.0 ± 12.4% for CMC:HEC-CA hydrogels (Fig. 12e). No significant

differences were observed between either the hydrogel group or the positive control ($p = 0.170$ for CMC-CA; $p = 0.156$ for CMC:HEC-CA).

Morphologically, cells retained elongated, fibroblasts-like shapes (as seen in Figs. 12a, b, and c) characteristic of NIH3T3 cells [84]. These findings exceed the 70 % viability threshold defined by ISO 10993-5:2009 for non-cytotoxic materials, supporting the hydrogels' cytocompatibility. It is worth noting that the leaching method may have promoted greater diffusion of components than would occur during typical skin contact. Moreover, the human skin, especially the stratum corneum, acts as a robust bio-barrier against external agents [85]. While further biocompatibility testing is warranted for consumer applications, these CA-crosslinked hydrogels demonstrate strong potential as safe alternatives to commercial sodium polyacrylate in personal hygiene products.

# 4. Conclusions

Hydrogels with the employment of only 1.5 % (w/w) naturally occurring citric acid (CA) crosslinker with sustainable cellulose derivatives—sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (Na-CMC) and hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC)—were developed via a simple, scalable process with yields exceeding 85 %. This effective strategy highlights the potential of sustainable polymer-based hydrogels for personal hygiene applications. The synthesized hydrogels, based on Na-CMC and CMC:HEC blends with the newly reported CA proportion, exhibited high water absorption and retention capacities. Notably, the CMC—1.5 % CA hydrogel achieved an equilibrium water absorption (Qeq) of 110 g/g in saline (164 g/g in water), while the CMC:HEC hydrogel reached 97 g/g in saline (429 g/g in water) at 38 °C.
