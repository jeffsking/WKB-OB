# Chapter 13 — Recycling of Plastic Waste

## 1. Chapter Overview

Chapter 13 (E. M. Abdel-Bary) surveys the recovery of plastic waste, framed by the environmental and economic pressure of ever-growing polymer landfill. It opens with the dominant waste-stream polymer mix (LDPE > HDPE > PP > PS > PET, with plastic films ~68% LDPE/LLDPE) and then lays out the four canonical routes — primary (clean in-plant), secondary (mechanical, with separation/washing), tertiary (chemical/feedstock) and quaternary (energy recovery) — each with its economics and caveats. The bulk of the chapter is devoted to collection & sorting (SPI codes, density float-sink, colour/electrification/NIR/XRF spectroscopy, selective dissolution) and to material-specific recycling of PET, PVC, PE and HDPE, including the contamination chemistry of reprocessing (gel/crosslink blow-outs, black specks, pigment pro-oxidants). It closes with radiation-technology upgrading and biodegradable polymers. For the hygiene expert the chapter is the sustainability playbook: how to design backsheet/film packaging for recycle, what degradation recycled polyolefins suffer, and which bio-based polymers can serve eco-positioned products.

## 2. Key Viewpoints

### Viewpoint 1 — Films are overwhelmingly LDPE/LLDPE

- Page: p.357
- Quote: > "The majority of plastic films are made from LDPE or linear low-density polyethylene (LLDPE), comprising approximately 68% of the total film production."
- Interpretation: Sets the feedstock reality for any film-recycling programme — polyolefin streams dominate, so hygiene-film recovery should target LDPE/LLDPE/HDPE, not niche resins.
### Viewpoint 2 — Four recycling routes

- Page: p.358
- Quote: > "There are four main approaches to recycling plastics … These are primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary recycling."
- Interpretation: The taxonomy that organises the whole chapter; the hierarchy (reuse > mechanical > chemical > energy) is the decision frame for sustainable film design.
### Viewpoint 3 — Density float-sink is the workhorse separation

- Page: p.364
- Quote: > "Water may be used to separate PP, LDPE and HDPE from PS, PVC and PET. A liquid having a density of about 930 kg/m3 may be used to separate PP and LDPE from HDPE."
- Interpretation: A concrete, implementable method for building a polyolefin film recycle stream — directly usable for backsheet/offcut recovery.
### Viewpoint 4 — Reprocessing creates gels that burst films

- Page: p.372
- Quote: > "Loosely crosslinked regions (known as 'gels') can act as stress concentrations in film and cause 'blow-outs' in bottles made from recycled HDPE."
- Interpretation: The central failure mode of recycled PE: antioxidant depletion during remelting permits crosslinking → gels → weak spots. This is the root cause of "fish-eyes" / web breaks in recycled backsheet.
### Viewpoint 5 — Pigments can catalyse degradation

- Page: p.372
- Quote: > "green pigments are usually based on chromium(III) oxide, which can readily catalyse the thermooxidative degradation of HDPE when present even in trace quantities."
- Interpretation: Colourant choice matters for recycle stability; a "natural" recycled stream free of chromic pigments is explicitly pursued (p.372) — relevant when specifying tinted hygiene films.
### Viewpoint 6 — Biodegradable polymers as an end-of-life option

- Page: p.375
- Quote: > "Polymer types being studied include cellulose derivatives (such as cellulose acetate) [70], polysaccharides such as chitin [71], starch and poly(3-hydroxybutrate) [72]."
- Interpretation: Names the bio-based candidates (cellulose acetate, starch, PHB, chitin) for flushable/degradable hygiene films; the chapter treats degradability as a functional design route, not just disposal.

## 4. Argumentation Process

The chapter argues by levels of value: (1) prevention/collection is cheapest (primary in-plant recycling ideal); (2) once mixed, separation cost dominates, so source-segregation is the lever (p.362); (3) mechanical recycling is downgraded by incompatibility, so compatibilisation helps only in specific cases; (4) chemical recycling recovers feedstock but destroys polymerisation value-added; (5) energy recovery is the last resort with emission liabilities. The contaminant chemistry (§13.6) then explains why recycled PE underperforms: antioxidant consumption → crosslinking/pigment-catalysed degradation → gels/black specks. The biodegradable-polymer section (§13.9) offers an alternative end-of-life logic for short-life films.

## 5. Role in the Book

Ch.13 is the sustainability capstone. It consumes the material science of Ch.8–12: a recycled film's properties are governed by the same orientation, crosslinking, stabilisation and barrier principles already developed, but now complicated by contamination and degradation history. For hygiene R&D it links to Ch.9 (barrier/packaging), Ch.10 (stabilisation/UV), Ch.12 (testing of recycled film) and Ch.8 (surface treatment of recycled webs).
