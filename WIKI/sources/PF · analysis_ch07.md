# Chapter 7 — Interaction of Polymers with the Nitrogen Oxides in Polluted Atmospheres

## 1. Chapter Overview (100–200 words)

Chapter 7 (Pariiskii, Gaponova & Davydov) is a mechanistic review of how atmospheric nitrogen oxides — chiefly NO and NO₂ (with N₂O₄ dimer) — attack solid polymers, closing the book's additive/degradation trilogy by adding gaseous pollutants as an ageing driver alongside heat/UV/oxygen (Ch.3) and HCl (Ch.5). It classifies polymers into three groups (vinyl polyolefins; unsaturated rubbers; amide/urethane/imide) and shows NO₂ is the active radical: it adds to C=C double bonds (Ea 8–16 kJ/mol), abstracts labile H (Ea 56–68 kJ/mol in PE, 60 in PP), and — critically — decomposes polymer hydroperoxides, the very species UV oxidation (Ch.3) creates, via a trace-NOₓ-accelerated chain (Scheme 7.76). Polyamides/urethanes are surprisingly vulnerable through N–H nitrosation that collapses the hydrogen-bond network. The chapter's practical warning: accelerated tests use 10²–10⁴× atmospheric NOₓ and linearly extrapolate — "a priori ambiguous" (p.187) — so real-service lifetimes under pollution are uncertain. For the hygiene reader this mechanistically explains the NOₓ/SOₓ colour-centre intensification noted in Ch.3 (p.89) and flags nylon/PU elastic elements in traffic-exposed storage.

## 2. Key Viewpoints (3–6, each with page + verbatim quote + interpretation)

### Viewpoint 1 — NO₂ is the active pollutant radical; NO mostly terminates chains

- Page: p.188
- Quote: > "The paramagnetic nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) is more active compared with NO. This gas is universally present in equilibrium with its dimer molecule: 2NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄."
- Interpretation: NO₂ (not NO) does the damage; NO mostly recombines with radicals and can inhibit oxidation (p.208). This dual role is why pollutant ageing is subtle and test-conditions matter.
### Viewpoint 2 — NO₂ adds to C=C and abstracts H; double bonds are the easy entry

- Page: p.189
- Quote: > "NO₂ reacts at room temperature with the >C=C< double bonds originally contained in PE with the formation of dinitro compounds and nitronitrites … The activation energy of the NO₂ addition to the double bonds of PE is 8-16 kJ/mol. The activation energies of H atom abstraction are 56-68 kJ/mol in PE and 60 kJ/mol in PP."
- Interpretation: Unsaturation (already a UV weak point, Ch.3) is also the NOₓ entry point at near-zero barrier; H-abstraction is far harder. So a film's residual double bonds govern its pollution sensitivity.
### Viewpoint 3 — Accelerated-test extrapolation to atmospheric NOₓ is unreliable

- Page: p.187
- Quote: > "To establish the effect of a given aggressive gas on a particular polymer, the reaction is generally studied at pollutant concentrations that are much higher than those actually existing in polluted atmospheres. The results obtained by this means are then linearly extrapolated to the concentrations of reactants found in the atmosphere. This expedient is, a priori, ambiguous."
- Interpretation: Every service-life claim from accelerated NOₓ testing should be discounted; the chapter itself questions linear scaling because acceleration changes the rate-limiting step.
### Viewpoint 4 — NO₂ decomposes polymer hydroperoxides (the UV legacy) via trace-accelerated chain

- Page: p.207–208
- Quote: > "the interaction of hydroperoxide with NO₂ is more likely to proceed according to the scheme: ROOH + N₂O₃ → [ROONO] + HNO₂ … ROONO → RO• + NO₂ … The increase in the rate of hydroperoxide decomposition with time can be related to reactions proceeding with participation of such nitroso compounds."
- Interpretation: This is the mechanistic bridge to Ch.3: UV-made hydroperoxides (p.86–88) are detonated by NOₓ, accelerating chain scission. Autoacceleration comes from nitroso intermediates — a positive feedback loop.
### Viewpoint 5 — Polyamides are surprisingly NO₂-sensitive via N–H nitrosation

- Page: p.197
- Quote: > "nitrosation through the amide group is the main process of PCA transformation in an NO₂ atmosphere, which leads to disintegration of the system of hydrogen bonds."
- Interpretation: Even strong N–H bonds fail because the consequence (hydrogen-bond network collapse) destroys crystalline cohesion. Relevant to nylon hygiene components (elastic leg cuffs, nonwovens).
### Viewpoint 6 — PU films lose tensile strength under NO₂

- Page: p.200
- Quote: > "The exposure of polyurethane films to an NO₂ atmosphere or a mixture of NO₂ with air leads to the progressive reduction of the tensile strength limit."
- Interpretation: PU elastic films (a hygiene elastic element) degrade mechanically under polluted air — a storage/transport risk near traffic or industry.

## 4. Argumentation Process

The author argues by polymer class, then unifies at the radical level:
1. Framing: pollutants (SO₂/O₃/NOₓ) matter; testing-by-extrapolation is flawed (§7.1).
2. Vinyl group (§7.2.1): NO₂ adds to C=C, abstracts H; PE/PP/PVC only mildly change even at high [NO₂]+UV.
3. Unsaturated/rubber (§7.2.2): far more sensitive; ESR proves nitroxyl spin-labels; reaction front is surface (membrane regime).
