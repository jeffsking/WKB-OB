## Key Findings 关键发现

1. **PP is a family, not a resin.** Tacticity, molecular weight and its distribution, comonomer incorporation and controlled-rheology (CR 受控流变) treatment together span a property range wide enough that "commodity resin" understates the material.
2. **Grade-to-process map for fibres — the actionable core of the book.** Stated quantitatively: slit film uses **non-CR homopolymer 2–4 g/10 min**, with higher MFR allowing higher line speed but lower MFR giving higher tenacity at a given draw ratio; continuous filament uses **CR homopolymer ≈35 MFR**, processed near **250 °C**, ~5 dpf, CR chosen for narrow MWD; BCF **12–20 MFR**, normal MWD, ~20 dpf; staple **homopolymer 10–30 MFR**; monofilament **low MFR 1–4 g/10 min** (~250 dpf) for tenacity.
3. **Nonwovens are the single largest PP fibre application**, ahead of carpet and slit film. Three families named: thermobonded staple, spun-bonded, melt-blown. The book notes the fabrics differ in properties and appearance and are **often combined two at a time**.
4. **Spun-bond specifics.** Molten filaments are air-quenched, drawn by high-pressure air, laid on a moving porous belt under vacuum, and bonded by heated calender rolls; the usual resin is **CR homopolymer ≈35 g/10 min MFR**. Spun-bonded fabric is strong.
5. **Melt-blown specifics.** A dedicated die delivers high-temperature air at high velocity to the exiting filaments with additional cool-air quench; solidification occurs very close to the die. Requires **very high MFR, ≈500 g/10 min or higher**, and **narrow MWD**, giving fine fibres and lightweight uniform fabric that is **soft but not strong**.
6. **Barrier mechanism.** Fabrics from fine melt-blown fibres transmit water vapour while resisting penetration of liquid water and aqueous solutions — hence medical use, and the direct analogue for a barrier/backsheet leg.
7. **The strength/softness split drives composite design.** Spun-bonded = strong; melt-blown = soft and high bulk. This is the founding logic of SM/SMS structures.
8. **Pigment-not-dye consequence.** PP cannot readily be dyed, so fibre colour is pigment added at extrusion; colour is distributed through the cross-section, so fibre breakage does not lose colour, and pigmented fibre resists staining and fading. Relevant to any coloured nonwoven with abrasion during converting.
9. **Compounding route (Ch.3).** Maleated PP (PPgMAH) and acrylic-acid-grafted PP (PPgAA) are the workhorse coupling agents; peroxide selection controls the grafting reaction, and MAH level measurably shifts melt flow rate — i.e. functionalisation and rheology are coupled, a caution for any compatibilised PP stream.

## Relevance to Hygiene R&D 与卫材研发的关联

**HIGH 高关联** — this is one of the few sources in the vault that links **resin specification → nonwoven process → delivered fabric property** with numbers attached, and it is the canonical citation for the MFR split between spunbond and meltblown. Directly serves:

- **SSMMS / coverstock work.** The ~35 MFR CR homopolymer (spunbond) vs. ≥500 MFR narrow-MWD (meltblown) pair is exactly what an SSMMS line buys in two different resin lots; see [[Polypropylene Handbook Spunbond and Meltblown Nonwoven Resins]] for the chapter-level extraction and [[Manufactured Fibre Technology Ch19 Spunbonding and Melt-Blowing Processes]] for the process mechanics.
- **PP fibre feedstock selection.** [[Manufactured Fibre Technology Ch16 Polypropylene Fibres]] covers the same fibre families from the fibre-textile side; this handbook supplies the resin-side anchor.
- **Bicomponent / thermal bonding.** ES and PE-PP sheath-core work needs the homopolymer baseline before the sheath-core deviation makes sense — see [[Bicomponent Fibre Technology Development Trends]]. Note honestly: this 2003 edition predates much of the modern metallocene-catalysed nonwoven grade development, so treat the specific MFR figures as period-correct rather than current specification.
- **Adhesive and bonding interface.** The grafted-PP coupling chemistry in Ch.3 is relevant background for adhesion onto polyolefin substrates — see [[Adhesive Handbook Part 2 Fundamental Aspects]].
