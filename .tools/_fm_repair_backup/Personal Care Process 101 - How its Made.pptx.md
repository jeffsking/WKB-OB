#   Process Draws

Control Point: Any point in the process that drives the web continuously and at a fixed speed.

Control Point Examples: driven wrapped rolls
driven nip rolls
vacuum conveyors
function rolls

Draw: The percent difference in speed between two control points.

This effect puts tension on materials being fed into the machine

Why Important:

$\bullet$ Too much draw creates high web tensions which necks down the web as it is stretched or even worse the material breaks

$\bullet$ Too little draw creates low web tensions which makes webs loose, and floppy, and wrinkles or weaves because it is not being controlled

$\bullet$ Different draws are needed depending on material type, basis weight, (single vs. multi-materials) etc.

#   Pressure Bonding

* How does it work?

$\bullet$ Two rolls (one a pattern roll, the other a smooth anvil roll) rotate together to mechanically deform and bond nonwoven under high pressure.

* When is Pressure Bonding Used?

$\bullet$ Often used in combination with adhesive bonding to increase bond strengths between material layers or reduce amount of adhesive needed

* Process Interactions

$\bullet$ Wear of the pattern roll and loading of the nip determines bond consistency and quality

$\bullet$ Material type (or changes) may require a different pattern or a different bonding method

#   Thermal Bonding

* How does it work?

$\bullet$ Similar to pressure bonding, but both rolls are heated to a specific temperature to help melt the non-woven polymers when pressed together between the pattern and anvil rolls

* When is Thermal Bonding Used?

$\bullet$ Materials with similar melt points of their polymer chains work best with thermal bonding

* Process Interactions

* Wear of the pattern roll, loading of the nip, and temperature determine bond consistency and quality

* Precise temperature control is needed to predict roll expansion. Improper gap control leads to over or under bonding and worn bonder pattern roll

$\bullet$ Material type (or changes) may require a different pattern or a different type bonding method

#   Ultrasonic Bonding

#   How does it work?

$\bullet$ A bonding horn is vibrated rapidly (between 20 to 40KHz) which mates against a pattern anvil. When non-woven materials are placed between these two surfaces the energy from the horn transfers into the material through Hysteresis causing the polymer chains to melt

$\bullet$ Geometry of patterned anvil determines type of bond

$\bullet$ Pressure bonding only cold flows amorphous portion due to insufficient time or energy

#   When is Ultrasonic Bonding Used?

* When thermal or pressure bonding alone does not work

* Ultrasonics have enough energy to quickly melt both amorphous and crystalline portions of polymer chains

#   Process Interactions

* High Investment and Maintenance Costs with Ultrasonics compared to thermal or pressure bonding

$\bullet$ Ultrasonic horns and their electronics to vibrate them are sensitive to heat, pressure, and material condition of the horn itself (analogy, a cracked bell does not ring/vibrate well... and various sizes or materials...)

#   How does this work?

$\bullet$ Incoming material travels slower than the base web

$\bullet$ Applicator travels at the base web speed

$\bullet$ Material "slips" on the applicator as it's being pulled until it gets cut

$\bullet$ Cut material speeds up and gets applied to the base web

#   Common issues & solutions

$\bullet$ Vacuum pattern is critical

* Gap is critical – too big, it won't stick, too small, it can cause web handling issue

* Re-pitch modules are based on rotation

* Linear velocity (fpm) = Angular velocity (rpm) X radius (ft)

* Works by changing the effective radius throughout the cycle

$\bullet$ Not all re-pitch modules rotate components or products

* Examples:

* OSCAR module (WEA, Insert Application)

#   Continuous Folding

How does it work?

$\bullet$ The material to be folded enters the infeed plane of the folding board

$\bullet$ (Optional) At the apex, adhesive coated elastic strands meet with the carrier sheet

$\bullet$ Material runs over the wings and fingers before finally wrapping over the finger edges

$\bullet$ The folded material exits the folding board underneath the folding fingers

* Common issues & solutions

* Adhesive & fiber build up

* Examples:

* Flaps and LAWN waist band for BCC

$\bullet$ Geo-folder for Child Care wrapsheet

#   Web Guide Sensor Arrangements

1. Edge Guiding: (critical for aligning one edge to the process)

Examples: Attaching Side Panels or Ears to outside edges of chassis, or folding and aligning flap edges to each other

2. Center Guiding: (critical for centering narrow webs to the process)

Example: most chassis materials that tend to weave or neck down and need to remain centered to the process

3. Center Guiding w/ Two Sensors: (critical for wide webs)


Example: same as #2 above but for really-wide webs
