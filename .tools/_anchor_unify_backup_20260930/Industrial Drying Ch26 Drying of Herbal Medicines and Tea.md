# Drying of Herbal Medicines and Tea

> Source: Mujumdar (ed.), Handbook of Industrial Drying, 4th ed., CRC Press, 2014, Ch.26, pp.633–644 (Guohua Chen & Arun S. Mujumdar).

## Chapter Overview 章节概览

Substrate-oriented survey of how thermal drying degrades or preserves the bioactive value of botanical materials — culinary/medicinal herbs, tea leaves (green/oolong/black), ginseng roots, cut herbal-medicine pieces. Thesis: for these materials the quality of the dried product (retained active ingredients, colour, aroma) — not merely moisture removal — is the design objective, set jointly by temperature, drying method, and moisture-transport path. Includes a quantitative dataset (Tables 26.2–26.5) on convective vs infrared drying of 27 Chinese herbs.

> Note: page numbers derived from PDF-page − 21; Tables 26.2–26.5 contain OCR-garbled cells — only unambiguous values cited.

## Key Viewpoints 关键观点

### 1. Quality = active-ingredient retention 质量即活性成分留存

> "The quality of the herbs depends very much on the contents of active ingredients. It is known that heating or thermal drying, if not carried out properly, can cause a significant loss of the active ingredients." (p.634)

A drying process is judged by what survives, not only by residual moisture.

### 2. The two-sided temperature window 双侧温度窗

> "The selection of proper drying temperature is vital. Too high a temperature may cause loss of active ingredients. On the other hand, too low a temperature may actually accelerate decomposition by promoting enzyme activity within the plant itself or even microbial attack, particularly for sugar-containing substances." (p.634)

A non-monotonic quality-vs-temperature relation: unlike inert engineering webs, biologically active substrates have a lower temperature bound too, because slow drying prolongs the window for enzymatic self-degradation. Degradation is a residence-time problem as much as a peak-temperature problem.

### 3. Tea: drying as compositional control lever 茶叶工艺

> "The majority of the teas can be classified as green tea, oolong tea, and black tea. Each requires a different process and hence a different drying system is required. The quality of dried tea depends to a great extent on the drying system and operating conditions used." (p.635)

Killing-out (green) arrests enzymatic oxidation early; black tea deliberately ferments before high-temperature firing — the same leaf yields opposite products purely through the heat/timing schedule.

### 4. Staged temperature programming 分阶段变温

> "In order to maintain a high drying rate and a low temperature of herb pieces, it is suggested to use staged drying with variation of temperatures of heated air." (p.641)

The Radix Glycyrrhizae data (Table 26.3): staged 30→77→60 °C gave mean rate 6.9 g/kg·min with material max 49 °C, versus 70 °C constant at 8.6 g/kg·min but material max 63 °C and internal ΔT 28 °C — near-70 °C productivity at near-40 °C thermal stress. Mechanism: evaporative cooling pins the solid temperature below air temperature while moisture is high; ramp down before the falling-rate period.

### 5. Heating mode is not one-directional 加热模式影响双向

> "When infrared drying is used, the loss of active ingredients is even higher for some herbs as shown in Table 26.4." (p.641–642)

IR is worse for some actives (peak-temperature-sensitive) and better for others (time-at-temperature-sensitive); combining IR with vibro-fluidized bed "improved the drying rate as well as the quality of the dried product significantly" (p.642).

### 6. Bed depth controls internal uniformity 床深效应

> "When the bed depth is increased from 25 to 50 mm, the drying time is 2.7 times longer and within the material, the maximum temperature differences increased from 10 to 18°C at a drying temperature of 66°C." (p.641)

Doubling bed depth nearly triples drying time and widens internal ΔT — the core lags and overheats relative to the surface; an optimum near 30 mm emerges.

## Research Method 研究方法

Descriptive review + compiled experimental dataset: measured drying rates, allowed vs actual temperatures, and %-ingredient-loss for 27 herbs under hot-air and IR drying, with named assays (HPLC/GC/UV). Data are single-sourced and not generated under a unified experimental design; no transport model or degradation kinetics fitted.

## Strengths and Weaknesses 优劣评估

- Strengths: states the quality objective mechanistically (two-sided window); rare quantitative degradation dataset with assays; bed-depth→ΔT→time coupling with numbers; candidly challenges received "allowed temperature" values as too high (p.641).
- Weaknesses: heavily descriptive, no kinetic framework — results cannot be extrapolated to new schedules; hot-air vs IR runs confound temperature and method; 100 %-loss anomalies for volatile actives (menthol, phenylethyl alcohol) conflate evaporative stripping with thermal decomposition.

## Hygiene Implication 卫材关联

Relevance: ★★☆☆☆ — method-transferable, substrate-irrelevant, stated plainly. Tea, ginseng and herb pieces are living plant tissue with assayable secondary metabolites; fluff pulp, SAP and nonwoven webs have no volatile actives, no enzymes and no microbial self-degradation pathway — their quality variables are fibre bonding/kink, brightness, SAP gel properties, and residual monomer. None of the temperature tables or tea schedules transfers numerically, and no hygiene application is invented.

What does transfer is the methodological architecture:

- Two-sided temperature window as a QA frame (p.634): do not treat "lower temperature = safer" — prolonged wet residence promotes microbial growth/odour in pulp bales and extends residual-monomer side reactions in SAP. Define both an upper bound (fibre hornification, SAP surface-crosslink over-cure) and a lower bound (wet-residence degradation).
- Staged temperature programming instead of single set-points (p.641): front-load heat while the evaporative-cooling sink is strong; ramp down before the falling-rate period.
- Bed depth as a first-order uniformity variable (p.641): run a depth-sweep and pick depth from the internal-ΔT curve, not throughput alone — prevents the core-wet/surface-over-dried defect.
- Benchmark heating mode against the damage mechanism, not rate (p.642–643): screen convective vs radiative modes on the actual assay of interest rather than excluding a mode a priori.
