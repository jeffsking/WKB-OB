## Executive Summary

This was the first **plant-level, in-depth technical exchange** between KC and Unergy (Tianjin; founded 2001, originally a subsidiary of the Tianjin pharmaceutical project, entered hygiene in 2008). The supplier is not a nonwoven manufacturer — it positions itself as a **full-chain enabler of functional formulations**: converting actives into liquids (micro-emulsions), powders (microcapsules) or chips (laminated inserts), then applying them to substrates, laminating, and quantifying them in-house.

Five key judgments emerged:

1. **The supplier's real moat is not the formulation — it is the closed loop of "formulation → application → quantitative verification."** Nonwoven mills can apply liquid but cannot measure uniformity, wet pick-up or per-piece content. Cosmetic houses can formulate but cannot apply to fabric. The supplier has occupied this gap for ~20 years and has built the analytical assets (GC + LC, same instrument class as third-party labs) to defend it.
2. **The most valuable technical clarification of the meeting was "contact cooling ≠ actual temperature reduction."** The supplier openly acknowledged that phase-change materials (PCM) in hygiene products are "mostly a concept" — the loading required for perceivable, sustained heat absorption is high enough to damage the nonwoven's base performance. This single statement settles PCM's positioning at KC.
3. **An unresolved technical deadlock exists: adhesion vs. immediate availability.** The inorganic particles KC needs (PCM / zinc oxide / titanium dioxide type) are neither water- nor oil-soluble; once dried they have zero affinity to fibre and inevitably dust off. The standard fix is encapsulation (microcapsules), but encapsulation requires an external trigger to release — while KC's use case requires efficacy the moment the product is converted. **The two are mechanistically mutually exclusive.** This is the item most in need of a dedicated project.
4. **There is a hard commercial red line:** KC policy does not permit shared/joint patent ownership with suppliers (stated explicitly by KC during the meeting). Any proposal for "joint application" must therefore become either KC sole ownership, or supplier sole ownership plus an exclusive licence to KC.
5. **Capacity is a hard constraint with no short-term fix.** The supplier has a single post-treatment coating line, running 20 days/month on a single shift; a second line is planned but not yet ordered. Against KC's global volume, the supplier's own line cannot support scale-up. **The only realistic path is a three-party structure** — KC brokering "supplier formulation + nonwoven mill line (Jinlan/Da Yuan etc.) + KC quality standards."

## 5. Topic 1: Antibacterial and Microbiome

### 5.1 Comparison of Three Technology Routes

| Route | Representative substance | Mechanism | Maturity | Supplier stance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chitosan | Water-soluble chitosan (not chitin powder) | **Positively charged, adsorbs negatively charged harmful bacteria (electrostatic adsorption)** | Most mature; supplier's mainstay | Primary recommendation; different molecular weights target different strains |
| LAE | Ethyl lauroyl arginate HCl | Cationic surfactant-type antibacterial | Small-batch validation; 180-day ageing in progress | **Key new direction**, can claim "natural origin" |
| Silver ion | Silver ion / silver + PHMB combination | Metal-ion antibacterial | Common in Japanese brands | Supplier **does not** offer silver ion |

### 5.2 Key Mechanistic Clarification (important)

KC raised a common misconception: **does chitosan work by "porous-structure adsorption" of harmful bacteria, and would a dried emulsion block those pores?**

The supplier explicitly refuted this: **chitosan's primary antibacterial mechanism is electrostatic adsorption (chitosan positively charged, harmful bacteria negatively charged), not porous physical adsorption.** The "pore blocking" concern therefore does not apply, and drying does not impair antibacterial performance.

> This correction is worth recording — it also explains why chitosan remains effective in the dry state, whereas porous materials such as PCM lose function under the same conditions when pores are filled. **The mechanisms differ and cannot be analogised.**

### 5.3 LAE: Regulatory Window and New Direction

| Dimension | Content |
|---|---|
| Chemical name | Ethyl lauroyl arginate HCl (LAE) |
| Origin | Natural-origin synthesis (arginine + fatty acid) |
| Regulatory status | Listed in NMPA's cosmetics-ingredient catalogue in 2020 (**announcement number TBC**) |
| Claim space | Can claim "natural origin"; if not compounded with other antibacterials, can claim 100% natural origin |
| Measured antibacterial rate | ≥90% (**supplier self-test, not independently verified**) |
| Stability | 40 °C / 75% RH ageing, tested to month 5, level maintained; project started Feb 2026 |
| Supplier judgement | "Ready for market" (**supplier statement**) |
| Application form | Sampled on ADL (not topsheet) |

> **Expert commentary**: LAE's commercial value is not the antibacterial rate itself (90% is comparable to chitosan) but **claim compliance** — it gives "pure natural antibacterial" a regulatory foothold for the first time. Two caveats: (1) it still falls short of advertising-law "pure natural" wording; the supplier itself concedes "natural-origin synthetic" is the accurate phrase; (2) LAE is a cationic surfactant and may undergo charge neutralisation with anionic hydrophilic finishes (certain phosphate esters). **Compatibility must be verified before application**, otherwise the classic failure mode occurs: "formulation passes in the lab, fails on the line."

### 5.4 The Real Bottleneck: Process–Efficacy Stability

The supplier stated plainly: **failed antibacterial rates are almost never a formulation problem — they are a wet pick-up uniformity problem.**

| Phenomenon | Supplier explanation | Engineering implication |
|---|---|---|
| Antibacterial rate varies by location on the same batch | Insufficient coating uniformity; sampling point landed in a low pick-up zone | Must use CV, not mean, as the process-capability metric |
| Previous batch 90+, next batch fails | Coating speed, oven temperature, substrate lot variation | Requires inline closed-loop wet pick-up control |
| Weak-acid product shows different colours at different points | Local pH off target | Same as above — process, not formulation |

The supplier's mitigation: it performs antibacterial testing on the customer's behalf before shipment, and strengthens wet pick-up and uniformity monitoring. Customers perform unscheduled random sampling.

> **Expert commentary**: This exposes an industry-wide gap — **the "process capability" of functional post-treated fabric is not part of any acceptance system**. If KC is to bring such materials into a formal BOM, it must define and write into contract three metrics: (1) mean and CV of wet pick-up; (2) per-piece lower limit of active content (not the mean); (3) lot-to-lot consistency (range across 3 consecutive lots). Otherwise the supplier's "batch passes" becomes "batch drift" on KC's high-speed lines.

### 5.5 Regulatory and Claim Risk

| Risk | Current state | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| "99.9%" antibacterial claim | Sharply declining across the industry; leading brands abandoning it. Supplier: "this number is becoming rarer now" | Adopt tiered wording (≥90% / ≥99%) and retain raw data |
| "Long-lasting" wording | "Long-acting antibacterial" has largely disappeared, replaced by "8 h / 12 h" | Requires measured time-course data for the stated duration |
| Test-method sensitivity | Rate is affected by sample, bacterial generation, operator technique, sample conditioning; data scatter grows beyond 90% | Contract should specify methodology (strain, generation, inoculum) and retest rules |
| Main-ingredient labelling | New rules require labelling the main ingredient of the claimed efficacy and its addition level | Requires supplier per-piece quantitative reports as the labelling basis |

## 6. Topic 2: Odour Management (Deodorizing / Odour-Neutralising)

### 6.1 Combined Logic of Three Technical Means

The supplier's deodorizing system is a **three-means combination**; any single means is insufficient:

| Means | Representative materials | Mechanism | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| ① Suppress harmful bacteria | Chitosan, LAE | Cuts the source: "bacterial growth → odorous metabolites" | No immediate effect on already-generated odour |
| ② Chemical neutralisation | Lactic acid (primary), other acids | Urine/menstrual fluid is neutral-to-alkaline; acid neutralises alkaline odorants | Limited buffering capacity; pH fluctuates at high load |
| ③ Physical adsorption | Diatomite, tea polyphenols, bamboo/microporous carbon, yellow dextrin/cyclodextrin | Natural microporous physical capture of odorant molecules | Fails once saturated; adds grammage and thickness |

> Supplier statement: **"Any single one alone probably won't deodorise well; we compound several actives together."**

### 6.2 National Standard and Test Methods

From 2025, hygiene products have a dedicated deodorizing standard (previously by reference to textile standards). **The standard number is rendered "四五三六九" in ASR, suspected GB/T 45369 — TBC.**

| Product category | Mandatory odorants | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Baby diapers | **Ammonia** | Primary urine odorant; others optional |
| Adult incontinence | Ammonia + **indole** | Indole corresponds to faecal odour |
| Feminine hygiene | **Trimethylamine + isovaleric acid** | Simulates menstrual ammonia odour and sweat odour |

Method: gas chromatography, supplemented by detector-tube measurement (ppm level).

> **Cross-reference with this expert's knowledge base**: `references/21-纸尿裤异味控制与消臭技术.md` lists nine typical odorants (NH₃, H₂S, TMA, indole, DMDS, butanone, isovaleric acid, 2-ethylacrolein, methanethiol). **The national standard requires only 2–3 of them** — this is the "compliance floor," not the "experience ceiling." If KC wants genuine sensory performance, it should extend the test panel internally (notably H₂S and DMDS, whose contribution in the confined diaper environment is underestimated), while external claims remain anchored to the standard items.

### 6.3 Deodorizing Powder and SAP Dosing — An Engineering Problem (expert addition)

A US case was mentioned: a North American brand (ASR renders it "Rascal's", **brand name TBC**) markets a deodorizing baby diaper series using deodorizing powder alongside SAP. The supplier provided the key data: **deodorizing powder 30–60 µm; SAP 400–500 µm.**

This is an engineering problem that cannot be ignored:

| Dimension | Deodorizing powder | SAP | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particle size | 30–60 µm | 400–500 µm | ~10× difference |
| Density | Inorganic/plant-based, notably higher than SAP | ~0.6–0.7 g/cm³ (bulk) | Large settling-velocity difference |
| Flowability | Fine powder, prone to agglomeration and dusting | Better | Segregation is inevitable after blending |

Per the derivation in this knowledge base's `references/86-SAP落料速度匹配与容差规范.md`, **dosing slip distance is proportional to particle diameter and particle density** (s ∝ d·ρ). Taking 450 µm as baseline, a 710 µm particle slips 4.8× further. It follows that **a 30–60 µm deodorizing powder behaves completely differently from SAP in the same air/gravity dosing system; direct co-blending is very unlikely to guarantee distributional consistency.**


> **Recommendation (inference; requires verification)**: Do not pursue "pre-blending with SAP." Use an **independent metering unit and independent dosing point**, treating the deodorizing powder as its own functional layer (above the core surface or between SAP layers), with its own servo/rotary-valve-controlled add-on. Rationale: (1) avoids segregation; (2) allows deodorizing powder add-on to be decoupled from SAP add-on; (3) enables single-point traceability. This conclusion is based on a dosing physics model and **has not been validated on a production line**.

### 6.4 Demonstration and Verification Capability

The supplier can provide a "visual deodorising demonstration": colour development with ammonia/indole reagents (colour change under alkaline conditions), showing the effect intuitively. It can also issue quantitative reports using the national-standard method (GC + detector tube).

> **Expert commentary**: the colour demonstration is a **marketing asset, not engineering evidence**. KC's internal evaluation must rely on GC quantification plus mannequin/panel data; the colour demo is for consumer communication only.

## 7. Topic 3: Weak Acid and Skin Health

### 7.1 Technology Route

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Primary raw material | **L-lactic acid** (from corn fermentation) |
| Rationale | Gentler and more stable than malic, fruit or citric acid; widely used in food and cosmetics, high consumer acceptance; affinity with human skin lipids |
| Target pH | 4.0–5.5, controllable to 4.5–5.5 or a narrower window |
| Function | Counteracts the neutral-to-alkaline environment of urine/menstrual fluid; relieves irritation; indirectly suppresses odour-producing bacteria |

### 7.2 Key Distinction: Leachable vs. Non-leachable (an important technical point)

The supplier proactively flagged an industry "pseudo weak-acid" phenomenon:

- **Weak-acid fibres** (acidic component introduced at spinning) have existed for over a decade, but the acidic component **does not leach out on contact with liquid**;
- It therefore cannot neutralise the alkaline substances continuously generated during use (urine, menstrual fluid) — **the claim and the effect diverge**;
- The supplier's technology is **post-treatment based**: the acidic component sits outside the fibre and leaches on contact with liquid, enabling genuine neutralisation.

> **Expert commentary**: this is a highly valuable distinction, and the first question KC must ask any weak-acid topsheet supplier — **"does your acid leach or not?"** The test is simple: immerse the fabric in deionised water and measure the aqueous pH before and after; separately measure surface pH on the same batch by the standard method (GB/T). If aqueous pH is unchanged while surface pH is on target, it is the "non-leachable" type and efficacy is questionable.

### 7.3 National Standard pH Tightening

| Standard | Version change | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| GB/T 8939 (sanitary napkins) | New 2025 version; pH tightened from **4–9 to 4–7.5** | Neutral-to-alkaline formulations no longer compliant; higher demands on functional post-treatment |
| GB 15979 (hygienic standard for disposable sanitary products) | Foundational standard for microbial limits and antibacterial evaluation | Compliance base for antibacterial claims |

> ⚠️ **TBC**: whether GB/T 28004.1 for baby diapers has similarly adjusted its pH limit was not addressed in this meeting. The standards team must confirm before the formulation window is fixed.

## 8. Topic 4: Cooling and Thermal Management (highest technical value of the meeting)

### 8.1 Conceptual Clarification: Contact Cooling ≠ Actual Temperature Reduction

This was the densest passage of the entire meeting and must be preserved verbatim in substance:

| Concept | Mechanism | Persistence | Supplier judgement |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contact cooling (Q-max type)** | High-thermal-conductivity material rapidly draws heat from the skin surface, **with no heat transfer/absorption**; actual temperature does not fall | Short — strongest at first contact, then decays | "Very common in the industry, but only technical" |
| **Actual cooling (PCM latent heat)** | Phase-change material absorbs latent heat on melting; **genuine heat transfer occurs** | Theoretically sustained; in practice limited by loading | "At current add-on levels, there is actually no way to achieve sustained heat absorption that lowers temperature" |

Supplier's exact words: **"Phase-change material can't give a sustained sensation — it's not obvious. And if you put it onto nonwoven, you need a fairly high add-on before you feel even a little, but that then affects the material's base performance."**

The supplier further advised on positioning: **"If you want PCM, it's mostly a concept. For sustained cooling, we advise customers to compound menthol-type actives — that gives you persistence."**

> **Expert commentary (key)**: the candour here deserves credit — the supplier actively undermined its own selling point, which is rare in supplier meetings. KC should draw firm conclusions:
>
> 1. **Do not establish PCM as an efficacy project**; keep it only as a concept/marketing reserve;
> 2. If PCM enters the topsheet or ADL, it will **occupy pore volume, raise grammage, and reduce breathability and strike-through speed**, directly conflicting with the **void volume target of 80–117 cm³/g @ 0.5 kPa** pursued in the REL project per this knowledge base's `references/26-REL结构优化与选材技术方案.md`. **This is the most important cross-project risk flag from this meeting.**
> 3. If sustained cooling is genuinely required, the technical path is **menthol-type actives + controlled release (microcapsules)**, not PCM.

### 8.2 Three Application Modes for PCM and Their Suitability

| Mode | Approach | Suitable | Unsuitable |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-fibre addition | PCM blended into spinning dope to make PCM-containing fibre | Apparel, home textiles (mature) | Hygiene — ineffective at low loading; fibre mechanics and hydrophilicity degrade at effective loading |
| Post-treatment coating/spraying | PCM dispersion coated onto existing fabric | Feasible in lab | Volume production — adhesion problem (Section 10) and impaired strike-through |
| Micro-encapsulation | Shell coats PCM; friction/liquid triggers release | Scenarios needing a "releases in use" demonstration | KC scenarios — must work immediately; encapsulation defeats the purpose |

### 8.3 Cooling Agent System

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Core materials | Menthol family (natural plant origin): menthol, menthone, menthyl esters, menthyl amides |
| Not used | **Borneol** — provides cooling but is prohibited under the Cosmetics Safety Technical Specification; strong medicinal odour; possible irritation with long-term use |
| Formulation forms | Both micro-emulsion and microcapsule routes; the Babycare project uses microcapsules |
| Perceived duration | Self-tested formulations reach **4–5 hours** (**supplier self-test, subjective blinded panel, not independently verified**) |
| Sensation curve | Slight coolness initially → intensifies on liquid contact → peak → gradual decay |
| Special requirement | "Orderless cooling": remove menthol odour while retaining cooling |
| Differentiation | Export customers in Africa demand stronger cooling; domestic formulations are more compounded with gentler dosage |

### 8.4 Orderless Cooling — Status of Development

KC (Andy's team) requirement: **odourless, with cooling lasting ≥5 hours.**

The supplier's direct response: **"A bit contradictory."**

| Conflict | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Needs to be odourless | Menthol (the main odour source) must be suppressed |
| Needs to last long | Menthol loading must be increased |
| Current status | Supplier self-test "does not reach the 5-hour level"; development ongoing |

> **Expert commentary**: this is a classic "physics-constrained" requirement, and short-term supplier breakthrough should not be expected. Three workable compromises: (1) **split the spec** into two SKUs, one satisfying "odourless" and one "5 hours"; (2) **change the sensory dimension** — substitute part of the menthol with non-menthol coolants (e.g. WS-3/WS-23 type), which requires fresh safety and regulatory assessment (**outside the verified scope of this meeting; would need a separate project**); (3) **relax the duration threshold** — if 3 hours is acceptable, difficulty drops markedly. We recommend KC first establish the provenance of the "5 hours" figure (consumer research vs. competitor benchmark) before further investment.

### 8.5 Test and Calibration Methodology (directly reusable)

The supplier presented a four-level verification system with sound structure; we recommend KC adopt it directly as an internal template:

| Level | Method | Output |
|---|---|---|
| L1 Instrumental | GC quantification of menthol / menthone / menthyl esters / menthyl amides | Content of each component per piece (µg/piece) |
| L2 Layer-resolved | Separate topsheet / ADL / core, measure each | Distribution profile; back-infers spray pressure and topsheet thickness adequacy |
| L3 Ageing curve | 40 °C / 75% RH at 14 / 30 / 90 days (extendable to 180) | Active retention curve |
| L4 Blinded panel | Colleague blinded test, applied to forearm, cooling rated every 30 min | Subjective duration and curve |

> **Expert commentary**: L2 (layer-resolved quantification) is the most valuable and most easily overlooked element. It links "customer line process parameters" directly to "product efficacy" — low menthol in topsheet but high in ADL means excessive spray pressure or too thin a topsheet, with menthol penetrating downward. **No other supplier in KC's current base has this diagnostic capability.**

## 9. Topic 5: Selection of Formulation Form (Micro-emulsion / Microcapsule / Powder / Chip)

### 9.1 Why a "Formulation" Is Needed Instead of Direct Spraying

The supplier used fragrance as an example to illustrate three problems with direct spraying:

| Problem | Mechanism | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Poor affinity of oily components with nonwoven | PP-based nonwoven has low surface energy (~30 mN/m); oily substances wet poorly | Greasy hand-feel, weak adhesion |
| Impedes liquid strike-through | Oil film forms a hydrophobic barrier on the fibre surface | Slower strike-through, potentially below the national standard |
| Dose must be increased | Low adhesion → over-spray to compensate | Higher cost + safety risk |


The micro-emulsion approach **converts the oily component into an oil-in-water structure** — the outermost phase is aqueous, compatible with nonwoven, achieving "efficient application without blocking strike-through."

### 9.2 Decision Matrix (directly usable for project selection)

| Dimension | Micro-emulsion | Microcapsule (liquid/powder) | Direct spraying | Laminated chip |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Suitable actives | Heat-stable, water-soluble or emulsifiable antibacterial/weak-acid actives | Volatile, oxidation-prone, deactivation-prone, requiring controlled release (menthol, essential oils, botanicals) | Simple water-soluble actives | High-load actives; needs visual demonstration |
| Release mode | **Immediate** (on fibre surface, releases on liquid contact) | **Triggered** (friction / liquid / temperature breaks the shell) | Immediate | Triggered |
| Storage robustness | Medium | **Good** (can replace aluminium-laminate packaging) | Poor (volatilises, yellows) | Good |
| Impact on fabric performance | Small | Small (powder/chip does not occupy topsheet pores) | **Large** (blocks strike-through) | None (sits in the chip layer) |
| Loading ceiling | Limited by solubility | Limited by shell ratio (mostly **partial encapsulation**) | Low | **High** |
| Cost | Low | Medium-high | Lowest | High (extra material layer) |
| Demonstrability | Weak | **Strong** (visible release) | None | Strong (printable) |

### 9.3 The Reality of "Partial Encapsulation"

The supplier disclosed: commercially available microcapsules are **mostly partially encapsulated; full encapsulation is unrealistic**. Reasons:

- Too much shell material makes the liquid viscous and unsprayable/unc oat-able;
- Menthol itself has an addition ceiling in liquid; exceeding it causes **emulsion breaking**;
- Hence "the active is only the portion that settles out" is the norm.

> **Expert commentary**: this means "microcapsule = 100% protection" is marketing language. When evaluating suppliers, KC should demand two numbers — **encapsulation efficiency** and **payload** — rather than accepting "uses microcapsule technology" as a claim.

### 9.4 Knock-on Packaging and BOM Impact (an easily overlooked cost opportunity)

The supplier noted that conventional cooling products use **aluminium-laminate pouches with resealable tabs** to prevent volatilisation, oxidation and yellowing. **Once micro-encapsulated, a paper box plus ordinary PE bag suffices.**

> **Expert commentary**: this is an under-appreciated cost opportunity. Following the cost-tiering approach in `references/22-纸尿裤BOM成本结构与降本策略.md`, packaging is a non-trivial share of sanitary-napkin BOM; if micro-encapsulation removes the aluminium layer, packaging cost falls and one laminate layer disappears (helping recyclability claims). **The "packaging simplification benefit" should be included in the ROI calculation for any microcapsule proposal**, not just the formulation cost increment.

## 10. Topic 6: Feasibility of Applying Inorganic Insoluble Particles (the core deadlock)

### 10.1 Problem Definition

KC raised: a class of functional material whose **physical properties are fixed** — a purely inorganic powder, neither water- nor oil-soluble, particle size ~2–3 µm.

Requirements:
1. Uniformly dispersible (no settling);
2. Adherent to nonwoven, **no dusting after drying**;
3. **Must not be encapsulated** — efficacy required the moment it is converted, not triggered by water/friction/heat.

### 10.2 Mechanistic Analysis: Why Dusting Is Inevitable

The supplier gave a textbook explanation worth recording in full:

> **"Mechanistically it will certainly happen. Why? Because if your suspension is pure water, then once it evaporates, that dry powder and the fabric fibres have no adhesion at all — it will definitely fall off."**

Three necessary conditions, none optional:

| Condition | Function | Consequence if absent |
|---|---|---|
| Dispersion | Keep particles uniformly suspended, no settling | Uneven application, local accumulation |
| Adhesion | Form a bond between particle and fibre after drying | **Dusting** |
| Accessibility | Active not enclosed by shell; effective on contact | Ineffective |

The dilemma for inorganic particles: **they inherently have no crosslinking or adhesion to any material.** An externally added "binding phase" is mandatory.

### 10.3 The Supplier's Solution: Surfactant / Solubilisation System

The supplier's route is to **build a binding phase using surfactants plus co-agents**:

- Surfactants lower the interfacial tension between inorganic particles and water, breaking down the "two worlds" boundary;
- After drying, the surfactant forms a glue-like film with inherent tack, "carrying" the inorganic particles onto the fibre;
- Multi-surfactant compounding (designed per raw material), not a single surfactant.

Supplier's track record cases (**evidence grading below**):

| Case | Material | Status | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Titanium dioxide | Inorganic | Sampled and produced | Supplier states "acceptable, no dusting" |
| Zinc oxide | Inorganic | Lab-scale small formulation only | **No volume production, adhesion after drying not verified** |
| Phase-change material | Inorganic | Supplier considers it a PCM component | Low dosage; current result acceptable |
| Probiotics (inactivated) | Organic particles | In volume production for a brand | Supplier states "a lot of upfront work to make it adhere" |
| Hyaluronic acid | Organic (hard to dissolve) | In volume production (largest North American OEM) | Dissolved and detected in finished product |

> **Evidence grading (per this expert's red lines)**:
> - TiO₂, probiotics, hyaluronic acid: **supplier states volume production, not independently verified**;
> - ZnO: **lab-scale only; volume-production adhesion explicitly unverified**;
> - PCM: **no successful case; supplier explicitly says "we really haven't done much with inorganics"**;
> - Therefore the proposition "the supplier can solve inorganic adhesion" currently rests on **one weak reference case (TiO₂)** — insufficient to support a KC material project.

### 10.4 The Nature of the Deadlock

Stacking the three requirements reveals a closed-loop contradiction:

| Step | Necessarily implies | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| ① Immediate efficacy required | Must not encapsulate | Loses the means of "protecting the active" |
| ② No encapsulation | Must rely on a binding phase for adhesion | Introduces additional auxiliaries |
| ③ Binding phase | Is essentially a film that coats the particle surface | Active becomes masked |
| ④ Coating the surface | Reduces accessibility of the active | Slower, weaker efficacy |
| ⑤ Reduced accessibility | **Returns to the conflict in ①** | Closed loop; unresolved |

The supplier's microcapsule route **solves adhesion but destroys immediate accessibility**; the surfactant binding route **preserves accessibility but gives uncertain adhesion strength**. This is why the supplier repeatedly said "this doesn't quite suit our use scenario."

> **Expert commentary (recommend project initiation)**: this is not a problem one supplier can solve — it is an **industry-level interfacial chemistry challenge**. If KC proceeds, we recommend the following minimum viable test (MVT) sequence:
>
|  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Step | Content | Criterion |
> |---|---|---|
> | MVT-1 | Apply 3 candidate binding phases (surfactant / water-soluble polymer / inorganic binder) to 2–3 µm inorganic powder in lab coating | ≥80% powder retention after 50 dry rub cycles |
> | MVT-2 | On MVT-1 passers, measure active accessibility (release rate / activity on liquid contact) | Release ≥70% and onset within 5 min |
> | MVT-3 | On MVT-1/2 passers, measure base fabric performance | Strike-through no worse than 10% vs. blank; rewet no worse than control |
> | MVT-4 | Only if MVT-1–3 all pass, discuss line trial and capacity | — |
>
> The material qualifies for KC's system **only if MVT-1 and MVT-2 pass simultaneously**. Passing either alone does not count.

### 10.5 Another Engineering Risk: Guide-Roll Residue

The supplier proactively raised a volume-production risk: **at high active loading, residue accumulates on the customer's guide rolls**, requiring periodic cleaning; some customers mitigate by wrapping guide rolls with release paper.

> **Expert commentary**: this is a generic problem of post-treated fabric, independent of whether the material is inorganic. If KC adopts high-load functional fabric, its line SOP must add a "guide-roll cleaning interval" item, and the acceptance criteria must include "guide-roll residue after 8 hours of continuous running." Otherwise it will escalate into a downtime incident.

## 12. Supply Chain, Business Models and Capacity

### 12.1 Three Cooperation Models

| Model | Structure | Supplier stance | Fit for KC |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 Supplier formulation + supplier application | Supplier processes on its own coating line with its own formulation; delivers functional fabric | Strongly preferred | Medium — capacity-limited |
| M2 KC formulation, toll coating | KC provides the formulation; supplier only coats | **Conditionally accepted**: "willing if the margin is sufficient" | Low — supplier explicitly fears cannibalising its own products |
| M3 Supplier supplies liquid + third-party mill applies | Supplier provides the treatment liquid; nonwoven mill (Jinlan/Da Yuan etc.) applies; supplier monitors quality | Already in practice | **High — the only scalable path** |

The supplier was candid about M2: **"We have only one machine, so we need sufficient margin to support it. If it becomes just a plain toll-coating machine, we can't run other products, and that would affect our development."**

### 12.2 Capacity Constraint and Order-of-Magnitude Estimate

Current capacity:

- 1 coating line, 20 days/month, single shift (8 h, no night shift);
- 2 laminators (serving sanitary-napkin chips, higher capacity than coating);
- A second coating line in preparation; equipment lead time ~2 months; building renovated with footprint reserved.

> **Expert estimate (inference; assumptions below; not confirmed by supplier)**
>
|  |  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> Assumptions: web width 1.6 m | line speed 90 m/min | single shift 8 h | 20 days/month | substrate 20 gsm
>
> - Monthly linear capacity ≈ 90 × 60 × 8 × 20 = **864,000 linear metres/month**
> - Substrate weight ≈ 864,000 × 1.6 × 20 g = **≈ 27.6 t/month**
> - At 0.1 m² topsheet consumption per size-M diaper → **≈ 13.8 million pieces/month ≈ 166 million pieces/year (theoretical full utilisation)**
>
> **But this is a theoretical ceiling**: (1) single shift only — actual available hours ~50%; (2) the line must be shared across multiple customers and SKUs, so KC's attainable share may be only 20–30%; (3) functional fabric is used on only some SKUs.
>
> → **Realistically allocatable to KC: on the order of 10–30 million functional-topsheet-equivalent pieces per year.** That is enough to support a regional differentiated SKU, **but not enough for a global or China-wide switchover**.

**Conclusion: capacity is a hard constraint that the supplier's own line build-out cannot resolve within 2–3 years. M3 is mandatory.**

### 12.3 The "Vertical and Horizontal Alliance" Path (the most important commercial conclusion)

The supplier stated clearly: **"Most likely it has to be an alliance. If your brand takes the lead, cooperation would be smoother and faster."**

It cited a precedent: a brand (ASR renders it "泰安社", **TBC**) previously brokered a "supplier × Da Yuan" cooperation.

Recommended three-party structure:

| Role | Responsibility | Key control point |
|---|---|---|
| KC | Define requirements at brand level, set acceptance standards, lead the tripartite agreement | Must secure **formulation ownership or exclusive rights**, otherwise KC is effectively cultivating a supply chain for competitors |
| Unergy | Provide formulation, treatment liquid, QC methodology and test data package | Must accept KC's per-piece quantitative acceptance criteria |
| Nonwoven mill (Jinlan/Da Yuan etc.) | Provide line, width and speed capability | Must adopt the supplier's QC method, or accept supplier on-site monitoring |

> **Expert caution**: in this structure KC's greatest risk is **"teaching the supply chain and benefiting rivals"** — the supplier simultaneously serves most major domestic brands (Best, Hengan, ABC, Deyou, Da Wang, Babycare, etc.). The tripartite agreement must therefore include: (1) an exclusivity period for the KC-customised formulation; (2) a prohibition on using that formulation for competitors; (3) a prohibition on reusing KC's test methods/data templates for other customers.

### 12.4 IP Arrangement (red line)

The supplier proposed four ownership scenarios; KC stated the hard constraint on the spot:

| Scenario | Supplier proposal | KC position |
|---|---|---|
| KC proposes concept; supplier develops on its own process | KC-led | Acceptable |
| Extension of supplier's existing formulation | Joint application | ❌ **Not acceptable** — KC policy prohibits joint patent ownership with suppliers |
| Supplier independent development | Supplier-owned | Acceptable, but KC requires an exclusive licence |
| Joint development | Depends on lead party | Case-by-case |

KC's exact words: **"This is not our personal preference — it is our corporate policy. KC simply cannot share joint patent ownership with a supplier."**

> **Expert note**: this red line must be communicated in writing **before any technical data exchange**, otherwise a commercial dispute will arise where the supplier invests on the expectation of joint ownership and ends up unable to share the patent. We recommend signing a separate *IP Arrangement Confirmation Letter* alongside the NDA.

### 15.2 Three Recommended Thrusts

**Thrust 1: Enter the relationship through "analytical capability," not formulation.**
The supplier's most valuable and least substitutable asset is GC/LC per-piece and layer-resolved quantification. KC should first engage in the name of "analytical services" (low risk, fast results), using data to build trust and leverage before discussing formulation and lines. This path also naturally sidesteps the capacity bottleneck.

**Thrust 2: Land "weak acid + cooling" first, not deodorising.**
Deodorising has the clearest standard but involves powder dosing engineering (§6.3) and therefore the longest implementation chain. Weak acid and cooling are both "liquid post-treated fabric" — shortest process path, shortest validation cycle, and the supplier has extensive volume track record. Use these two to prove out the "KC standard + supplier formulation + mill line" tripartite model, then replicate to deodorising.

**Thrust 3: Immediately formalise IP and the tripartite framework in writing.**
R-02 and R-03 are the highest-level commercial risks from this meeting, and both are racing against time — once the supplier invests on the expectation of joint patents, or KC's custom formulation leaks to competitors, the cost of correction is extremely high. Complete C-05 and C-04 within 6 weeks.

### 15.3 One-Sentence Summary

> **This supplier merits deep cooperation — but the target is not "buying its functional fabric," it is "buying its formulation-engineering capability plus analytical data capability," while solving the line problem through a tripartite structure. At the same time, KC must clearly drop PCM and treat inorganic particle adhesion as a research problem requiring dedicated effort, not as a one-off purchase.**
